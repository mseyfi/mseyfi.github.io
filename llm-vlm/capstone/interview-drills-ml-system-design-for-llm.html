    Interview drills: ML system design for LLM, VLM and video services

    System design interviews for LLM, VLM and video services test one skill: turning a vague product request into numbers, a design, and a clear view of what breaks first. This session gives you one answer frame and runs it on three drills: a chat API with a latency target, natural-language search over a sports video archive, and real-time key-moment alerts on a live broadcast.

    Time: 3 to 4 hours (reading 2 hours, drills and exercises 1 to 2 hours)Level: Capstone. Uses the inference, retrieval and video tracks.Before this: Inference metrics, KV cache, batching, text-to-video retrieval and key-moment detection (sessions 80, 81, 135, 208, 207)

    What you will learn

    • A five-step answer frame for any ML system design question: requirements, numbers, design, bottleneck, failure modes
    • Back-of-envelope formulas you can do on a whiteboard: Little's law, KV bytes per token, TPOT floor, compute utilization, replicas
    • Drill 1: size a chat API for a TTFT and TPOT target, and find out that the prompt, not the answer, is the main cost
    • Drill 2: design natural-language search over 10,000 hours of sports video: index size, backfill cost, two-stage retrieval and re-ranking
    • Drill 3: design real-time key-moment alerts and reason about the trade-off between alert delay and false alarms
    • How to name the bottleneck and the failure modes before the interviewer asks, and how to handle follow-up questions such as “now 10 times the traffic”

    Why this matters

    In a system design interview you get one vague sentence, such as “design a chat API” or “let fans search old matches by describing a moment”. You have about 45 minutes. The interviewer does not want the one correct design; there is none. They want to see how you think.

    Most weak answers fail in the same way. The candidate draws boxes at once (“a load balancer, then vLLM, then a vector database”) and never says how big anything is. Then the interviewer asks “how many GPUs?” or “what happens at the final whistle when everyone searches at once?”, and the design has no numbers to answer with. Strong answers turn the vague sentence into numbers early, let the numbers choose the design, and name the part that breaks first before anyone asks.

    Interview tipSay the frame out loud at the start: “I will first fix the requirements, then do some quick numbers, then the design, then the main bottleneck and the failure modes.” Interviewers relax when they know where you are going, and it gives them an easy place to steer you.

    This session is a drill book. It builds on almost every track of the course: the inference metrics and serving ideas of Track 5, the retrieval ideas of session 208, and the key-moment work of session 207 and session 235. We first fix one answer frame. Then we run it three times, each time with real numbers that you can check with a calculator:

    • Drill 1. A chat API with a latency target. The numbers tell you how many GPUs you need and that the prompt is the main cost.
    • Drill 2. Natural-language search over a sports archive. The numbers tell you how big the index is, how long the backfill takes and where the query time goes.
    • Drill 3. Real-time key-moment alerts on a live broadcast. The numbers tell you the price of every second of delay in false alarms.

    The previous session, session 237, measured where a real system fails. This session is the spoken version of the same skill: explain a system, its numbers and its failures to another engineer in a short time.

    The answer frame

    We use five steps, always in the same order. Each step has one job.

    StepJobWhat you produceTime in a 45-minute round
    1. RequirementsTurn the vague request into things you can measureUsers and traffic, inputs and outputs, latency targets, quality target, cost limit, what is out of scopeabout 5 min
    2. NumbersSize the problem before choosing partsRequests per second, tokens or frames per second, bytes in memory, FLOPs per second, number of GPUsabout 8 min
    3. DesignChoose the parts that the numbers allowA diagram of the online path and the offline path, with the model and hardware choice for each boxabout 15 min
    4. BottleneckSay which resource runs out first, and the fix“Compute is the limit, because …; we fix it with …”about 7 min
    5. Failure modesShow that you have run real systemsWhat breaks, how you detect it, what the system does then; how you evaluate qualityabout 7 min

    The times are a suggestion, not a rule. The order matters more: numbers come before the design, because the numbers decide the design. A system with 1 request per second and one with 1,000 requests per second are different systems.

    AnalogyA doctor does not start with surgery. First the symptoms (requirements), then the tests (numbers), then the treatment (design), then the main risk of that treatment (bottleneck), then what to watch after (failure modes). Skipping the tests is how you operate on the wrong organ.

    Step 1: requirements as numbers

    Ask, or state as an assumption, these five things. If the interviewer says “you decide”, choose a round number and write it down.

    1. Load. Requests per second at peak (not on average), and how “bursty” it is.
    2. Size of each request. For text: prompt tokens \(n_{\text{in}}\) and output tokens \(n_{\text{out}}\). For video: hours of video, frames per second, clips per query.
    3. Latency targets. A service level objective (SLO) is a target such as “95% of requests get their first token in under 1 s”. For streaming text we use two numbers from session 81: TTFT (time to first token) and TPOT (time per output token, the gap between two tokens).
    4. Quality. How you will measure it: recall@10 for search, precision and recall of alerts, a judge score for chat.
    5. Cost. A budget in GPUs or dollars, or “as cheap as possible for this SLO”.

    Step 2: the five formulas you need

    Almost every number in this session comes from a handful of formulas. The first is Little’s law from queueing theory: in a stable system, the average number of items inside equals the arrival rate times the average time each item stays.

    \[ B = \lambda \cdot W, \qquad W \approx \text{TTFT} + n_{\text{out}} \cdot \text{TPOT} \tag{238.1} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(B\)
    the average number of requests inside the system at one time (for an LLM server: the running batch)
    \(\lambda\)
    the arrival rate, in requests per second
    \(W\)
    the average time one request stays, in seconds
    \(n_{\text{out}}\)
    output tokens per request

    Little’s law needs no assumption about the arrival pattern, only a stable system [Certain]. It is the bridge from “requests per second” (what the product team says) to “requests in memory at once” (what the GPU sees). In the formulas below we use the part of \(W\) spent decoding, \(n_{\text{out}}\cdot\text{TPOT}\), because that is when a request holds a slot in the decode batch.

    The second formula is the size of the KV cache (session 80): every token in the context keeps one key and one value vector in every layer.

    \[ s_{\text{kv}} = 2 \cdot L \cdot n_{\text{kv}} \cdot d_h \cdot b \tag{238.2} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(s_{\text{kv}}\)
    bytes of KV cache per token of context
    \(2\)
    one key and one value
    \(L\)
    number of layers
    \(n_{\text{kv}}, d_h\)
    number of key/value heads (fewer than query heads with GQA) and the size of each head
    \(b\)
    bytes per number: 2 for BF16, 1 for FP8

    The other three formulas (memory, step time, compute) come in Drill 1, where we need them. Before that, here are the facts you should know by heart. Everything else you compute.

    NVIDIA L424 GB, 300 GB/s, 121 TFLOP/s dense BF16NVIDIA datasheet (242 is “with sparsity”) [Certain]

    We also need assumptions about how well real software uses the hardware. We reuse the ones from session 141: matrix multiplies reach \(u = 50\%\) of peak FLOP/s, memory reads reach \(\eta = 80\%\) of peak bandwidth, every decode step has \(t_0 = 1\) ms of fixed overhead, and 90% of GPU memory is usable. These are planning assumptions, not measurements. Say them out loud in the interview; a good interviewer cares more that you know they exist than about their exact values.

    Steps 3 to 5: design, bottleneck, failure modes

    For the design, always draw two paths. The online path is what happens while a user waits; it has a latency budget. The offline path is everything that can run in the background: indexing, training, evaluation. Moving work from the online path to the offline path is the most common trick in system design.

    For the bottleneck, name one resource: GPU compute, GPU memory bandwidth, GPU memory size, network, CPU, or a human (labels, review). Then say the fix and what it costs. For the failure modes, give three to five concrete ones, each with how you detect it and what the system does then.

    Algorithm 1: the answer frame
    1. input: a vague product request, about 45 minutes
    2. write down load \(\lambda\) at peak, request size, SLOs (p95 TTFT, TPOT, or end-to-end), quality metric, cost limit // ask or assume
    3. compute per-request work (tokens, frames, FLOPs) and per-request memory (KV bytes, index bytes)
    4. multiply by \(\lambda\) and use Little’s law (238.1) to get totals that must exist at the same time
    5. divide totals by one GPU’s usable limits to get a first GPU count // compute, bandwidth, memory: the largest wins
    6. draw the online path (within the latency budget) and the offline path (no user waiting)
    7. name the binding limit, and one fix that moves it (cache, batch, quantize, cascade, move work offline)
    8. list failure modes with detection and fallback; state how you measure quality before and after launch
    9. when the interviewer changes a number (“10× traffic”, “half the budget”) do go back to line 3, not to line 6

    Drill 1: a chat API with a latency target

    The prompt. “We want a chat assistant API for our product, built on an open model. It must feel fast. Design the serving system.”

    Requirements

    We fix these numbers and say them out loud: peak load \(\lambda = 20\) requests per second; on average \(n_{\text{in}} = 1{,}000\) prompt tokens (system prompt plus chat history) and \(n_{\text{out}} = 300\) output tokens; p95 TTFT under 1 s; TPOT under 50 ms, which is 20 tokens per second, faster than people read [Likely]. Model: Llama 3.1 8B in BF16 for a first plan, with the 70B as a follow-up. Out of scope: training, safety filters, billing.

    Numbers

    We need three more formulas. Memory: the running requests must fit next to the weights. With \(M_W\) the weight bytes and \(C\) the GPU memory:

    \[ B \cdot (n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}}) \cdot s_{\text{kv}} \;\le\; 0.9\,C - M_W \tag{238.3} \]

    Step time: one decode step reads all weights and the KV cache of the batch, and also does some FLOPs. It costs the larger of the two, plus a fixed overhead. With continuous batching and chunked prefill (session 135), the same steps also process waiting prompt tokens. Putting \(B = \lambda\, n_{\text{out}}\,\text{TPOT}\) into the step time and solving (this is formula 141.8 of session 141) gives:

    \[ \text{TPOT} = \max\!\left( \frac{t_0 + M_W/(\eta\,\text{BW})}{1 - \beta},\; \frac{t_0}{1 - c} \right), \qquad \beta = \frac{\lambda\, n_{\text{out}}\, \bar L\, s_{\text{kv}}}{\eta\,\text{BW}} \tag{238.4} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(t_0 + M_W/(\eta\,\text{BW})\)
    the TPOT floor: the time to read all weights once. No batch size can beat it.
    \(\beta\)
    the share of each step spent reading KV cache; it grows with load
    \(\bar L = n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}}/2\)
    the average context length while a request is decoding
    \(c\)
    compute utilization, formula (238.5)
    \(\text{BW}\)
    memory bandwidth of the GPU, bytes per second

    Compute: each request costs about \(2N\) FLOPs per token it pushes through the model. If a share \(h\) of the prompt is already in a prefix cache (the KV of a shared system prompt or of earlier chat turns, kept from before, session 134), those tokens need no compute.

    \[ C_{\text{req}} = 2N\left( (1-h)\, n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}} \right), \qquad c = \frac{\lambda\, C_{\text{req}}}{u\,F}, \qquad \text{TTFT} \approx \frac{t_0 + 2N(1-h)\,n_{\text{in}}/(uF)}{1 - c} \tag{238.5} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(N\)
    model parameters; \(2N\) FLOPs per token for the linear layers (attention FLOPs are small at this context length: under 2% here)
    \(h\)
    prefix-cache hit rate: the share of prompt tokens whose KV is already stored
    \(F, u\)
    peak FLOP/s of the GPU and the share we really reach (50%)
    \(1/(1-c)\)
    a simple queueing factor: waiting time grows without bound as the GPU gets full. It is the same shape as the M/M/1 queue, used here as a rough rule

    Finally the plan. Let \(\mu^{*}\) be the largest load one replica can carry while meeting every target. We do not plan to run at 100% of \(\mu^{*}\): traffic has bursts and long requests. With headroom \(H\) (we use 0.8):

    \[ \mu^{*} = \max\{\lambda : \text{TPOT} \le \tau_{\text{TPOT}},\ \text{TTFT} \le \tau_{\text{TTFT}},\ (238.3) \text{ holds}\}, \qquad R = \left\lceil \frac{\lambda}{H\,\mu^{*}} \right\rceil \tag{238.6} \]
    Worked example: Llama 3.1 8B on H100s

    Memory. Weights \(M_W = 8.03\times10^9 \times 2 = 16.06\) GB. KV per token (238.2): \(2 \times 32 \times 8 \times 128 \times 2 = 131{,}072\) bytes = 128 KiB. One request holds up to \(1{,}300\) tokens, so about 170 MB.

    Compute. \(C_{\text{req}} = 2 \times 8.03\times10^9 \times 1{,}300 = 20.9\) TFLOP. At 20 requests per second that is 417.6 TFLOP/s. One H100 gives \(uF = 0.5 \times 989 = 494.5\) TFLOP/s, so on one GPU \(c = 0.84\).

    Latency on one GPU. TPOT floor \(= 1 + 16.06/(0.8 \times 3.35) = 7.0\) ms. KV share \(\beta = 20 \times 300 \times 1{,}150 \times 131{,}072 / (0.8 \times 3.35\times10^{12}) = 0.34\). So TPOT \(= \max(7.0/0.66,\ 1/0.16) = 10.6\) ms, far under 50 ms. But TTFT \(= (1 + 32.5)/(1 - 0.84) = 215\) ms, and it explodes as \(c \to 1\). Little’s law: \(B = 20 \times 300 \times 0.0106 = 63\) requests in the batch, holding 10.8 GB of KV, far under the 55.9 GB free.

    Plan. Bisection on (238.6) gives \(\mu^{*} = 22.9\) requests per second per H100, limited by TTFT (that is, by compute). With headroom 0.8: \(R = \lceil 20 / (0.8 \times 22.9) \rceil = 2\) H100s. Each then runs at 10 requests per second: compute 42%, TPOT 8.4 ms, TTFT 58 ms, 25 requests in the batch, 4.3 GB of KV.

    Two follow-ups. With 50% of prompt tokens in the prefix cache, \(\mu^{*} = 37.2\) and one H100 is enough. With the 70B model on groups of 4 H100s (tensor parallel, communication ignored), \(\mu^{*} = 10.0\) per group, so 3 groups = 12 GPUs. On an L4 the 8B plan is impossible: the TPOT floor is \(1 + 16.06/(0.8 \times 0.3) = 67.9\) ms, above the 50 ms target, for any load.

    The surprise for many candidates: the answer has 300 tokens and the prompt 1,000, so 77% of the compute is prompt processing (prefill). Decode takes most of the wall-clock time of a request, but prefill takes most of the FLOPs. This is why prefix caching and short system prompts are the cheapest speed-ups for chat, and why some systems put prefill and decode on different GPUs (session 137). Try it in Figure 2.

    Design

    Now the numbers allow a simple design. Online path: an API gateway (authentication, rate limits per customer) sends requests to a router, which picks one of \(R\) model replicas. Each replica runs an engine with continuous batching, chunked prefill and a paged KV cache with prefix caching (vLLM, SGLang or TensorRT-LLM, session 139). Tokens stream back to the client as they are made. The router should be prefix-aware: send the next turn of a chat to the replica that already holds its history in the cache, or the prefix cache hit rate drops by about a factor \(R\) [Likely]. Offline path: logs of TTFT, TPOT, queue length and cache hit rate per replica; load tests with a realistic mix of prompt lengths; an evaluation set to check quality after every model or quantization change.

    ComponentChoiceWhy, from the numbers
    Model replicaLlama 3.1 8B, BF16, one H100 eachWeights 16 GB fit with room for KV; TPOT floor 7 ms ≪ 50 ms
    Replicas2 at peak (autoscale down at night)\(\mu^{*} = 22.9\) req/s per GPU, headroom 0.8
    BatchingContinuous, chunked prefillPrefill is 77% of FLOPs; chunking stops long prompts from freezing decode
    KV cachePaged, with prefix cachingChat history is reused every turn; each 10% of hits cuts prefill by 10%
    RouterLeast-loaded, with session or prefix affinityKeeps cache hits high; spreads bursts

    Bottleneck

    Compute, through prefill. Our numbers show TPOT has a lot of slack (8 ms against 50 ms), while TTFT grows fast as compute fills up. Fixes, in order of cost: prefix caching and shorter system prompts (free); FP8 weights and activations, which roughly doubles peak FLOP/s on an H100 [Likely]; more replicas; and, at large scale, separate prefill and decode GPUs so that each can be sized for its own limit (session 137). Speculative decoding (session 128) would not help much here: it trades extra compute for lower TPOT, and TPOT is not our problem.

    Watch outMeans hide tails. A few users paste 30,000-token documents. One such prompt costs as much compute as 30 normal ones, and without chunked prefill it blocks the decode steps of every request on that replica. Plan with p95 prompt lengths, and cap the maximum prompt per tier.

    Failure modes

    • Burst at peak (a product launch). Detect: queue length and TTFT p95 rising. Act: admission control (return “busy, retry” for the lowest tier), scale out; do not let queues grow without limit.
    • KV memory full. Detect: preemptions per minute in the engine. Act: the engine swaps or recomputes some requests (higher latency); cap max context per request.
    • A replica dies. Detect: health checks. Act: the router stops sending to it; streams in progress are lost, so the client retries. With \(R = 2\) and headroom 0.8, one replica alone cannot carry the full peak, so \(R = 3\) is the honest answer if the SLO must hold during a failure (“N+1”).
    • Quality drop after a change (new quantization, new model). Detect: offline evaluation set before launch, and a small share of live traffic (canary) compared with the old version.
    • Very long outputs (a loop that repeats). Detect: output length distribution. Act: a max-token limit and repetition checks.

    Drill 2: natural-language video search over a sports archive

    The prompt. “We have 10,000 hours of football matches. Editors want to type ‘header goal from a corner in the last minute’ and get the right moments. Design it.”

    Requirements

    Users: a few hundred editors, so the query load is small (say under 10 queries per second at peak). Each query returns the top 20 segments with timestamps. Latency: under 1 s for the first results; editors accept 2 to 3 s if results get better. Quality: recall@20 on a labeled test set of queries; the right moment should usually be in the first page. Archive: 10,000 hours now, plus new matches every day. Cost: indexing can be slow (days are fine); the query path must be cheap.

    The first insight comes from this list: the load is tiny, but the archive is big. So the hard part is not serving queries; it is turning 36 million seconds of video into something we can search in milliseconds.

    Numbers

    We cut every match into short segments (for example 4 s), sample frames at \(f\) frames per second, encode them with an image–text encoder such as CLIP, and pool the frame vectors into one vector per segment (session 208). The index size and the one-time encoding cost (the backfill) are:

    \[ N_{\text{seg}} = \frac{3600\, H_v}{\Delta}, \qquad S_{\text{index}} = N_{\text{seg}} \cdot d \cdot b_v \tag{238.7} \]
    \[ T_{\text{backfill}} = \frac{3600\, H_v \cdot f \cdot G_{\text{frame}}}{u\,F} \tag{238.8} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(H_v\)
    hours of video in the archive
    \(\Delta\)
    segment length in seconds
    \(d, b_v\)
    vector size and bytes per number in the index (4 for FP32, 2 for FP16, 1 for INT8); with product quantization (PQ) every vector becomes a fixed code, here 64 bytes
    \(f\)
    frames sampled and encoded per second of video
    \(G_{\text{frame}}\)
    FLOPs to encode one frame

    What is \(G_{\text{frame}}\)? A ViT with \(L\) layers, width \(d\) and \(T\) tokens costs about \(2L\,(12\,T d^2 + 2T^2 d)\) FLOPs. For ViT-B/16 at 224 pixels (\(L = 12\), \(d = 768\), \(T = 197\)) that is 34.9 GFLOPs per frame; for ViT-L/14 (\(L = 24\), \(d = 1024\), \(T = 257\)) it is 161.7 GFLOPs. These match the usual published figures of about 17.6 and 81 billion multiply-adds [Likely].

    Worked example: 10,000 hours, 4-second segments

    Index. \(N_{\text{seg}} = 3600 \times 10{,}000 / 4 = 9{,}000{,}000\) segments. With 512-d CLIP ViT-B/16 vectors: FP32 18.4 GB, FP16 9.2 GB, INT8 4.6 GB, PQ with 64-byte codes 0.58 GB. All of these fit in one server’s RAM; FP16 even fits on one L4.

    Backfill. At 1 frame per second: \(3.6\times10^{7}\) frames \(\times\) 34.9 GFLOPs \(= 1.26\times10^{18}\) FLOPs. One L4 at \(u = 0.5\) gives 60.5 TFLOP/s, so about 1,734 frames per second and 5.8 GPU-hours. With ViT-L/14 it is 26.7 GPU-hours. Video decoding on the CPU, not the GPU, is often the real limit of this job [Likely].

    Query. A brute-force (“flat”) scan over 9 M FP16 vectors of size 512 reads 9.2 GB; on an L4 at 80% of 300 GB/s that is 38 ms. An approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) index such as HNSW or IVF-PQ reads only a small part of it (session 150).

    Re-ranking. Re-ranking 20 candidates with an 8B VLM, each with 4 frames of 256 visual tokens plus 50 text tokens (1,074 tokens), costs \(20 \times 2 \times 8.03\times10^9 \times 1{,}074 = 345\) TFLOP: about 0.7 s on one H100 at \(u = 0.5\).

    So the numbers say: the index is small and cheap; the backfill is a one-day batch job; and the expensive part of a query is the re-ranker. Figure 3 lets you change the archive and the choices.

    Design

    Offline path: (1) decode each match and sample frames; (2) encode frames with the image encoder and pool them per segment; (3) also store cheap metadata per segment: match, teams, minute of play, and the transcribed commentary from speech recognition (Whisper), because commentators say “goal!” and player names; (4) build an ANN index (HNSW or IVF-PQ in FAISS) plus a text index over the commentary. New matches are added each night.

    Online path, in three stages, each smaller and more expensive than the one before:

    Algorithm 2: one search query
    1. input: query text \(q\), filters (team, season), \(k = 20\)
    2. \(\mathbf{e} \leftarrow \text{TextEncoder}(q)\) // a few ms on a GPU
    3. \(A \leftarrow\) ANN top 500 segments by \(\cos(\mathbf{e}, \mathbf{v}_s)\), only those that pass the filters // stage 1: about 10 ms
    4. \(A' \leftarrow\) top 500 by keyword score on commentary (BM25) // catches names and rare words
    5. \(C \leftarrow\) top 50 of \(A \cup A'\) by fused rank // stage 2: reciprocal-rank fusion
    6. merge neighboring segments of the same match into one moment with start and end times
    7. \(S \leftarrow\) VLM re-ranker scores the top 20 moments of \(C\) with a few frames each // stage 3: the costly step, about 0.7 s
    8. return top \(k\) moments of \(S\), with thumbnails and timestamps; show stage-2 results first if stage 3 is slow

    Bottleneck

    Not the index and not the load: the re-ranker. It costs about 0.7 s of an H100 per query in our example, and it grows linearly with the number of candidates and frames. Fixes: re-rank fewer candidates; use a smaller re-ranker (a 2B VLM is about 4× cheaper than the 8B); cache results of popular queries; stream stage-2 results first. The second real limit is quality in time: a 4-second segment either contains the header or it does not, and the pooled vector of a busy segment is a blur. That is why we merge neighbors and why a temporal grounding model (session 207) can refine the boundaries of the final few moments.

    Failure modes

    • Football-specific words. CLIP was not trained on “offside trap” or “nutmeg”. Detect: low recall on those query types in the test set. Act: the commentary text index, and fine-tuning the encoder on sports clip–caption pairs.
    • Replays and highlights packages. The same goal appears 5 times (live, 3 replays, a studio clip). Act: near-duplicate grouping with video fingerprints (session 209) so that one goal is one result.
    • Index and model mismatch. If you update the encoder, old vectors are no longer comparable with new query vectors. Act: version the index, and re-run the backfill (we know it costs about 6 GPU-hours, so this is affordable).
    • Recall measured only on easy queries. Act: build the test set from real editor queries, by type (event, player, tactic, emotion), and report recall per type (session 213).

    Drill 3: real-time key-moment alerts on live broadcast

    The prompt. “During live matches, send an alert to editors (and later to fans) within seconds of a goal, a red card or a big save. Design it.”

    Requirements

    Load: up to 50 live matches at the same time on a busy day. Events: about 10 alert-worthy moments per 90-minute match (our assumption for the drill). Latency: the alert should arrive within about 10 s of the moment; social media is often faster than that, so every second counts. Quality: almost no missed goals, and very few false alerts, because each false alert to millions of fans is visible and costly. In this drill the key quality number is false alerts per match, not accuracy per frame.

    Numbers

    Two budgets matter: time and false alarms. First, the time. A detector looks at a window of the last \(w\) seconds and fires when its score passes a threshold \(\theta\). For an event that raises the score by \(A\) from the moment it starts, the windowed mean passes \(\theta\) after about \(\theta w / A\) seconds. The full delay is:

    \[ D_{\text{alert}} \approx \underbrace{\frac{\theta\, w}{A}}_{\text{evidence}} + \underbrace{\Delta_{\text{hop}}}_{\text{wait for next check}} + t_{\text{model}} + t_{\text{verify}} + t_{\text{send}} \tag{238.9} \]

    Second, the false alarms. If the score is pure noise with standard deviation \(\sigma\) per second, the mean of \(w\) seconds has standard deviation \(\sigma/\sqrt{w}\). With one check per second over a match of \(T\) seconds, the expected number of noise crossings is at most:

    \[ \E[\text{noise alarms}] \;\lesssim\; T \cdot Q\!\left( \frac{\theta \sqrt{w}}{\sigma} \right), \qquad Q(z) = P(Z \gt z),\ Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1) \tag{238.10} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(w\)
    the length of the evidence window, in seconds
    \(\theta\)
    the alert threshold on the windowed mean score
    \(A\)
    how much a real event raises the per-second score
    \(\Delta_{\text{hop}}\)
    time between two checks of the detector (1 s here)
    \(Q(z)\)
    the tail probability of a standard normal variable

    Read the two formulas together: a longer window \(w\) and a higher threshold \(\theta\) push the noise term down very fast (it is a Gaussian tail), but they add delay linearly. This is the core trade-off of the whole drill. Formula (238.10) only covers random noise. Real false alarms often come from look-alikes: a replay of an earlier goal, a shot that hits the post, a loud crowd. Those are not noise, so no threshold on noise statistics removes them. Duration helps (a replay may be shorter than the real moment), and a second, smarter model helps more.

    Third, compute. With one GPU giving \(uF\) FLOP/s and a frame encoder costing \(G_{\text{frame}}\) FLOPs:

    \[ \text{streams per GPU} = \left\lfloor \frac{u\,F}{f \cdot G_{\text{frame}}} \right\rfloor \tag{238.11} \]

    For ViT-B/16 at \(f = 2\) frames per second on an L4: \(\lfloor 60.5\times10^{12} / (2 \times 34.9\times10^{9}) \rfloor = 866\) streams. Even ViT-L/14 at 2 frames per second gives 187. So with 50 matches, the cheap always-on detector is not the cost. The cost is the heavy model that checks candidates, and that runs only a few times per minute.

    Worked example: the delay–false-alarm trade-off

    Take \(\sigma = 0.3\), \(A = 1\), a 90-minute match (\(T = 5{,}400\) s). With \(w = 1\), \(\theta = 0.7\): \(z = 0.7 / 0.3 = 2.33\), \(Q(2.33) \approx 0.0098\), so up to \(5{,}400 \times 0.0098 = 53\) noise crossings per match: useless. With \(w = 4\), \(\theta = 0.7\): \(z = 0.7 \times 2 / 0.3 = 4.67\), so 0.008 noise alarms per match; the evidence delay is \(\theta w / A = 2.8\) s. With \(w = 8\), \(\theta = 0.6\): \(z = 5.66\) and an evidence delay of 4.8 s. Figure 4 runs these settings on a simulated match, where look-alikes also exist.

    The simulation shows the three regimes of formula (238.9) and (238.10): noise-limited (short windows), look-alike-limited (medium windows), and miss-limited (windows longer than the event). In a real design, we do not ask one threshold to do everything. We use a cascade: a cheap, always-on detector tuned for high recall (it may fire on look-alikes), and a heavy verifier that runs only on its candidates and is tuned for precision.

    Design

    Per live match: ingest the broadcast stream; decode and sample 2 frames per second, plus audio; run a light video model and an audio model (crowd noise and commentator excitement are strong, fast signals) every second over a sliding window; fuse their scores. When the fused score passes a low threshold, send a candidate (the last 20 s of frames and audio transcript) to the verifier: a VLM prompted to answer “was there a goal, red card or big save, and is this live play or a replay?”. Only verified events become alerts. An editor can approve alerts for the fan channel in the first weeks, which also creates labels.

    Algorithm 3: streaming alert loop for one match
    1. input: live stream, window \(w\), low threshold \(\theta_1\), refractory time \(r\)
    2. every \(\Delta_{\text{hop}} = 1\) s do
    3. \(s_t \leftarrow \text{fuse}(\text{VideoModel}(\text{last } w \text{ s of frames}),\ \text{AudioModel}(\text{last } w \text{ s}))\)
    4. if \(s_t \gt \theta_1\) and no alert in the last \(r\) seconds then
    5. clip \(\leftarrow\) last 20 s of frames + transcript // small: send to the verifier queue
    6. \((y, \text{type}) \leftarrow \text{VLMVerifier}(\text{clip})\) // 1 to 2 s; also says “replay or live”
    7. if \(y\) = yes then send alert(type, \(t\)) // at-most-once, with an event id
    8. log \(s_t\), candidates and verifier answers // for threshold tuning and labels

    Bottleneck

    Not compute (one L4 can serve hundreds of streams for the light detector, by (238.11)). The bottleneck is the delay–false-alarm trade-off, plus the verifier’s latency at peak: when a goal is scored in 10 matches at the same minute, 10 verifier calls arrive at once. Give the verifier its own GPU pool, sized for bursts, and a time limit; if it times out, send the alert to the editor queue instead of to fans.

    Failure modes

    • Replays fire again after the real moment. Act: the refractory window, a “replay” output in the verifier, and a broadcast graphics detector (replays often start with a logo wipe [Likely]).
    • Stream problems: the feed freezes or lags. Detect: frame timestamps against wall-clock time. Act: pause alerts for that match; never alert on a frozen frame.
    • Disallowed goals (offside, video review). Act: send a correction alert linked to the first one; for fans, consider waiting for the restart of play.
    • Duplicates when the service restarts. Act: an event id per match and moment, and at-most-once delivery.
    • Drift: a new competition with different graphics or camera style. Detect: the rate of candidates and verifier rejections per match moves. Act: re-tune thresholds on the new data.

    Code: the three drills in a few lines

    The whiteboard numbers above come from these three programs. The first is the capacity plan of Drill 1, written in plain Python (it is just arithmetic).

    import math

def chat_capacity(lam, n_in, n_out, hit=0.0, tpot_max=0.050, ttft_max=1.0,
                  N=8.03e9, L=32, n_kv=8, d_h=128, b=2,
                  mem=80e9, bw=3.35e12, flops=989e12, u=0.5, eta=0.8, t0=1e-3):
    """Back-of-envelope plan for one model replica (formulas 238.1 to 238.6)."""
    M_W = N * b                                   # weight bytes
    s_kv = 2 * L * n_kv * d_h * b                 # KV bytes per token (238.2)
    L_bar = n_in + n_out / 2                      # mean context while decoding
    C_req = 2 * N * (n_in * (1 - hit) + n_out)    # FLOPs per request (238.5)
    c = lam * C_req / (u * flops)                 # compute utilization
    kv_read = lam * n_out * L_bar * s_kv / (eta * bw)
    if c >= 1 or kv_read >= 1:
        return None                               # this replica is overloaded
    floor = t0 + M_W / (eta * bw)                 # read all weights once
    tpot = max(floor / (1 - kv_read), t0 / (1 - c))          # (238.4)
    ttft = (t0 + 2 * N * n_in * (1 - hit) / (u * flops)) / (1 - c)
    B = lam * n_out * tpot                        # Little's law (238.1)
    kv_bytes = B * (n_in + n_out) * s_kv          # (238.3)
    ok = tpot <= tpot_max and ttft <= ttft_max and kv_bytes <= 0.9 * mem - M_W
    return dict(ok=ok, c=c, tpot=tpot, ttft=ttft, B=B, kv_gb=kv_bytes / 1e9)

def replicas(lam, n_in, n_out, headroom=0.8, **kw):
    lo, hi = 0.0, 1000.0                          # bisection for mu* (238.6)
    for _ in range(60):
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2
        r = chat_capacity(mid, n_in, n_out, **kw)
        lo, hi = (mid, hi) if (r and r["ok"]) else (lo, mid)
    return lo, math.ceil(lam / (headroom * lo))

mu, R = replicas(20, 1000, 300)
print(f"one H100: mu* = {mu:.1f} req/s, replicas for 20 req/s: {R}")
r = chat_capacity(20 / R, 1000, 300)
print({k: round(v, 3) for k, v in r.items() if k != "ok"})
mu, R = replicas(20, 1000, 300, hit=0.5)
print(f"with 50% prefix-cache hits: mu* = {mu:.1f}, replicas: {R}")
# one H100: mu* = 22.9 req/s, replicas for 20 req/s: 2
# {'c': 0.422, 'tpot': 0.008, 'ttft': 0.058, 'B': 25.236, 'kv_gb': 4.3}
# with 50% prefix-cache hits: mu* = 37.2, replicas: 1

    The second is the two-stage search of Drill 2 in PyTorch: a coarse scan over a 4× smaller INT8 index, then an exact re-rank of the top 100. The “ground truth” here is what an exact FP32 scan would return.

    import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

torch.manual_seed(0)
n, d, q = 50_000, 256, 200
db = F.normalize(torch.randn(n, d), dim=1)            # segment embeddings (fp32)
target = torch.randint(0, n, (q,))
queries = F.normalize(db[target] + 1.5 * torch.randn(q, d) / d**0.5, dim=1)

# exact search: the ground truth for "what the fp32 index would return"
exact = (queries @ db.T).topk(10, dim=1).indices

# stage 1: int8 index (4x smaller than fp32), coarse scores, keep top 100
scale = db.abs().amax(dim=1, keepdim=True) / 127
db_q = torch.round(db / scale).to(torch.int8)         # what we store
coarse = queries @ (db_q.float() * scale).T
cand = coarse.topk(100, dim=1).indices                 # (q, 100)

# stage 2: rerank the 100 candidates with exact fp32 vectors
fine = torch.einsum("qd,qkd->qk", queries, db[cand])
top10 = cand.gather(1, fine.topk(10, dim=1).indices)

def recall(a, b):  # share of the exact top-10 that we also returned
    return sum(len(set(x.tolist()) & set(y.tolist())) for x, y in zip(a, b)) / a.numel()

print("index MB fp32 / int8:", db.numel() * 4 / 1e6, db_q.numel() / 1e6)
print("recall@10, int8 only:", recall(exact, coarse.topk(10, dim=1).indices))
print("recall@10, int8 + rerank:", recall(exact, top10))
# index MB fp32 / int8: 51.2 12.8
# recall@10, int8 only: 0.984
# recall@10, int8 + rerank: 1.0

    The third is the streaming detector of Drill 3: a windowed mean (a 1-D convolution), a threshold, and a refractory time. It contains one real moment at second 200 and one look-alike at second 400.

    import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

torch.manual_seed(0)
T, sigma = 600, 0.3                       # 10 minutes of per-second scores
score = sigma * torch.randn(T)
score[200:208] += 1.0                     # a real key moment (8 s)
score[400:405] += 0.8                     # a replay that looks similar (5 s)

def alerts(score, w, theta, refractory=30):
    kernel = torch.full((1, 1, w), 1.0 / w)
    mean = F.conv1d(score.view(1, 1, -1), kernel).view(-1)  # mean[i] covers i..i+w-1
    out, last = [], -10**9
    for i, m in enumerate(mean.tolist()):
        t = i + w                         # time when this window is complete
        if m > theta and t - last >= refractory:
            out.append(t)
            last = t
    return out

for w, theta in [(1, 0.7), (4, 0.7), (8, 0.6)]:
    print(w, theta, alerts(score, w, theta))
# 1 0.7 [60, 201, 338, 394, 453, 539]   noise alarms everywhere
# 4 0.7 [203, 405]                      the replay fires too
# 8 0.6 [205]                           only the real moment, 5 s after it starts

    Note the last line: the alert comes at second 205, five seconds after the moment starts. Formula (238.9) predicts \(\theta w / A = 0.6 \times 8 / 1 = 4.8\) s of evidence delay, and the windows are checked once per second, so the first window that passes is the one ending at 205.

    Common mistakes

    • Drawing boxes before numbers. The design for 1 request per second and for 1,000 is not the same. Do the numbers step first, even if roughly.
    • Using average load. Systems fail at peak. Plan for the peak and for p95 request sizes, and keep headroom.
    • Forgetting the prompt. Many candidates size an LLM service by output tokens only. In Drill 1, prompts are 77% of the compute.
    • Ignoring floors. No batch size makes TPOT lower than the time to read the weights once. If the floor is above the target, the hardware or the model must change.
    • “Accuracy” as the only quality number. For search it is recall@k by query type; for alerts it is false alerts per match and delay; for chat it is a judge score plus user signals.
    • No failure modes, or only “the GPU dies”. The interesting failures are data failures: replays, new vocabulary, drift, duplicates.
    • One big model for everything. Most real systems are cascades: cheap and wide first, expensive and narrow last.
    • Invented precision. “The verifier takes 1.37 s” sounds false. Give ranges and say how you would measure.

    Interview questions

    What is Little’s law and why is it useful for LLM serving?basic
    In a stable system, the average number of items inside equals the arrival rate times the average time in the system: \(B = \lambda W\) (238.1). It needs no assumption about the arrival distribution. For LLM serving it turns product numbers (requests per second, latency) into the number of concurrent requests, which sets the batch size and the KV-cache memory. Example: 20 req/s, 300 output tokens at 10.6 ms each gives about 63 requests decoding at the same time.
    Compute the KV cache per token for Llama 3.1 8B in BF16. How much for a 1,300-token request?basic
    \(2 \times 32 \text{ layers} \times 8 \text{ KV heads} \times 128 \times 2 \text{ bytes} = 131{,}072\) bytes = 128 KiB per token (238.2). For 1,300 tokens: about 170 MB. Without GQA (32 KV heads) it would be 4× more, which is why GQA matters so much for serving.
    Your chat service misses its TTFT target but has a lot of TPOT slack. What do you do?core
    TTFT is set by prefill compute and queueing; TPOT slack means decode is not the problem. Cut prefill work: prefix caching (with a prefix-aware router so hits stay high), shorter system prompts, chunked prefill so that long prompts do not block others, FP8 compute, more replicas. At scale, disaggregate prefill and decode so prefill GPUs can be added alone. Do not reach for speculative decoding: it improves TPOT at the cost of extra compute.
    Why can no batch size make an 8B BF16 model reach 50 ms TPOT on an L4?core
    Each decode step must read all 16.06 GB of weights. At 80% of 300 GB/s that takes 67 ms, plus overhead: the TPOT floor is about 67.9 ms. Batching shares that read across more requests, so it raises throughput, but it cannot make one step faster than one read of the weights. Options: quantize weights (INT4 is about 4 GB, so a floor of about 18 ms), a smaller model, or a GPU with more bandwidth.
    Size the index for natural-language search over 10,000 hours of video.core
    State the segment length and vector size. With 4-second segments, \(3600 \times 10{,}000 / 4 = 9\)M vectors (238.7). At 512-d: FP32 18.4 GB, FP16 9.2 GB, INT8 4.6 GB, PQ 64-byte codes 0.58 GB. All fit on one server, so the index is not the hard part; recall per query type and the cost of re-ranking are.
    Why use a two-stage (retrieve then re-rank) design for video search?core
    A dual encoder (text vector against pre-computed segment vectors) can score millions of segments in milliseconds, but it compresses each segment into one vector and misses fine details such as “from a corner” or “last minute”. A cross-encoder or VLM that sees query and frames together is much more accurate but costs about 17 TFLOP per candidate for an 8B VLM in our example, so it can only look at tens of candidates. Retrieve wide and cheap, then re-rank narrow and expensive. Add keyword search on commentary for names.
    Explain the trade-off between alert delay and false alarms in live key-moment detection.core
    The detector needs evidence. A window of \(w\) seconds and threshold \(\theta\) add about \(\theta w / A\) seconds of delay (238.9), linear in \(w\). The noise false-alarm rate falls like a Gaussian tail in \(\theta\sqrt{w}/\sigma\) (238.10), much faster. So a few seconds of delay buys a huge drop in noise alarms. Look-alikes (replays, near misses) are not noise and need a different fix: duration, a replay detector, or a verifier model. If the window becomes longer than the event, real events get missed.
    How would you evaluate the live alert system before and after launch?advanced
    Before: replay recorded full matches through the real streaming code (not offline clips) and measure recall per event type, false alerts per match and the delay distribution (median and p95) against human-labeled event times; check by competition and broadcaster. Tune thresholds on some matches and report on others. After: shadow mode (alerts go to editors only), then a canary. Track editor rejections, corrections, verifier rejection rate and stream lag as drift signals. The metrics must match the product promise: “no false goal alerts” is per match, not per frame.
    One of your two chat replicas fails at peak. What happens and how should you have planned?advanced
    The other replica gets all 20 req/s. One H100 can meet the targets up to about 22.9 req/s in our model, so it survives but at about 87% of its capacity with no headroom: TTFT rises from about 58 ms toward about 215 ms and any burst breaks the SLO. In-flight streams of the failed replica are lost; clients must retry. If the SLO must hold during one failure, plan N+1 (here 3 replicas), or allow graceful degradation: admission control for low tiers, a shorter max output, or a smaller fallback model.
    The interviewer says: “now add image input to the chat API”. What changes in your numbers?advanced
    An image becomes visual tokens (hundreds to thousands per image, depending on the encoder and resolution), and those are prompt tokens: they add prefill FLOPs (\(2N\) per token in the LLM) and KV-cache memory exactly like text tokens, plus the vision encoder’s own cost. So TTFT and compute grow, and the cache hit rate may drop (images are rarely repeated, but the same image is reused across turns, so cache it). Options: fewer tokens per image (pooling, lower resolution), a separate encoder pool, and caching the image embeddings and their KV across turns.
    How do you choose between more replicas and a quantized model to cut cost?advanced
    First find the binding limit. If it is compute (prefill), FP8 weight and activation quantization raises usable FLOP/s; weight-only INT4 helps bandwidth (TPOT floor) more than compute. If it is memory bandwidth (TPOT floor near target), weight quantization helps directly. Then check quality on your own evaluation set, because quantization errors are task-dependent. More replicas always work but cost linearly; quantization is a one-time engineering cost with a quality risk. Often you do both: quantize, then size replicas with the new numbers.

    Key takeaways

    • Use one frame every time: requirements, numbers, design, bottleneck, failure modes. Numbers come before boxes.
    • A few formulas cover most questions: Little’s law (238.1), KV bytes (238.2), the TPOT floor and step time (238.4), compute utilization (238.5), replicas with headroom (238.6).
    • Drill 1: for chat, prompts are most of the compute, so prefix caching and chunked prefill matter more than faster decoding; 20 req/s of Llama 3.1 8B needs about 2 H100s with our assumptions.
    • Drill 2: a 10,000-hour archive is only 9 M segments and a few GB of index; the hard parts are recall on sports language and the cost of re-ranking.
    • Drill 3: delay grows linearly with the evidence window, noise alarms fall like a Gaussian tail; look-alikes need a cascade with a verifier, not a higher threshold.
    • Name the binding limit and the failure modes before you are asked, and answer follow-ups by changing the numbers, not the habits.
