    Policy gradient and REINFORCE

    How do you train a language model from a score instead of from a correct example? The answer is the policy gradient: push up the log-probability of answers that scored well, push down the ones that scored badly. This session derives it with the log-derivative trick, builds REINFORCE for whole generated answers, and shows why baselines and advantages are needed to make it work in practice.

    Time: 2.5 to 3 hours (reading 2 hours, exercises 45 minutes)Level: Beginner to interview levelBefore this: Sessions 3, 4 and 63

    What you will learn

    • Why we cannot backpropagate through sampling and a reward, and what the policy gradient does instead
    • The log-derivative trick, step by step: the gradient of an expected reward is an expected reward times a gradient of a log-probability
    • REINFORCE for a whole generated answer: one reward, many tokens, one surrogate loss
    • Why the estimate is so noisy, and how a reward shift can make the noise more than 400 times larger
    • Baselines: why subtracting them adds no bias, and which baseline gives the lowest variance
    • The advantage, and the group baselines used for LLMs: mean, leave-one-out (RLOO) and GRPO normalization

    Why this matters

    In session 63 we described text generation as a game: the language model is a policy, each next token is an action, and at the end of the answer a reward says how good the answer was. This session answers the next question: how do we change the weights so that the reward goes up?

    In supervised fine-tuning (session 54) we had a correct answer to copy, so the loss was a simple cross-entropy. Now we only have a score: “this answer to the math problem is right (1)” or “wrong (0)”, or a number from a reward model. The score comes from a checker or another network, not from a formula we can differentiate. And the answer itself was sampled: the model drew random tokens. There is no gradient through a random draw.

    The policy gradient solves both problems with one identity, the log-derivative trick. The result is surprisingly simple: sample answers, score them, and do weighted maximum likelihood on your own samples, with a large weight for good answers and a negative weight for bad ones. The oldest version of this idea is REINFORCE (Williams, 1992). [Certain] Every RL method for LLMs that you will meet later in this track is built on it: PPO (session 66), the RLHF pipeline of InstructGPT, RLOO, ReMax, and GRPO, the method used to train DeepSeek’s reasoning models (session 70).

    Interview tipTypical questions: “Derive the policy gradient.”, “Why does subtracting a baseline not change the expected gradient?”, “Why is REINFORCE high-variance?”, “What is the advantage?”, “How does GRPO compute its advantage, and why does it not need a critic?”. All of them are answered below, with numbers.

    The plan: first the goal (an expected reward), then the trick that gives its gradient, then REINFORCE for a whole generated answer. Then we see the big weakness, variance, and fix it with baselines and the advantage. The next session, session 65, replaces the simple baselines here with a learned critic.

    The goal: maximize the expected reward

    Write \(\pi_\theta(y \mid x)\) for the probability that the model with weights \(\theta\) writes the answer \(y\) to the prompt \(x\). The answer \(y = (y_1, \dots, y_T)\) is a sequence of tokens. A reward function \(R(x, y)\) gives one number for the finished answer. We want weights that make good answers likely. Because answers are random, “good on average” is the right goal:

    \[ J(\theta) = \E_{x \sim \mathcal{D}}\ \E_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)} \big[ R(x, y) \big] = \E_{x \sim \mathcal{D}} \sum_{y} \pi_\theta(y \mid x)\, R(x, y) \tag{64.1} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(J(\theta)\)
    the objective: the average reward the model gets; we want to make it larger (gradient ascent)
    \(\mathcal{D}\)
    the set of prompts we train on
    \(\pi_\theta(y \mid x)\)
    the policy: the language model’s probability of the whole answer \(y\)
    \(R(x, y)\)
    the reward of the finished answer: a correctness check, a unit test, or a reward model score
    \(\sum_y\)
    a sum over all possible answers. There are astronomically many, so we can never compute it exactly

    Look at where \(\theta\) appears. It is not inside \(R\): the reward does not care how the answer was produced, it only reads the text. It is inside the probabilities \(\pi_\theta(y \mid x)\). So the gradient must flow through the probabilities of the answers, and never through the reward. This is why the reward can be any black box: a Python checker, a compiler, a human click.

    AnalogyA cook tries recipes and the guests give a score. The cook cannot take the “derivative of the guests”. But the cook can change how often each recipe is cooked: cook the high-score ones more often, the low-score ones less. The policy gradient is exactly this rule, made precise.

    The log-derivative trick

    We want \(\nabla_\theta J\). Take one prompt and drop \(x\) to keep the notation short. The gradient of a sum is the sum of gradients, and \(R\) does not depend on \(\theta\):

    \[ \nabla_\theta J = \sum_y R(y)\, \nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(y). \]

    This is correct but useless as it stands: it is a sum over all answers, not an average we can estimate by sampling. The trick is one line of calculus. Since \(\nabla \log f = \nabla f / f\), we can write

    \[ \nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(y) = \pi_\theta(y)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y) \tag{64.2} \]

    Put this back into the sum. The factor \(\pi_\theta(y)\) appears again, and a sum weighted by \(\pi_\theta(y)\) is an expectation:

    \[ \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \sum_y \pi_\theta(y)\, R(y)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y) = \E_{y \sim \pi_\theta} \Big[ R(y)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y) \Big] \tag{64.3} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y)\)
    the score function: the direction in weight space that makes the answer \(y\) more likely. It is the same gradient that SFT computes when \(y\) is the target
    \(R(y)\)
    a plain number that scales that direction: large reward, big push up; zero reward, no push; negative reward, push down
    \(\E_{y \sim \pi_\theta}\)
    an average over answers sampled from the current model, so we can estimate it with a few samples

    This is the whole idea. To estimate the gradient, sample answers from the model, compute the log-probability gradient of each one (ordinary backprop), multiply by its reward, and average. No gradient of the reward is needed, and no gradient through the sampling either. The same identity is also called the score-function estimator or the likelihood-ratio gradient. Its general form for many-step problems is the policy gradient theorem (Sutton et al., 1999). [Certain]

    What the score looks like for a softmax

    A language model’s last layer is a softmax over logits \(\mathbf{z}\). For one choice \(a\) with \(\pi = \softmax(\mathbf{z})\), the gradient of \(\log \pi_a\) with respect to the logits is very simple (you met it as the cross-entropy gradient in session 3):

    \[ \nabla_{\mathbf{z}} \log \pi_a = \mathbf{e}_a - \pi, \qquad \text{so} \qquad \nabla_{\mathbf{z}} J = \E_{a \sim \pi}\big[ R(a)\,(\mathbf{e}_a - \pi) \big] \tag{64.4} \]

    Here \(\mathbf{e}_a\) is the one-hot vector of the chosen answer. In words: raise the logit of the chosen answer by \((1 - \pi_a)\), lower every other logit by its probability, and scale all of it by the reward.

    Worked example: the trick on three answers

    A prompt has three possible answers A, B, C with probabilities \(\pi = (0.2, 0.5, 0.3)\). Only A is correct: \(R = (1, 0, 0)\). So the expected reward is \(J = 0.2\).

    Exact gradient. For a softmax, \(\partial J / \partial z_j = \pi_j (R_j - J)\). This gives \((0.2 \times 0.8,\ 0.5 \times (-0.2),\ 0.3 \times (-0.2)) = (0.16, -0.10, -0.06)\): raise A, lower B and C.

    The REINFORCE view. If we sample A, the score is \(\mathbf{e}_A - \pi = (0.8, -0.5, -0.3)\) and the reward is 1, so this sample’s estimate is \((0.8, -0.5, -0.3)\). If we sample B or C, the reward is 0, so the estimate is \((0, 0, 0)\). Average over the three cases with their probabilities: \(0.2 \times (0.8, -0.5, -0.3) = (0.16, -0.10, -0.06)\). Exactly the true gradient, as formula (64.3) promises.

    But notice the cost: 80% of the samples give a gradient of zero, and the other 20% give a gradient five times larger than the mean. Each single estimate is far from the truth. This is the variance problem we fix later.

    Figure 2 turns the trick into a learning algorithm on the simplest RL problem, a multi-armed bandit: a machine with four arms A–D. Pulling arm \(a\) pays 1 with probability \(\mu_a = (0.2, 0.5, 0.8, 0.4)\) and 0 otherwise. The policy is a softmax over four logits, it starts uniform, and it does not know \(\mu\). Each iteration samples a batch of pulls, builds the estimate (64.4) from them, and takes one gradient step. One episode is one pull, so a bandit is exactly the answer-level view of an LLM: prompt in, one whole answer out, one score. The baseline switch is explained in the section on baselines below; leave it off for now.

    REINFORCE for a whole generated answer

    For a language model, an “action” in formula (64.3) is a whole answer of many tokens. The model writes it one token at a time, so its probability is a product, and its log-probability is a sum:

    \[ \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log \pi_\theta\big(y_t \mid x, y_{\lt t}\big) \tag{64.5} \]

    This is the same sum of token log-probabilities that SFT uses. Each term comes from one softmax, at one position, with the prompt and the tokens written so far as context. Put it into (64.3) and replace the expectation by an average over \(N\) sampled answers \(y^{(1)}, \dots, y^{(N)}\):

    \[ \hat{\mathbf{g}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} R\big(x, y^{(i)}\big) \sum_{t=1}^{T_i} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta\big(y^{(i)}_t \mid x, y^{(i)}_{\lt t}\big) \tag{64.6} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\hat{\mathbf{g}}\)
    our estimate of \(\nabla_\theta J\); the hat means “estimated from samples”
    \(y^{(i)}\)
    the \(i\)-th answer sampled from the current model for the prompt \(x\)
    \(T_i\)
    the length of that answer in tokens, including the end-of-sequence token
    \(R(x, y^{(i)})\)
    one number for the whole answer, used as the weight of every token in it

    In code nobody writes the gradient (64.6) by hand. We build a surrogate loss: a scalar whose ordinary gradient equals \(-\hat{\mathbf{g}}\). The rewards are constants (no gradient flows into them), so

    \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{PG}}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} R\big(x, y^{(i)}\big)\, \log \pi_\theta\big(y^{(i)} \mid x\big) \tag{64.7} \]

    Compare it with the SFT loss \(-\log \pi_\theta(y^{\ast} \mid x)\) for a human-written answer \(y^{\ast}\). REINFORCE is SFT on the model’s own samples, with each sample weighted by its reward. With rewards in \(\{0, 1\}\) it is exactly “SFT on the answers that passed the check”. This is why the method is also called reward-weighted maximum likelihood.

    Watch outThe value of \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{PG}}\) has no meaning on its own. It is not a loss that should go down. It can go up while the reward improves, because the samples change every step. Monitor the reward, not this number.
    Algorithm 1: REINFORCE for a language model (no baseline yet)
    1. input: policy \(\pi_\theta\), prompts \(\mathcal{D}\), reward function \(R\), samples per prompt \(N\), learning rate \(\eta\)
    2. repeat:
    3.   pick a batch of prompts \(x\) from \(\mathcal{D}\)
    4.   for each \(x\): sample \(N\) answers \(y^{(i)} \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)\) token by token // no gradient while sampling
    5.   score each answer: \(r_i \leftarrow R(x, y^{(i)})\) // a black box; treat as a constant
    6.   run one forward pass on prompt + answer and sum the token log-probabilities, formula (64.5)
    7.   \(\mathcal{L} \leftarrow -\frac{1}{N}\sum_i r_i \log \pi_\theta(y^{(i)} \mid x)\) // formula (64.7)
    8.   \(\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}\) // ascent on J
    9.   throw the samples away: they came from the old \(\theta\) // on-policy

    The last line matters. Formula (64.3) is an expectation under the current policy. After one update, the old samples come from a slightly different policy, and reusing them gives a biased gradient. PPO (session 66) adds an importance ratio and clipping so that each batch can be used for several updates.

    Worked example: one answer, four tokens

    The model writes “The answer is 5” with token probabilities \(0.9, 0.6, 0.8, 0.5\). The answer’s probability is \(0.9 \times 0.6 \times 0.8 \times 0.5 = 0.216\), and its log-probability is \(-0.105 - 0.511 - 0.223 - 0.693 = -1.532\) (formula 64.5).

    The answer is correct, \(R = 1\). The gradient on each chosen token’s logit is \(R\,(1 - \pi_t) = 0.1, 0.4, 0.2, 0.5\). The uncertain final token “5” gets the biggest push, and the confident “The” the smallest.

    Now suppose this answer came from a group of 8 samples where 2 were correct, and we subtract the group mean \(b = 0.25\). The weight becomes \(A = 0.75\) and the pushes become \(0.075, 0.3, 0.15, 0.375\). A wrong answer with the same probabilities except a final “6” at 0.3 gets \(A = -0.25\) and pushes \(-0.025, -0.1, -0.05, -0.175\): it is now made less likely, which plain REINFORCE with 0/1 rewards never does.

    Credit assignment, and the reward-to-go

    Every token of an answer gets the same weight. The model cannot see which token made the answer right or wrong: that must be learned from many samples, where good tokens appear more often in good answers. This is the credit assignment problem, and it is one big reason for the noise.

    One free improvement comes from causality: an action at step \(t\) cannot change rewards that came before it. If rewards \(r_{t'}\) arrive at several steps, each token only needs the rewards after it, the reward-to-go:

    \[ \nabla_\theta J = \E\Big[ \sum_{t=1}^{T} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t \mid s_t)\ \hat{G}_t \Big], \qquad \hat{G}_t = \sum_{t'=t}^{T} r_{t'} \tag{64.8} \]

    Here \(s_t = (x, y_{\lt t})\) is the state: the prompt and the text so far. The past rewards that we dropped have expectation zero in the gradient, so this is still unbiased, with less noise. For an LLM with one reward at the end, \(\hat{G}_t = R\) for every token, and (64.8) is the same as (64.6). It starts to matter when there are rewards along the way, for example a per-token KL penalty that keeps the model close to its SFT starting point (used in RLHF, session 67), or rewards for each step of a solution (session 73). LLM work usually uses no discount, \(\gamma = 1\). [Likely]

    Why REINFORCE is so noisy

    The estimator (64.6) is unbiased: on average it points in the right direction. But one batch can point almost anywhere. Three things make the variance large for LLMs:

    • Sampling. Each answer is one random draw from a huge space. Two runs of the same prompt can give very different answers and rewards.
    • One reward for hundreds of tokens. The whole sum of token scores is multiplied by one number, so the noise of every token adds up. Long reasoning answers have thousands of tokens.
    • The reward’s offset. If all rewards are large and positive, every sample is pushed up, good and bad alike. Only the small differences between the pushes carry the signal; the large common part is pure noise.

    The third point is surprising, so let us measure it. Shift every reward by a constant \(c\): \(R'(y) = R(y) + c\). The expected gradient does not change, because \(\E[c\, \nabla \log \pi] = c \cdot 0 = 0\) (we prove this in the next section). But the variance can change enormously.

    Worked example: how a constant ruins the estimate

    Take the three-answer problem again: \(\pi = (0.2, 0.5, 0.3)\), \(R = (1, 0, 0)\). For a single sample, the estimate \(R(a)(\mathbf{e}_a - \pi)\) has mean \((0.16, -0.10, -0.06)\) and total variance (the sum of the variances of the three components) \(0.1568\).

    Now add \(c = 10\) to every reward, so the rewards are \((11, 10, 10)\). The mean is still \((0.16, -0.10, -0.06)\), but the total variance is \(66.08\): about 421 times larger. You would need about 421 times more samples for the same accuracy. Figure 4 lets you try any shift.

    This is why reward scale and centering are never details in RL for LLMs. A reward model that outputs scores around +5 for every answer would make plain REINFORCE nearly useless. The fix is to subtract something.

    Baselines that add no bias

    A baseline \(b\) is a number we subtract from the reward before using it as a weight. The key fact: the score function has mean zero. The probabilities sum to 1 for every \(\theta\), so

    \[ \E_{y \sim \pi_\theta}\big[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y) \big] = \sum_y \pi_\theta(y)\, \frac{\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(y)}{\pi_\theta(y)} = \nabla_\theta \sum_y \pi_\theta(y) = \nabla_\theta 1 = 0 \tag{64.9} \]

    So for any \(b\) that does not depend on the sampled answer \(y\), subtracting it changes nothing on average:

    \[ \E_{y \sim \pi_\theta}\big[ (R(y) - b)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y) \big] = \nabla_\theta J(\theta) - b \cdot 0 = \nabla_\theta J(\theta) \tag{64.10} \]

    The baseline may depend on the prompt \(x\) (or the state \(s_t\)), on the weights, on other samples, but not on the answer whose gradient it multiplies. If it does, (64.9) no longer applies and the gradient becomes biased.

    AnalogyA teacher grades on a curve. If the whole class scored 90 on an easy test, a 90 says nothing; a 95 is good and an 85 is bad. Subtracting the class average does not change who did better than whom. It only removes the part of the score that everyone shares, and that part was noise for learning.

    Which baseline is best?

    Every valid baseline keeps the mean, but they reduce the variance by different amounts. Minimizing the total variance of \((R - b)\,\mathbf{s}\), with \(\mathbf{s} = \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y)\), over \(b\) is a one-variable quadratic problem. Its solution is (Greensmith et al., 2004) [Certain]:

    \[ b^{\ast} = \frac{\E\big[ R(y)\, \lVert \mathbf{s}(y) \rVert^2 \big]}{\E\big[ \lVert \mathbf{s}(y) \rVert^2 \big]} \tag{64.11} \]

    It is the average reward, weighted by how large each answer’s score vector is. In practice nobody computes it: the plain average reward \(b = \E[R] = J\) is almost as good and easy to estimate. That average, for the current prompt, is exactly the state value \(V(x)\) of the next session.

    Worked example: three baselines on the same problem

    Same three answers, \(\pi = (0.2, 0.5, 0.3)\), \(R = (1, 0, 0)\). The squared lengths of the score vectors \(\mathbf{e}_a - \pi\) are \(0.98, 0.38, 0.78\).

    • No baseline, \(b = 0\): total variance \(0.1568\).
    • Mean reward, \(b = J = 0.2\): total variance \(0.1032\), a 34% reduction.
    • Optimal, \(b^{\ast} = (0.2 \times 1 \times 0.98) / (0.2 \times 0.98 + 0.5 \times 0.38 + 0.3 \times 0.78) = 0.196 / 0.62 = 0.316\): total variance \(0.0948\).
    • A bad baseline, \(b = 1\): total variance \(0.3848\). Too large a baseline is worse than none.

    With the reward shift \(c = 10\) from before, the mean baseline \(b = 10.2\) brings the variance from \(66.08\) back down to \(0.1032\). A baseline makes the method insensitive to the reward’s offset.

    The advantage: how much better than expected

    With the mean baseline, the weight on an answer is \(R - V\): the reward we got minus the reward we expected. This quantity has a name, the advantage. In RL terms, for a state \(s\) and an action \(a\):

    \[ A(s, a) = Q(s, a) - V(s), \qquad \text{for a whole LLM answer:} \quad A(x, y) = R(x, y) - V(x) \tag{64.12} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(Q(s, a)\)
    the expected return if we take action \(a\) in state \(s\) and then follow the policy
    \(V(s)\)
    the expected return from state \(s\) when the policy chooses the action itself; the average of \(Q\) over actions
    \(A(s, a)\)
    positive: this action was better than the policy’s average, so make it more likely. Negative: worse than average, make it less likely. Zero: as expected, no change
    \(V(x)\)
    for an LLM: the expected reward of a fresh answer to the prompt \(x\), for example the fraction of answers to this math problem that are correct

    The advantage fixes two problems at once. It centers the weights, which cuts the variance. And it makes the signal relative: an easy prompt where 90% of answers are right and a hard one where 10% are right both give useful pushes, toward what is better than usual for that prompt. Without it, the easy prompts would dominate the gradient.

    The policy gradient with an advantage, \(\E[A \,\nabla \log \pi]\), is the form used by almost every modern method. They differ in how they estimate \(V\):

    • A learned critic. A second network predicts \(V(s_t)\) for every token. This is the actor–critic idea and PPO’s choice for RLHF; it needs memory for a second large model. Next session: session 65.
    • A group of samples. Sample \(N\) answers for the same prompt and use the other answers’ rewards to estimate \(V(x)\). No critic at all. RLOO and GRPO do this.
    • One extra greedy answer. ReMax uses the reward of the greedy (most likely) answer as the baseline. [Likely] Self-critical sequence training did the same for image captioning in 2017, with the reward of the model’s own test-time output. [Certain] ReMax reports about 46% less GPU memory than PPO for a 7B model, because there is no value network. [Certain]

    Group baselines: mean, leave-one-out and GRPO

    For one prompt with \(N\) sampled answers and rewards \(r_1, \dots, r_N\), the three common group advantages are:

    \[ \begin{aligned} A^{\text{mean}}_i &= r_i - \bar{r}, \qquad \bar{r} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} r_j \\ A^{\text{RLOO}}_i &= r_i - \frac{1}{N-1} \sum_{j \ne i} r_j = \frac{N}{N-1}\,\big(r_i - \bar{r}\big) \\ A^{\text{GRPO}}_i &= \frac{r_i - \bar{r}}{\operatorname{std}(r_1, \dots, r_N) + \varepsilon} \end{aligned} \tag{64.13} \]

    RLOO (REINFORCE leave-one-out; Kool et al., 2019; used for RLHF by Ahmadian et al., 2024) is the clean one: the baseline for answer \(i\) uses only the other answers, so it does not depend on \(y^{(i)}\), and (64.10) holds exactly. The plain mean includes \(r_i\) itself, which looks like a bias, but the second line of (64.13) shows it is just RLOO times \((N-1)/N\): the same direction, slightly smaller. GRPO (from DeepSeekMath) also divides by the group’s standard deviation. [Certain] That makes every prompt’s advantages the same size, so prompts with nearly all-same rewards are pushed as hard as mixed ones; it is no longer exactly the policy gradient of (64.1), and Dr. GRPO argues this scaling (and the per-length averaging) adds a bias. [Likely] GRPO is covered in depth in session 70.

    Ahmadian et al. report that such simple REINFORCE-style methods outperform PPO, DPO and RAFT in their RLHF experiments. [Certain] That is one result from one setup, not a general law, but it shows that a good baseline does much of the work of a critic when the reward comes once per answer.

    Worked example: a group of 8 answers

    A math prompt, 8 samples, 2 correct: \(r = (1, 0, 0, 1, 0, 0, 0, 0)\). The mean is \(\bar{r} = 0.25\).

    • Mean baseline: correct answers get \(+0.75\), wrong ones \(-0.25\). The advantages sum to 0.
    • RLOO: a correct answer is compared with the other 7, which have 1 correct: \(1 - 1/7 = 0.857\). A wrong answer is compared with 7 that have 2 correct: \(0 - 2/7 = -0.286\). Both are \(8/7\) times the mean-baseline values.
    • GRPO: the standard deviation (dividing by \(N\)) is \(\sqrt{0.25 \times 0.75} = 0.433\), so the advantages are \(+1.732\) and \(-0.577\).

    If all 8 answers are wrong (or all right), every advantage is 0. The prompt gives no learning signal, which is why RL training sets for reasoning are filtered to prompts that the model sometimes, but not always, solves. [Likely]

    Putting it together

    Here is REINFORCE as it is used for LLMs today: a group of samples per prompt, a leave-one-out baseline, and the surrogate loss with the advantage in place of the reward.

    Algorithm 2: REINFORCE with a group (leave-one-out) baseline
    1. input: policy \(\pi_\theta\), prompts, reward \(R\), group size \(N \ge 2\), optimizer
    2. repeat:
    3.   for each prompt \(x\) in the batch: sample \(y^{(1)}, \dots, y^{(N)} \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)\)
    4.     \(r_i \leftarrow R(x, y^{(i)})\)
    5.     \(A_i \leftarrow r_i - \frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j \ne i} r_j\) // formula (64.13); a constant, no gradient
    6.     \(\ell_i \leftarrow \sum_t \log \pi_\theta(y^{(i)}_t \mid x, y^{(i)}_{\lt t})\) // formula (64.5); mask out prompt and padding
    7.   \(\mathcal{L} \leftarrow -\operatorname{mean}_{x, i}\, A_i\, \ell_i\) // formula (64.7) with A in place of R
    8.   one optimizer step on \(\mathcal{L}\); then discard the samples

    Figure 7 runs this on a tiny “language model”. The answer has 4 tokens from a vocabulary of 5, and each position has its own softmax (it starts uniform). The reward is the fraction of the 4 tokens that match a hidden target, plus a constant shift \(c\). So the reward comes once per answer, and the expected reward starts at 0.2.

    import torch
torch.manual_seed(0)

# 1) The log-derivative trick on a 3-answer "policy" (formula 64.3)
z = torch.log(torch.tensor([0.2, 0.5, 0.3])).requires_grad_(True)  # logits
R = torch.tensor([1.0, 0.0, 0.0])                                  # reward of each answer
pi = torch.softmax(z, -1)
J = (pi * R).sum()                                  # exact expected reward
exact, = torch.autograd.grad(J, z)
a = torch.multinomial(pi.detach(), 100_000, replacement=True)    # sample answers
logp = torch.log_softmax(z, -1)[a]
est, = torch.autograd.grad((R[a] * logp).mean(), z)              # REINFORCE estimate
print(exact, est)   # tensor([0.1600, -0.1000, -0.0600]) tensor([0.1602, -0.1001, -0.0601])

# 2) REINFORCE with a leave-one-out baseline on a tiny autoregressive policy
V, T, N = 5, 4, 8                      # vocab, answer length, samples per prompt
target = torch.tensor([2, 0, 3, 1])
class TinyLM(torch.nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.emb = torch.nn.Embedding(V + 1, 16)      # token V = start token
        self.pos = torch.nn.Embedding(T, 16)
        self.out = torch.nn.Linear(16, V)
    def forward(self, prev, t):                       # next-token logits
        return self.out(torch.tanh(self.emb(prev) + self.pos(t)))

policy = TinyLM()
opt = torch.optim.Adam(policy.parameters(), lr=0.05)

def sample(n):
    prev = torch.full((n,), V); toks, logps = [], []
    for t in range(T):
        logits = policy(prev, torch.full((n,), t))
        dist = torch.distributions.Categorical(logits=logits)
        y = dist.sample()
        toks.append(y); logps.append(dist.log_prob(y)); prev = y
    return torch.stack(toks, 1), torch.stack(logps, 1)    # (n, T) each

for step in range(61):
    y, logp = sample(N)
    r = (y == target).float().mean(1)                     # one reward per answer
    baseline = (r.sum() - r) / (N - 1)                    # leave-one-out mean, formula (64.13)
    adv = (r - baseline).detach()                         # advantage: a constant
    loss = -(adv * logp.sum(1)).mean()                    # formula (64.7) with A in place of R
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    if step % 10 == 0:
        print(step, round(r.mean().item(), 3))
# 0 0.188 | 10 0.719 | 20 0.969 | 30 1.0 | ... the policy learns the target

    Note what is not in the code: no gradient of the reward, and no gradient through dist.sample(). The gradient flows only through dist.log_prob(y), exactly as in formula (64.3). In a real LLM, sampling is done by a fast inference engine (such as vLLM), and the log-probabilities are recomputed by the training model in one forward pass over prompt + answer.

    The pattern is always the same: a discrete, sampled choice (tokens, frames, timestamps, tool calls) and a score that we cannot differentiate. Whenever you see both, the log-derivative trick is the tool.

    Common mistakes

    • Letting gradient flow into the advantage. The reward, the baseline and the advantage must be constants (.detach()). If the baseline is computed from the policy’s outputs with gradient attached, the loss optimizes something else.
    • A baseline that depends on the answer itself. Using a quantity computed from \(y^{(i)}\) (for example, a value model that reads the full answer and is evaluated on the same answer) breaks formula (64.9) and biases the gradient. A per-token value \(V(s_t)\) is fine because it only reads the text before token \(t\).
    • Reusing old samples as if they were fresh. REINFORCE is on-policy. Several optimizer steps on one batch without an importance ratio drift away from the true gradient. That is the problem PPO’s ratio and clipping solve.
    • Including the prompt or padding in the log-probability. Only the generated tokens are actions. Mask the prompt and the padding, as in SFT; otherwise you “reward” the model for the prompt.
    • Forgetting the end-of-sequence token. It is an action too. If it is masked out, the model never learns when to stop, and answers grow without limit.
    • Changing the normalization without noticing. Averaging the loss per token instead of per answer gives long answers a smaller weight per token, which changes the objective and can bias the length of answers. [Likely] Pick one on purpose and keep it the same across runs.
    • Uncentered rewards. A reward model whose scores are all around +5, used without a baseline, gives the high-variance regime of Figure 4. Always center: a baseline, group advantages, or at least reward normalization.
    • Reading the surrogate loss as progress. The value of (64.7) can rise or fall while the policy improves. Track the mean reward, its spread, the KL to the starting model and the answer length.

    Interview questions

    Derive the policy gradient \(\nabla_\theta \E_{y \sim \pi_\theta}[R(y)]\).basic
    Write the expectation as \(\sum_y \pi_\theta(y) R(y)\). The reward does not depend on \(\theta\), so the gradient is \(\sum_y R(y) \nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(y)\). Use \(\nabla \pi = \pi \nabla \log \pi\) (the log-derivative trick) to get \(\sum_y \pi_\theta(y) R(y) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y) = \E_{y \sim \pi_\theta}[R(y)\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y)]\). It is an expectation under the policy, so we estimate it by sampling answers and averaging \(R \nabla \log \pi\).
    Why can’t we just backpropagate the reward, as we do with a loss?basic
    Two reasons. The answer is produced by sampling discrete tokens, and a random discrete draw has no useful derivative with respect to the logits. And the reward is often a black box: a checker, unit tests, a human rating, or a separate model whose input is discrete text. The policy gradient puts \(\theta\) only in \(\log \pi_\theta(y)\), which is differentiable, and treats the reward as a constant weight.
    How does REINFORCE for an LLM relate to SFT?core
    The surrogate loss is \(-\frac{1}{N}\sum_i R_i \log \pi_\theta(y^{(i)} \mid x)\). SFT is \(-\log \pi_\theta(y^{\ast} \mid x)\) on a human answer. So REINFORCE is SFT on the model’s own samples, each weighted by its reward (or advantage). With 0/1 rewards and no baseline, it is SFT on the samples that passed, which is close to rejection-sampling fine-tuning. The difference: the data comes from the current policy and changes every step, and with a baseline, bad samples get negative weight.
    Prove that subtracting a baseline does not bias the gradient. What must the baseline not depend on?core
    \(\E_{y}[\nabla \log \pi_\theta(y)] = \sum_y \nabla \pi_\theta(y) = \nabla \sum_y \pi_\theta(y) = \nabla 1 = 0\). So for a constant \(b\), \(\E[(R - b)\nabla \log \pi] = \E[R \nabla \log \pi] - b \cdot 0\). The same holds if \(b\) depends on the prompt or state, or on other independent samples, but not if it depends on the action \(y\) whose score it multiplies. At token level, \(b\) may depend on \(s_t = (x, y_{\lt t})\) but not on \(y_t\) or later tokens.
    Why is REINFORCE high-variance, and name three ways to reduce the variance.core
    Each estimate uses a few random answers, one reward multiplies the sum of scores of hundreds or thousands of tokens, and any common offset in the rewards adds noise without signal (in the three-answer example, a shift of 10 makes the variance about 421 times larger). Fixes: a baseline (mean reward, leave-one-out, greedy answer, or a learned value), more samples per prompt and bigger batches, reward-to-go when rewards arrive during the answer, reward normalization, and lower-variance advantage estimators such as GAE with a critic.
    What is the advantage and why is it a better weight than the raw reward?core
    \(A(s, a) = Q(s, a) - V(s)\): how much better this action was than what the policy does on average from that state. For one LLM answer, \(A = R(x, y) - V(x)\). It is centered (mean zero under the policy), so variance is much lower, and it is relative to the prompt: a correct answer to a hard prompt gets a big push and a correct answer to an easy prompt a small one, so easy prompts do not dominate. Its expected gradient is the same as with the raw reward.
    Explain RLOO. Why use the leave-one-out mean rather than the plain group mean?core
    Sample \(N\) answers per prompt and use, as answer \(i\)’s baseline, the mean reward of the other \(N - 1\) answers. That baseline is independent of answer \(i\), so the estimator is exactly unbiased, and no value network is needed. The plain mean includes \(r_i\), but \(r_i - \bar r = \frac{N-1}{N}(r_i - \bar r_{-i})\), so it only scales the gradient by \((N-1)/N\); in practice both work, RLOO is the clean form.
    GRPO divides the centered rewards by the group standard deviation. What does this change?advanced
    It gives every prompt advantages of similar size regardless of how mixed its rewards are. For 0/1 rewards with fraction \(p\) correct, the std is \(\sqrt{p(1-p)}\), so prompts with \(p\) near 0 or 1 get their small differences amplified. This acts like per-prompt adaptive weighting; it is no longer the plain gradient of the expected reward, and Dr. GRPO argues the std division and per-length averaging introduce biases (for example toward question difficulty and answer length). When all rewards in the group are equal, the advantage is 0 (the \(\varepsilon\) prevents division by zero).
    What is the optimal constant baseline, and why is the mean reward used instead?advanced
    Minimizing the trace of the covariance of \((R - b)\mathbf{s}\) over \(b\) gives \(b^{\ast} = \E[R\lVert\mathbf{s}\rVert^2] / \E[\lVert\mathbf{s}\rVert^2]\), a reward average weighted by the squared score norm. Computing per-sample score norms for billions of parameters is expensive, and the gain over \(b = \E[R]\) is usually small (0.1032 vs 0.0948 in the session’s example, against 0.1568 with no baseline). The mean reward is cheap to estimate and is also the value \(V(x)\) that a critic would learn.
    For an LLM with only an end-of-answer reward, what does reward-to-go change? When does it matter?advanced
    With a single terminal reward and \(\gamma = 1\), the reward-to-go at every token equals \(R\), so nothing changes. It matters when rewards arrive at many tokens: a per-token KL penalty \(-\beta \log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}})\) as in PPO-based RLHF, process rewards for each reasoning step, or multi-turn agents with rewards per turn. Then each token should be weighted only by rewards that come after it, which drops noise from earlier rewards it could not influence.
    Your RL run’s reward stays flat. Many prompts give all-wrong answers. What is going on, and what do you do?core
    With group advantages, a prompt whose \(N\) samples all fail (or all succeed) gives advantage 0 for every sample, so it contributes no gradient. If most prompts are like that, the effective batch is tiny. Fixes: filter or curriculum the prompts to ones the model solves sometimes, increase \(N\), start from a better SFT model, or add partial-credit rewards (format, intermediate checks) while making sure they cannot be gamed.

    Key takeaways

    • RL for LLMs maximizes the expected reward \(J(\theta)\) (64.1). The weights appear only in the probabilities of the answers, never in the reward.
    • The log-derivative trick (64.2) turns the gradient into an expectation, \(\E[R\,\nabla \log \pi]\) (64.3), which we estimate by sampling. No gradient of the reward or through sampling is needed.
    • For a whole answer, \(\log \pi\) is the sum of token log-probabilities (64.5). REINFORCE is reward-weighted SFT on the model’s own samples (64.7). It is on-policy: fresh samples every step.
    • The estimate is unbiased but very noisy. A constant shift in the rewards keeps the mean and can multiply the variance by hundreds.
    • Because \(\E[\nabla \log \pi] = 0\) (64.9), any baseline that does not depend on the answer adds no bias (64.10). The mean reward is nearly optimal (64.11).
    • The advantage \(A = R - V(x)\) (64.12) is the reward relative to what was expected. LLM methods estimate \(V\) with a critic (PPO), with the other samples in the group (RLOO, GRPO, 64.13), or with a greedy answer (ReMax).

    Key formulas

    \[ \nabla_\theta J = \E_{y \sim \pi_\theta}\big[ (R(x, y) - b(x))\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x) \big], \qquad \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x) = \sum_t \log \pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{\lt t}) \]
    \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{PG}} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_i A_i \log \pi_\theta(y^{(i)} \mid x), \qquad A^{\text{RLOO}}_i = r_i - \frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j \ne i} r_j \]
