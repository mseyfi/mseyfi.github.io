    68Track 4: Post-training and RL · session 16 of 25

    DPO: alignment without a separate reward model

    Classic RLHF trains a reward model and then runs PPO against it, with four models in memory and a sampling loop. DPO (Direct Preference Optimization) shows that the best policy of the KL-regularized RLHF objective has a closed form, so the reward can be written as a log-ratio of two language models. Put that into the Bradley–Terry preference model and RLHF becomes one classification-style loss on preference pairs.

    Time: 3 to 4 hours (reading 2 to 2.5 hours, exercises 1 hour)Level: Intermediate to interview levelBefore this: Sessions 3, 54, 66 and 67

    What you will learn

    • The KL-regularized RL objective of RLHF, and why its best policy is the reference policy tilted by exp(r / β)
    • How to turn the closed-form policy around: any reward is β times a log-ratio of policies plus a term that depends only on the prompt
    • Why that prompt-only term (the partition function) cancels in the Bradley–Terry model, and how this gives the DPO loss
    • The DPO gradient: which answers it pushes up and down, and why the weight σ(r̂_l − r̂_w) matters
    • What β and the reference model do, and how to read the training logs (implicit rewards, margins, accuracy, log-probabilities)
    • What DPO cannot do: it learns only from a fixed (offline) set of pairs, so it cannot discover good answers that are not in the data
    • A full DPO training step in pseudo-code and in runnable PyTorch, with memory numbers compared to PPO-RLHF

    Why this matters

    In session 67 we trained a reward model on pairs of answers (“A is better than B”), and in session 66 we used PPO to push a language model toward high reward while a KL penalty kept it close to a frozen reference. That pipeline works, but it is heavy: two training stages, four models in GPU memory, and a slow loop that must generate new answers at every step. DPO (Direct Preference Optimization, Rafailov et al., 2023) gets the same objective with one loss on the preference pairs, no reward model, no sampling and no critic.

    The trick is a piece of algebra. The RLHF objective has a best policy that we can write in closed form. Turn that formula around, and the reward becomes a log-ratio between the policy and the reference model. Put this reward into the Bradley–Terry model of preferences, and an unknown normalizing term cancels. What is left is a logistic loss that we can minimize with plain gradient descent, like supervised fine-tuning. The paper’s subtitle says it: “Your language model is secretly a reward model”. [Certain]

    • Simple. A DPO training step is two forward passes (chosen and rejected answer) through the policy, plus log-probabilities from a frozen reference model. No generation during training.
    • Widely used. Zephyr-7B was trained with DPO on AI-ranked answers (UltraFeedback, \(\beta = 0.1\)). [Certain] Every round of Llama 3 post-training was SFT followed by DPO; Meta reports that DPO “required less compute for large-scale models and performed better” than PPO in their tests, especially on instruction following. [Certain] Tülu 3 uses a length-normalized DPO in its pipeline. [Likely]
    • Limits. DPO learns only from a fixed set of pairs (offline data). It cannot explore, and careful comparisons find that well-tuned PPO can still beat it, for example on code generation. [Certain] Knowing exactly why is a common interview topic.
    Interview tipThe classic questions: “Derive the DPO loss from the RLHF objective”, “Why does the partition function cancel?”, “What does β do?”, “Why do both the chosen and the rejected log-probabilities go down during DPO training?”, “DPO or PPO: when would you pick each?”. All of them are answered below.

    The starting point: the KL-regularized objective

    Let us recall the setting from session 67 and session 66. A prompt \(x\) comes from a dataset. The policy \(\pi_\theta(y \mid x)\) is our language model; it gives a probability to every full answer \(y\). The reference policy \(\pi_\text{ref}\) is a frozen copy of the model before this stage, usually the SFT model from session 54. A reward function \(r(x, y)\) says how good an answer is. RLHF maximizes

    \[ \max_{\pi}\ \E_{x \sim \mathcal{D}}\Big[\ \E_{y \sim \pi(\cdot \mid x)}\big[r(x, y)\big] \;-\; \beta\, \KL\big(\pi(\cdot \mid x)\,\big\|\,\pi_\text{ref}(\cdot \mid x)\big) \Big] \tag{68.1} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(r(x, y)\)
    the reward of answer \(y\) to prompt \(x\); in classic RLHF this is the learned reward model
    \(\KL(\pi \,\|\, \pi_\text{ref})\)
    \(\sum_y \pi(y \mid x) \log \frac{\pi(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}\), how far the policy has moved from the reference (see session 3)
    \(\beta\)
    a positive number, the price of moving away from the reference; large \(\beta\) means a short leash

    The KL term matters for two reasons. The reward model was trained on answers that look like the SFT model’s answers, so it is only accurate near \(\pi_\text{ref}\); far away, the policy finds answers that fool it (reward hacking). And without the KL term, the best policy would put all its probability on one answer, losing all diversity. [Certain]

    In PPO-RLHF we never solve (68.1) exactly. We sample answers, score them, and take small policy-gradient steps. DPO starts from a different question: what is the exact best policy for (68.1), if we could search over all possible distributions?

    The best policy has a closed form

    Fix one prompt \(x\) and drop it from the notation. Write the objective as one expectation over \(y \sim \pi\), and use \(\log a - \log b = \log(a/b)\):

    \[ \E_{\pi}\big[r(y)\big] - \beta\,\E_{\pi}\Big[\log \frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y)}\Big] = -\beta\, \E_{\pi}\Big[\log \frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y)\, e^{r(y)/\beta}}\Big] = -\beta\, \KL\big(\pi \,\|\, \pi^*\big) + \beta \log Z \tag{68.2} \]

    In the last step we divided \(\pi_\text{ref}(y)\, e^{r(y)/\beta}\) by its total \(Z\), so that it becomes a probability distribution, and called it \(\pi^*\):

    \[ \pi^*(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\, \pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)\, \exp\!\Big(\frac{r(x, y)}{\beta}\Big), \qquad Z(x) = \sum_{y} \pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)\, \exp\!\Big(\frac{r(x, y)}{\beta}\Big) \tag{68.3} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\pi^*\)
    the best possible policy for objective (68.1) with this reward and this \(\beta\)
    \(Z(x)\)
    the partition function: the number that makes the probabilities sum to 1. It depends on the prompt only, but it is a sum over every possible answer, so for a language model we can never compute it
    \(e^{r/\beta}\)
    a multiplier for each answer: answers with high reward get their reference probability multiplied by a large number

    Now read (68.2). The term \(\beta \log Z\) does not depend on \(\pi\). A KL divergence is never negative and is zero only when the two distributions are equal. So the objective is largest exactly when \(\pi = \pi^*\), and its best value is \(\beta \log Z\). We have solved RLHF in one line, at least on paper. [Certain]

    AnalogyThink of \(\pi_\text{ref}\) as the habits of a cook, and the reward as feedback from guests. The best new habit does not throw the old one away. It keeps every old dish, but cooks each one more or less often by a factor \(e^{r/\beta}\). A strict head chef (large \(\beta\)) allows only small changes; a relaxed one (small \(\beta\)) lets the guests’ favorite dish take over the menu.

    So the best policy is a re-weighting of the reference: it never gives probability to an answer that \(\pi_\text{ref}\) gives zero probability, and \(\beta\) sets how strongly the reward re-weights. As \(\beta \to \infty\), \(\pi^* \to \pi_\text{ref}\). As \(\beta \to 0\), \(\pi^*\) puts all its probability on the answers with the highest reward.

    Worked example: three answers, \(\beta = 1\)

    One prompt with three answers. Reference probabilities \(\pi_\text{ref} = (0.5, 0.3, 0.2)\), rewards \(r = (0, 1, 2)\).

    Multipliers \(e^{r/\beta} = (1, 2.718, 7.389)\). Unnormalized weights: \(0.5 \times 1 = 0.5\), \(0.3 \times 2.718 = 0.815\), \(0.2 \times 7.389 = 1.478\). Their sum is \(Z = 2.793\).

    So \(\pi^* = (0.179, 0.292, 0.529)\). The best answer went from 20% to 53%, but the worst one still keeps 18%. The expected reward is \(1.350\), the KL to the reference is \(0.323\) nats, and the objective is \(1.350 - 0.323 = 1.027 = \log Z\), as (68.2) promised.

    With \(\beta = 0.5\) the leash is looser: \(\pi^* = (0.037, 0.163, 0.801)\), expected reward \(1.764\), KL \(0.916\) nats. With \(\beta = 2\): \(\pi^* = (0.325, 0.322, 0.353)\), KL only \(0.084\).

    Turning it around: the reward is a log-ratio

    Formula (68.3) maps a reward to a policy. Take the logarithm of both sides and solve for the reward:

    \[ r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x) \tag{68.4} \]

    This is the key idea of DPO. If we know the best policy and the reference, we know the reward, up to one number per prompt, \(\beta \log Z(x)\). Check it on the worked example with \(\beta = 1\): \(\log(0.179/0.5) + \log 2.793 = -1.027 + 1.027 = 0\) and \(\log(0.529/0.2) + 1.027 = 0.973 + 1.027 = 2.000\). These are the rewards we started from.

    That unknown \(\beta \log Z(x)\) looks like a problem: we cannot compute it. But we do not need to. The next step only ever uses differences of rewards for the same prompt, and in a difference it cancels.

    Bradley–Terry, and the DPO loss

    From session 67: the Bradley–Terry model says that when a person compares two answers \(y_1\) and \(y_2\) to the same prompt, the probability that they prefer \(y_1\) is a sigmoid of the reward difference:

    \[ p(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma\big(r(x, y_1) - r(x, y_2)\big), \qquad \sigma(u) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-u}} \tag{68.5} \]

    A reward model is trained by maximizing the log of this probability on human-labeled pairs \((x, y_w, y_l)\), where \(y_w\) is the preferred (“winner”, chosen) answer and \(y_l\) the other one (“loser”, rejected). Now put (68.4) into (68.5). The two \(\beta \log Z(x)\) terms are equal (same prompt) and cancel:

    \[ p(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma\!\Big( \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w \mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l \mid x)} \Big) \tag{68.6} \]

    The human preference probability is now written with policies only. So instead of fitting a reward model and then finding its best policy, we fit the policy itself by maximum likelihood on the pairs. Replace \(\pi^*\) by our model \(\pi_\theta\) and take the negative log-likelihood. This is the DPO loss:

    \[ \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\theta) = -\,\E_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \log \sigma\!\Big( \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w \mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l \mid x)} \Big) \right] \tag{68.7} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\mathcal{D}\)
    a fixed dataset of preference triples: a prompt, a chosen answer, a rejected answer
    \(\pi_\theta(y \mid x)\)
    the probability our model gives to the whole answer; in practice we use its log, a sum over tokens (formula 68.10)
    \(\pi_\text{ref}\)
    the frozen reference, usually the SFT model; also the starting point of \(\pi_\theta\)
    \(\beta\)
    the same KL price as in (68.1); typical values are 0.1 to 0.5 [Likely]
    \(\log \sigma(\cdot)\)
    the log-likelihood of the observed preference; the loss is a logistic regression loss

    Each term inside the sigmoid has a name. The implicit reward of an answer is

    \[ \hat r_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)} \tag{68.8} \]

    so the DPO loss is just the reward-model loss of session 67, \(-\log \sigma(\hat r_\theta(x, y_w) - \hat r_\theta(x, y_l))\), with a special choice of reward model: one whose reward is \(\beta\) times a log-ratio of two language models. If this reward model fits the data perfectly, its best policy, by (68.3), is \(\pi_\theta\) itself. We train a reward model and get its optimal policy for free. [Certain]

    Watch outThe reward is only defined up to a function of the prompt: adding any \(f(x)\) to \(r\) changes neither the Bradley–Terry probabilities nor the best policy. So implicit rewards of answers to different prompts cannot be compared. Only the difference within one pair means something.
    Algorithm 1: the DPO derivation in five steps
    1. start: \(\max_\pi \E_\pi[r] - \beta\,\KL(\pi \,\|\, \pi_\text{ref})\) // RLHF objective (68.1)
    2. rewrite it as \(-\beta\,\KL(\pi \,\|\, \pi^*) + \beta \log Z\) // (68.2)
    3. so the best policy is \(\pi^* \propto \pi_\text{ref}\, e^{r/\beta}\) // (68.3)
    4. invert: \(r = \beta \log(\pi^*/\pi_\text{ref}) + \beta \log Z(x)\) // (68.4)
    5. plug into Bradley–Terry; \(\beta \log Z(x)\) cancels; replace \(\pi^*\) by \(\pi_\theta\); minimize the negative log-likelihood // (68.6), (68.7)

    What the gradient does

    Write \(m = \hat r_\theta(x, y_w) - \hat r_\theta(x, y_l)\) for the margin of one pair. The loss of the pair is \(-\log \sigma(m)\), and its derivative with respect to \(m\) is \(-\sigma(-m)\). By the chain rule,

    \[ \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO} = -\beta\, \E_{\mathcal{D}} \Big[ \underbrace{\sigma\big(\hat r_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat r_\theta(x, y_w)\big)}_{\text{weight}} \Big( \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_w \mid x) - \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_l \mid x) \Big) \Big] \tag{68.9} \]

    Read it in three parts. Gradient descent moves against this gradient, so it raises \(\log \pi_\theta(y_w)\) and lowers \(\log \pi_\theta(y_l)\). The size of the push is the weight \(\sigma(\hat r_l - \hat r_w)\): close to 1 when the implicit reward orders the pair wrongly, close to 0 when it already orders it right by a wide margin. Pairs that are already “solved” stop contributing. And the factor \(\beta\) scales everything.

    The weight is important. The DPO paper reports that a naive version, which only raises \(y_w\) and lowers \(y_l\) without this weight (an “unlikelihood”-style loss), makes the language model degenerate. [Certain] Without the weight, the model keeps pushing \(\log \pi_\theta(y_l)\) toward minus infinity, and since the two answers share many tokens, it damages the chosen answers too.

    Note also what the gradient does not say. It controls only the difference of the two log-ratios. Nothing stops both log-probabilities from going down, as long as the rejected one goes down faster. This happens in practice and is a known failure mode, especially when the chosen and rejected answers differ in only a few tokens (Pal et al., 2024). [Certain] Figure 3 lets you see it.

    Worked example: one pair, \(\beta = 0.1\)

    The reference gives the chosen answer \(\log \pi_\text{ref}(y_w) = -40.0\) and the rejected answer \(\log \pi_\text{ref}(y_l) = -38.0\) (sums of token log-probabilities). At the start, \(\pi_\theta = \pi_\text{ref}\): both implicit rewards are 0, the margin is 0, the loss is \(\log 2 = 0.693\), and the weight is \(\sigma(0) = 0.5\).

    After some training, \(\log \pi_\theta(y_w) = -39.0\) and \(\log \pi_\theta(y_l) = -39.5\). Implicit rewards: \(\hat r_w = 0.1 \times (-39.0 + 40.0) = 0.10\), \(\hat r_l = 0.1 \times (-39.5 + 38.0) = -0.15\). Margin \(0.25\), loss \(-\log \sigma(0.25) = 0.576\), the model’s preference probability \(\sigma(0.25) = 0.562\), and the weight drops to \(0.438\).

    A different outcome: \(\log \pi_\theta(y_w) = -42\) and \(\log \pi_\theta(y_l) = -46\). Now \(\hat r_w = -0.2\), \(\hat r_l = -0.8\), margin \(0.6\), loss \(0.438\): lower than before. Yet the chosen answer became \(e^{2} \approx 7.4\) times less likely. The loss is happy; you may not be.

    From pairs to tokens: computing the loss on a real model

    A language model gives probabilities to tokens, not to whole answers. The log-probability of an answer \(y = (y_1, \dots, y_T)\) is the sum over its tokens:

    \[ \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log \pi_\theta\big(y_t \mid x, y_{\lt t}\big) \tag{68.10} \]

    So every implicit reward costs one forward pass of the policy and one of the reference over prompt plus answer. Only the answer tokens are summed; the prompt tokens are masked out, exactly like the loss mask in session 54. Since the reference is frozen, its log-probabilities for the whole dataset can be computed once before training and stored, so only the policy needs to be in GPU memory during training.

    Algorithm 2: one DPO training step
    1. input: a batch of triples \((x, y_w, y_l)\); policy \(\pi_\theta\); frozen \(\pi_\text{ref}\); \(\beta\)
    2. build two sequences per triple: \([x; y_w]\) and \([x; y_l]\), with a mask that is 1 on answer tokens only
    3. \(\ell_w, \ell_l \leftarrow\) masked sums of token log-probs under \(\pi_\theta\) // (68.10), with gradient
    4. \(\ell^\text{ref}_w, \ell^\text{ref}_l \leftarrow\) the same under \(\pi_\text{ref}\) // no gradient; can be precomputed
    5. \(\hat r_w \leftarrow \beta(\ell_w - \ell^\text{ref}_w)\), \(\;\hat r_l \leftarrow \beta(\ell_l - \ell^\text{ref}_l)\) // implicit rewards (68.8)
    6. \(\mathcal{L} \leftarrow \text{mean}\big(-\log\sigma(\hat r_w - \hat r_l)\big)\) // (68.7)
    7. backward, clip, AdamW step // as in Session 4
    8. log: mean \(\hat r_w\), mean \(\hat r_l\), margin, accuracy \(= \text{mean}[\hat r_w \gt \hat r_l]\), and \(\ell_w, \ell_l\)

    The PyTorch version below trains a tiny causal model on 8 pairs, then checks the theory: on exact, noise-free preference data, the DPO minimum is the closed-form \(\pi^*\) of (68.3).

    import copy, torch
import torch.nn.functional as F
torch.manual_seed(0)

def seq_logp(model, ids, resp_mask):
    """Sum of log pi(token) over the response tokens only. ids: (B, T). Formula (68.10)."""
    logits = model(ids[:, :-1])                       # predict token t+1 from tokens up to t
    logp = torch.log_softmax(logits.float(), dim=-1)
    tok = torch.gather(logp, 2, ids[:, 1:].unsqueeze(-1)).squeeze(-1)
    return (tok * resp_mask[:, 1:]).sum(-1)           # (B,)

def dpo_loss(pol_w, pol_l, ref_w, ref_l, beta=0.1):
    r_w = beta * (pol_w - ref_w)                      # implicit reward of the chosen answer (68.8)
    r_l = beta * (pol_l - ref_l)                      # implicit reward of the rejected answer
    loss = -F.logsigmoid(r_w - r_l).mean()            # formula (68.7); logsigmoid is numerically stable
    stats = {"margin": (r_w - r_l).mean().item(),
             "acc": (r_w > r_l).float().mean().item()}
    return loss, stats

# a tiny causal "language model": embedding -> linear (a bigram model)
V, T = 16, 8
class Tiny(torch.nn.Module):
    def __init__(s):
        super().__init__(); s.emb = torch.nn.Embedding(V, 32); s.out = torch.nn.Linear(32, V)
    def forward(s, x): return s.out(s.emb(x))
policy = Tiny()
ref = copy.deepcopy(policy).eval()                    # the reference starts equal to the policy
for p in ref.parameters(): p.requires_grad_(False)

# 8 preference pairs: same 4-token prompt, two different 4-token answers
B = 8
prompt = torch.randint(0, V, (B, 4))
chosen = torch.cat([prompt, torch.randint(0, V // 2, (B, 4))], 1)      # chosen answers use tokens 0..7
rejected = torch.cat([prompt, torch.randint(V // 2, V, (B, 4))], 1)   # rejected answers use tokens 8..15
mask = torch.cat([torch.zeros(B, 4), torch.ones(B, 4)], 1)             # 1 on response tokens

with torch.no_grad():                                 # reference log-probs: computed once
    ref_w, ref_l = seq_logp(ref, chosen, mask), seq_logp(ref, rejected, mask)

opt = torch.optim.AdamW(policy.parameters(), lr=1e-2, weight_decay=0.0)
for step in range(101):
    pol_w, pol_l = seq_logp(policy, chosen, mask), seq_logp(policy, rejected, mask)
    loss, st = dpo_loss(pol_w, pol_l, ref_w, ref_l, beta=0.1)
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    if step % 50 == 0:
        print(f"step {step:3d}  loss {loss.item():.4f}  margin {st['margin']:.3f}  acc {st['acc']:.2f}  "
              f"d_logp chosen {(pol_w - ref_w).mean().item():+.2f}  rejected {(pol_l - ref_l).mean().item():+.2f}")

# theory check: on exact preference data, DPO's optimum is the closed-form pi* (formula 68.3)
ref_p = torch.tensor([0.30, 0.30, 0.25, 0.10, 0.05]); r = torch.tensor([0.0, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, -1.0]); beta = 0.5
pi_star = ref_p * torch.exp(r / beta); pi_star /= pi_star.sum()
z = ref_p.log().clone().requires_grad_(True)          # a "policy" that is just 5 logits
opt = torch.optim.SGD([z], lr=8.0)
i, j = torch.meshgrid(torch.arange(5), torch.arange(5), indexing="ij")
wpair = ref_p[i] * ref_p[j] * torch.sigmoid(r[i] - r[j]) * (i != j)   # P(pair) * P(i preferred to j)
wpair /= wpair.sum()
for _ in range(300):
    h = beta * (torch.log_softmax(z, 0) - ref_p.log())           # implicit rewards
    loss = -(wpair * F.logsigmoid(h[i] - h[j])).sum()
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
print("DPO  ", torch.softmax(z, 0).detach().numpy().round(3))
print("pi*  ", pi_star.numpy().round(3))

    Three lessons hide in this output. First, the loss starts at \(\log 2\) because policy and reference are equal; if your first DPO loss is not 0.693, your reference is wrong. Second, accuracy at step 0 is 0, not 0.5: with \(\hat r_w = \hat r_l\) exactly, the strict test \(\hat r_w \gt \hat r_l\) fails. Third, almost all of the margin comes from pushing the rejected answers down (−58 nats) rather than the chosen ones up (+5): making a sequence very unlikely is easy, and the loss does not care which side moves. Between steps 50 and 100 the chosen log-probability even falls a little (from +5.21 to +5.10).

    Does DPO find the RLHF solution? Coverage and offline data

    The derivation is exact. If the preference data were infinite, labeled by a Bradley–Terry model with a true reward \(r^*\), and covered every pair of answers, then the DPO minimum would be exactly the \(\pi^*\) of (68.3), the same policy that a perfect RLHF run would find. The code above shows this numerically. [Certain]

    Real data break the assumptions in two ways.

    • Finite data. With a few labels per pair, the empirical preference is often 100% one way. Then the loss keeps falling as the margin grows, and the implicit reward difference goes to infinity, whatever \(\beta\) says. The KL leash of (68.1) is not really enforced. This is the overfitting problem that IPO fixes (session 69). [Certain]
    • Coverage. DPO only sees answers that are in the dataset. The pairs were usually generated by some other model (often not your \(\pi_\text{ref}\)), and they are never refreshed. An excellent answer that nobody wrote down gets no direct signal; its probability changes only as a side effect of the others. PPO, in contrast, samples from the current policy, so it can find and reward new good answers. That is the meaning of offline versus online: the data are fixed before training versus produced during training (session 71).

    Figure 4 trains DPO on a toy problem where we know everything: one prompt, five answers A to E, a reference policy, and true rewards. Answer D is the best but rare under the reference.

    DPO with all pairs reaches \((0.036, 0.097, 0.219, 0.648, 0.001)\), the same to three digits. The KL from DPO to \(\pi^*\) is 0.85 nats.

    β and the reference model

    What β does. In theory, \(\beta\) is the KL price in (68.1): small \(\beta\) allows the policy to move far from the reference. In the loss, \(\beta\) multiplies the log-ratios. With a small \(\beta\), the model must change its log-probabilities a lot before the margin is large and the loss saturates; with a large \(\beta\), small changes already saturate the loss, so training stops early and the policy stays close to \(\pi_\text{ref}\). The original paper used \(\beta = 0.1\) for most experiments and \(0.5\) for TL;DR summarization, with RMSprop, learning rate \(10^{-6}\), batch size 64 and 150 warmup steps. [Certain] Zephyr used \(\beta = 0.1\) with a peak learning rate of \(5 \times 10^{-7}\); Llama 3 used \(\beta = 0.1\). [Certain] DPO learning rates are 10 to 100 times smaller than SFT learning rates. [Likely]

    What the reference model does. It appears in three places: it is the anchor of the KL in (68.1), it is the starting point of \(\pi_\theta\), and it is the baseline in every implicit reward. Its quality matters because the theory assumes the preference answers look like samples from \(\pi_\text{ref}\). If you take a public preference dataset written by other models, first run SFT on the chosen answers, so that the reference is not far from the data. The DPO paper does exactly this when no SFT model is available. [Certain]

    Can we drop it? If we remove \(\pi_\text{ref}\) from (68.7), the loss becomes \(-\log\sigma(\beta[\log\pi_\theta(y_w) - \log\pi_\theta(y_l)])\). Nothing then anchors the model: it can drift arbitrarily, and the unnormalized log-probability prefers short answers. Methods such as ORPO and SimPO remove the reference on purpose and add other fixes (an SFT term, length normalization, a margin). That is the topic of session 69.

    NumbersFor a 7B model with AdamW in mixed precision: the trained policy needs \(7 \times 10^9 \times 16\) bytes = 112 GB, each frozen BF16 model 14 GB. PPO-RLHF (policy + critic trained, reference + reward model frozen): 252 GB. DPO (policy + reference): 126 GB, or 112 GB if reference log-probs are precomputed. All before activations.

    A common middle ground is iterative (or online) DPO: train with DPO, sample new answers from the new policy, label them with a reward model or a judge LLM, and run DPO again on the fresh pairs. This brings back part of the exploration of online RL while keeping the simple loss (Xiong et al., 2023). [Certain] Llama 3’s several rounds of SFT and DPO, each on newly collected preference data, follow this pattern. [Likely]

    Length. Human and AI judges often prefer longer answers, and the chosen answer in many datasets is longer on average. DPO then learns to write longer answers, even when quality does not improve (Park et al., 2024). [Likely] Length-normalized variants and length-controlled evaluation are the usual fixes.

    Common mistakes

    • Summing the prompt tokens into the log-probability. The prompt is the same for both answers and the reference, so it only adds noise and padding bugs. Mask everything except the answer, including padding.
    • Averaging token log-probs when you meant to sum. Plain DPO uses the sum (68.10). Averaging changes the method (it is a length-normalized variant) and the meaning of \(\beta\).
    • A reference that is not the starting policy. At step 0 the loss must be exactly 0.693 and every implicit reward 0. If not, the reference or the masking is wrong (for example, a different chat template or dropout left on).
    • Different templates or tokenization for policy and reference. The log-ratio then measures the template, not the answer. Use the same tokenizer and template (session 9). Llama 3 also masks special formatting tokens out of the DPO loss for stability. [Certain]
    • Watching only the loss and accuracy. Both can look great while the chosen log-probability falls and the model gets worse. Log the chosen and rejected log-probs and the KL to the reference; consider adding a small SFT (negative log-likelihood) term on the chosen answers, as Llama 3 did with coefficient 0.2. [Certain]
    • Too many epochs or too high a learning rate. DPO overfits fast on one-sided pairs: margins keep growing and generation quality drops. One to three epochs with a learning rate around \(10^{-7}\) to \(10^{-6}\) are common. [Likely]
    • Using pairs where both answers are bad, or both good. The loss still pushes them apart. Filter pairs with a small quality gap, or label noise dominates.
    • Comparing implicit rewards across prompts. They are defined only up to a per-prompt constant \(\beta\log Z(x)\).

    Interview questions

    Derive the DPO loss starting from the RLHF objective.core
    Start from \(\max_\pi \E_{y\sim\pi}[r] - \beta\KL(\pi\|\pi_\text{ref})\). Combine the terms: \(-\beta\E_\pi[\log(\pi / (\pi_\text{ref}e^{r/\beta}))] = -\beta\KL(\pi\|\pi^*) + \beta\log Z\), with \(\pi^* = \pi_\text{ref}e^{r/\beta}/Z\). The KL is minimized at \(\pi = \pi^*\). Taking logs gives \(r = \beta\log(\pi^*/\pi_\text{ref}) + \beta\log Z(x)\). Insert into Bradley–Terry \(\sigma(r_w - r_l)\): the \(\beta\log Z(x)\) terms cancel. Replace \(\pi^*\) by \(\pi_\theta\) and minimize the negative log-likelihood of the observed preferences: \(-\log\sigma(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l)})\).
    Why is the partition function \(Z(x)\) not a problem?basic
    \(Z(x)\) is a sum over all possible answers, so it cannot be computed for a language model. But it depends only on the prompt. Bradley–Terry uses only the difference of rewards of two answers to the same prompt, so \(\beta\log Z(x)\) appears in both terms and cancels. This is also why implicit rewards can only be compared within one prompt.
    What does β control, and what happens at the extremes?basic
    \(\beta\) is the price of the KL to the reference. Large \(\beta\): the best policy stays close to \(\pi_\text{ref}\), and in the loss a small change in log-probabilities already gives a big margin, so training saturates early. Small \(\beta\): the policy may move far, toward putting all mass on the top answers, and the loss needs large log-probability changes before it saturates. Typical values are 0.1 to 0.5. In practice, with finite one-sided data the margin can still grow without bound, so \(\beta\) is a weaker leash than the theory suggests.
    Explain the DPO gradient. Why is the weight \(\sigma(\hat r_l - \hat r_w)\) important?core
    \(\nabla\mathcal{L} = -\beta\E[\sigma(\hat r_l - \hat r_w)(\nabla\log\pi(y_w) - \nabla\log\pi(y_l))]\). It raises the chosen answer and lowers the rejected one, weighted by how wrong the implicit reward currently is. Pairs already ordered correctly by a wide margin get almost no weight, which prevents the model from pushing log-probabilities to minus infinity. The DPO paper reports that the unweighted version (pure unlikelihood-style) makes the model degenerate.
    During DPO training, the log-probability of the chosen answers goes down. Is that a bug?advanced
    Not necessarily a code bug: the loss depends only on the difference of the two log-ratios, so both can fall as long as the rejected one falls faster. It is common when chosen and rejected answers share most tokens (small edit distance), because lowering shared tokens lowers both. It can hurt quality, since probability mass moves to answers that are in neither set. Fixes: an SFT/NLL term on chosen answers (Llama 3 used 0.2), DPO-Positive (Smaug), a lower learning rate, fewer epochs, or a larger \(\beta\). Log both log-probs to catch it.
    Why is DPO called “offline”, and what does that cost?core
    Its data are a fixed set of pairs collected before training, usually from other models, and never refreshed. So DPO cannot try new answers and learn from them; answers not in the data get no direct signal and their probability changes only through side effects. If the data come from a policy very different from \(\pi_\text{ref}\), the theory’s assumptions fail. PPO samples on-policy and can explore and correct its own mistakes, at higher cost. Iterative/online DPO (resample, relabel, retrain) is the usual compromise.
    Compare the compute and memory of DPO and PPO-RLHF for a 7B model.core
    PPO trains a policy and a critic (16 bytes per parameter each with AdamW: 112 GB + 112 GB) and keeps a frozen reference and reward model (14 GB each in BF16): 252 GB before activations, plus generation at every iteration. DPO trains only the policy (112 GB) plus a frozen reference (14 GB), 126 GB, or 112 GB with precomputed reference log-probs, and needs no generation: just forward and backward on chosen and rejected sequences.
    Why do we need a reference model at all? What if you remove it?core
    The reference is the anchor of the KL term, the baseline of each implicit reward, and the initial policy. Without it, the loss only compares \(\log\pi_\theta(y_w)\) and \(\log\pi_\theta(y_l)\); nothing keeps the model close to its starting point, and raw sequence log-probabilities favor short answers. Reference-free methods (ORPO, SimPO) add other terms: an SFT loss, length normalization, a target margin.
    In what sense is “your language model secretly a reward model”? How would you use it as one?advanced
    After DPO, \(\hat r(x, y) = \beta\log(\pi_\theta(y|x)/\pi_\text{ref}(y|x))\) is a reward model in the Bradley–Terry sense, and the paper shows every reward class can be represented this way. You can rank candidate answers to the same prompt with it (for best-of-N or to label new pairs) using two forward passes. It is defined only up to a per-prompt constant, so it cannot be compared across prompts, and it is reliable only near the training data distribution.
    Your DPO model writes longer and longer answers. Why, and what do you do?core
    In many preference datasets the chosen answer is longer on average, because judges reward detail. DPO learns whatever separates chosen from rejected, including length. Fixes: balance lengths in the data, use length-normalized or length-regularized variants, add a margin or SFT term, evaluate with length-controlled win rates, and keep \(\beta\) and epochs moderate.
    Under what conditions does DPO give exactly the RLHF solution?advanced
    If preferences follow Bradley–Terry with some true reward \(r^*\), the data cover all relevant pairs with enough labels to estimate the preference probabilities, the policy class can represent \(\pi^*\), and optimization finds the minimum, then the DPO minimum is \(\pi^* \propto \pi_\text{ref}e^{r^*/\beta}\), the same as the optimum of the KL-regularized objective. With finite, one-sided labels the margins diverge (the IPO critique); with missing coverage the result depends on what is in the data.
    You have only single thumbs-up or thumbs-down labels, not pairs. Can you use DPO?basic
    Not directly: DPO needs a chosen and a rejected answer for the same prompt. You can build pairs where the same prompt has one good and one bad answer, or use a method designed for unpaired labels such as KTO (next session).

    Key takeaways

    • RLHF maximizes reward minus \(\beta\) times the KL to a reference (68.1). Its exact best policy is the reference re-weighted by \(e^{r/\beta}\) and normalized by \(Z(x)\) (68.3).
    • Inverting it, any reward equals \(\beta\log(\pi^*/\pi_\text{ref})\) plus a prompt-only term (68.4). In Bradley–Terry that term cancels (68.6).
    • So the reward-model loss, written for this special reward, is the DPO loss (68.7): logistic regression on the difference of implicit rewards (68.8), computed from summed token log-probs (68.10).
    • The gradient (68.9) raises the chosen answer, lowers the rejected one, and weights each pair by how wrong it still is. It only controls the difference, so both log-probs can fall.
    • DPO needs one stage, two models and no generation, but it is offline: it cannot explore, it overfits one-sided pairs, and it can lose good answers that are not in the data. Iterative DPO and online RL address this.

    Key formulas

    \[ \pi^*(y|x) = \frac{\pi_\text{ref}(y|x)\,e^{r(x,y)/\beta}}{Z(x)}, \qquad r = \beta\log\frac{\pi^*}{\pi_\text{ref}} + \beta\log Z(x) \]
    \[ \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO} = -\E\Big[\log\sigma\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w|x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l|x)}\Big)\Big] \]
    \[ \nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO} = -\beta\,\E\big[\sigma(\hat r_l - \hat r_w)\,(\nabla\log\pi_\theta(y_w|x) - \nabla\log\pi_\theta(y_l|x))\big] \]
