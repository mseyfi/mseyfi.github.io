    LoRA vs QLoRA vs full fine-tuning

    In this lab you fine-tune the same small language model, Qwen2.5-0.5B, in three ways: full fine-tuning, LoRA and QLoRA. You train it for sentiment on IMDB reviews and for one-sentence summaries on XSum, predict and measure memory and speed, score summaries with ROUGE and a position-swapped LLM judge, and end with a short written decision about what you would ship.

    Time: 4 to 6 hours (reading 1.5 hours, GPU runs and analysis the rest)Level: Intermediate to interview level, hands-onBefore this: Sessions 54, 57, 58 and 61, Python, one CUDA GPU with 16 to 24 GB

    What you will learn

    • How full fine-tuning, LoRA and QLoRA differ for one linear layer: what is stored, what gets a gradient, and what is served
    • How to count LoRA parameters exactly (8,798,208 for Qwen2.5-0.5B at rank 16 on all linear layers) and predict training memory before a run
    • What NF4 and double quantization do to weights, and why the math still runs in BF16
    • How to fine-tune a decoder model as a document classifier (IMDB) and as a summarizer (XSum) with PEFT and TRL
    • How to evaluate summaries with ROUGE and with an LLM judge, and the weaknesses of each
    • How to tell a real difference from noise with standard errors and paired comparisons
    • How to write a ship decision from your own memory, speed and quality measurements

    Why this matters

    You have a small language model, one GPU and two tasks: decide if a movie review is positive or negative, and write a one-sentence summary of a news article. You can train all the weights (full fine-tuning), train a small low-rank add-on (LoRA), or do the same on top of a 4-bit copy of the model (QLoRA). In this lab you run all three on both tasks, measure memory, speed and quality yourself, and then write down which one you would ship.

    The earlier sessions gave the theory: session 57 explained the low-rank update, session 58 explained 4-bit NF4 weights, and session 61 explained why changing fewer weights can protect old skills. This lab turns that theory into numbers you measured. That matters because the right choice depends on your model size, your GPU, your data and how you will serve the model, and no paper can make that choice for you.

    You will:

    1. Predict the memory of each method before you run it, with a formula you can check (Figure 2).
    2. Fine-tune Qwen2.5-0.5B for sentiment on IMDB movie reviews and measure accuracy.
    3. Fine-tune it for summarization on XSum, and score the summaries with ROUGE and with an LLM judge.
    4. Fill one results table with memory, speed and quality for the three methods.
    5. Write a short “ship memo”: what you would deploy, and why.
    Interview tip“LoRA or full fine-tuning?” is one of the most common practical questions for LLM roles. A strong answer has numbers: how many parameters LoRA trains (formula 62.2), how much memory each method needs (formulas 62.3 to 62.6), and why LoRA does not reduce activation memory. All of these are on this page.

    Real systems use all three methods. The QLoRA authors fine-tuned a 65B model on a single 48 GB GPU, where regular 16-bit fine-tuning needs more than 780 GB [Certain]. LoRA adapters are the standard way to offer many customized models on one shared base model, and Hugging Face’s PEFT and TRL libraries (used in this lab) are the common open-source tools for it.

    Step 1: three ways to train one layer

    Start with one linear layer, the query projection q_proj of one block: a matrix \(W_0\) of shape 896 × 896, so 802,816 weights. The three methods differ only in what they store and what they update for this matrix.

    • Full fine-tuning updates \(W_0\) itself. Every weight gets a gradient and two Adam states.
    • LoRA freezes \(W_0\) and adds a small trainable path next to it: first a matrix \(A\) that maps the input down to \(r\) numbers, then a matrix \(B\) that maps them back up.
    • QLoRA does the same as LoRA, but stores the frozen \(W_0\) in 4 bits. Before each multiplication, a block of 4-bit weights is turned back into BF16 numbers (dequantized).
    AnalogyFull fine-tuning rewrites a whole textbook. LoRA keeps the book and adds a thin booklet of corrections that is read together with it. QLoRA does the same, but the book is a cheap, slightly blurry photocopy: it takes much less space, and the booklet must work with the blur.
    \[ \mathbf{h} = W_0 \mathbf{x} + \frac{\alpha}{r}\, B A \mathbf{x}, \qquad A \in \R^{r \times d_{in}},\quad B \in \R^{d_{out} \times r} \tag{62.1} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{h}\)
    the input vector of the layer (\(d_{in}\) numbers) and its output (\(d_{out}\) numbers)
    \(W_0\)
    the pretrained weight matrix, frozen in LoRA and QLoRA
    \(A, B\)
    the two small trainable matrices; \(A\) starts random and small, \(B\) starts at zero, so \(BA = 0\) and the model is unchanged at step 0
    \(r\)
    the rank: the width of the bottleneck (we use 16)
    \(\alpha\)
    a fixed scale (we use 32); the update is multiplied by \(\alpha / r\), here 2

    The number of trainable weights for one adapted layer is the size of \(A\) plus the size of \(B\):

    \[ P_{\text{layer}} = r\,(d_{in} + d_{out}) \quad\text{instead of}\quad d_{in}\, d_{out} \tag{62.2} \]

    For q_proj with \(r = 16\): \(16 \times (896 + 896) = 28{,}672\) weights instead of 802,816, which is 3.57% of the layer. Step through Figure 1 to see what happens in the forward pass, the backward pass and at serving time for each method.

    Worked example: the whole model

    Apply formula (62.2) to all seven linear layers of one Qwen2.5-0.5B block with \(r = 16\). The query and output projections are 896 → 896. The key and value projections are 896 → 128 (2 heads of 64). The gate and up projections are 896 → 4,864, and the down projection is 4,864 → 896.

    \(16 \times [\,2(896+896) + 2(896+128) + 3(896+4864)\,] = 16 \times 22{,}912 = 366{,}592\) per block. Times 24 blocks: 8,798,208 trainable weights, which is 1.78% of 494,032,768. PEFT’s print_trainable_parameters() reports the same 8,798,208. If you adapt only q_proj and v_proj (the setting of the original LoRA paper), you get 1,081,344 (0.22%).

    Step 2: predict the memory before you run

    A training run needs memory for four things: the weights, the gradients, the optimizer states (Adam keeps two numbers per trained weight), and the activations (values saved in the forward pass for the backward pass). Session 38 derived the classic budget. Here it is for our three methods, with \(N\) the number of parameters and \(P\) the number of LoRA parameters.

    Full fine-tuning with mixed precision keeps FP32 weights (4 bytes), FP32 gradients (4) and two FP32 Adam states (8), and runs the math in BF16:

    \[ M_{\text{full}} \approx 16\,N + M_{\text{act}} \tag{62.3} \]

    LoRA keeps the frozen base in BF16 (2 bytes, no gradient, no Adam states). The small LoRA weights are kept in FP32, so they cost the same 16 bytes each:

    \[ M_{\text{LoRA}} \approx 2\,N + 16\,P + M_{\text{act}} \tag{62.4} \]

    QLoRA stores the block linear weights \(N_{lin}\) in 4-bit NF4. Each block of 64 weights also needs a scale; with double quantization (Step 3) the total is 4.127 bits per weight. The other weights \(N_{oth} = N - N_{lin}\) (embeddings, norms) stay in higher precision. PEFT’s helper prepare_model_for_kbit_training casts them to FP32 (we checked its source code in PEFT 0.17), so they cost 4 bytes each:

    \[ M_{\text{QLoRA}} \approx b_4\, N_{lin} + 4\, N_{oth} + 16\,P + M_{\text{act}}, \qquad b_4 = \frac{4 + \frac{8}{64} + \frac{32}{64 \cdot 256}}{8} = 0.5159 \text{ bytes} \tag{62.5} \]

    The activations are about the same for all three methods, because the backward pass must still go through every layer. With gradient checkpointing (store only each block’s input, recompute the rest), a simple estimate is the stored block inputs in BF16 plus the output logits in FP32:

    \[ M_{\text{act}} \approx \underbrace{L \cdot b \cdot s \cdot d \cdot 2}_{\text{block inputs}} + \underbrace{b \cdot s \cdot V \cdot 4}_{\text{logits}} \tag{62.6} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(L, d, V\)
    the number of blocks, the hidden size and the vocabulary size
    \(b, s\)
    the micro-batch size (sequences per step on the GPU) and the sequence length in tokens
    \(N_{lin}, N_{oth}\)
    parameters in the block linear layers (quantized by QLoRA) and all other parameters
    \(b_4\)
    bytes per weight for NF4 with double quantization

    This is a lower bound, not a promise. Real peaks also include the recomputed activations of one block, temporary buffers, memory fragmentation and the CUDA context (often several hundred MB) [Likely]. Use it to rank the methods and to see which term dominates. Then measure the real peak with torch.cuda.max_memory_allocated().

    Worked example: Qwen2.5-0.5B, \(r = 16\), \(b = 8\), \(s = 512\)

    Full: \(16 \times 494{,}032{,}768 = 7.90\) GB. LoRA: \(2 \times 494{,}032{,}768 + 16 \times 8{,}798{,}208 = 0.988 + 0.141 = 1.13\) GB. QLoRA: \(0.5159 \times 357{,}826{,}560 + 4 \times 136{,}206{,}208 + 0.141 = 0.185 + 0.545 + 0.141 = 0.87\) GB.

    Activations: block inputs \(24 \times 8 \times 512 \times 896 \times 2 = 0.18\) GB; logits \(8 \times 512 \times 151{,}936 \times 4 = 2.49\) GB. So the estimated totals are 10.6 GB (full), 3.8 GB (LoRA) and 3.5 GB (QLoRA). All three fit on a 16 GB card at this batch size. Now compare with your measured peaks, and explain the gap.

    Step 3: what 4 bits do to the weights

    QLoRA stores each frozen weight as a 4-bit index into a list of 16 values. Which 16 values? Pretrained weights are roughly bell-shaped (normally distributed) around zero. Evenly spaced values (the INT4 grid) waste levels on the rare large weights. NF4 (4-bit NormalFloat) instead puts its 16 values at quantiles of a normal distribution, so each level is used about equally often [Certain]. The values are scaled to \([-1, 1]\); 0 is exact, there are 7 negative and 8 positive levels.

    The weights are cut into blocks of 64. Each block has its own scale \(c\), its largest absolute value (absmax):

    \[ c = \max_{i \in \text{block}} |w_i|, \qquad q_i = \argmin_{j} \left| \frac{w_i}{c} - v_j \right|, \qquad \hat{w}_i = c \cdot v_{q_i} \tag{62.7} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(v_0, \dots, v_{15}\)
    the 16 NF4 values, from \(-1\) through 0 to \(+1\)
    \(q_i\)
    the stored 4-bit index of weight \(i\)
    \(\hat{w}_i\)
    the dequantized BF16 weight that is actually used in the matrix multiplication

    Each block scale is one FP32 number for 64 weights: \(32/64 = 0.5\) extra bits per weight. Double quantization quantizes these scales again, to 8-bit numbers in blocks of 256, with one FP32 scale per 256 scales. The overhead drops to \(8/64 + 32/(64 \cdot 256) = 0.127\) bits per weight, which saves 0.373 bits per weight [Certain]. For the 6.5 billion block weights of Qwen2.5-7B, that is about 0.30 GB.

    One more point that is easy to miss: the forward and backward passes do not run in 4 bits. Each block is dequantized to BF16 right before it is used, and the gradient flows through the dequantized \(\hat{W}\). The 4 bits only save storage. The extra dequantization work is why a QLoRA step is usually slower than a LoRA step [Likely].

    Step 4: a toy you can train in your browser

    Before the real runs, build intuition with a model small enough to train in a fraction of a second. One 32 × 32 “pretrained” matrix \(W_0\) must learn a new task. The new task needs a change of rank \(k\): the target is \(W^\star = W_0 + \Delta\) with \(\operatorname{rank}(\Delta) = k\). We have \(n\) training examples with noisy labels. Full fine-tuning trains all 1,024 weights. LoRA trains \(A\) and \(B\) with rank \(r\), so \(64 r\) weights. QLoRA trains the same LoRA on top of an NF4 copy of \(W_0\). All three use Adam with learning rate 0.01 for 300 steps. We plot the held-out error: the error on new inputs, divided by the error of doing nothing (keeping \(W_0\)). So 1 means “no better than the base”, 0 means perfect.

    The toy is a single matrix with a known answer, so it is honest only about the shape of the trade-offs, not about real LLM numbers. Here is the same experiment in PyTorch, with a from-scratch LoRA layer and NF4 quantizer. It runs on a CPU in about a second and is deterministic.

    import torch, torch.nn as nn
torch.manual_seed(0)

# 1) The 16 NF4 code values (from the QLoRA paper / bitsandbytes)
NF4 = torch.tensor([-1.0, -0.6962, -0.5251, -0.3949, -0.2844, -0.1848, -0.0911, 0.0,
                    0.0796, 0.1609, 0.2461, 0.3379, 0.4407, 0.5626, 0.7230, 1.0])

def nf4_roundtrip(w, block=64):
    """Quantize w to NF4 with one absmax scale per block, then dequantize."""
    flat = w.reshape(-1, block)
    scale = flat.abs().amax(dim=1, keepdim=True)              # one FP32 number per block
    idx = ((flat / scale).unsqueeze(-1) - NF4).abs().argmin(-1) # 4-bit index per weight
    return (NF4[idx] * scale).reshape(w.shape)

# 2) A LoRA layer: frozen base weight + trainable low-rank update
class LoRALinear(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, W0, r=4, alpha=8, quantize=False):
        super().__init__()
        d_out, d_in = W0.shape
        base = nf4_roundtrip(W0) if quantize else W0.clone()
        self.register_buffer("W", base)                         # frozen: no gradient, no Adam state
        self.A = nn.Parameter(torch.randn(r, d_in) / d_in**0.5) # small random
        self.B = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(d_out, r))            # zero, so B A = 0 at step 0
        self.s = alpha / r
    def forward(self, x):
        return x @ self.W.T + self.s * (x @ self.A.T) @ self.B.T

class FullLinear(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, W0):
        super().__init__(); self.W = nn.Parameter(W0.clone())
    def forward(self, x):
        return x @ self.W.T

# 3) A "pretrained" W0 and a new task that needs a rank-4 change, with noisy labels
d, k, n = 32, 4, 64
W0 = torch.randn(d, d) / d**0.5
dW = torch.randn(d, k) @ torch.randn(k, d)
W_star = W0 + 0.5 * W0.norm() * dW / dW.norm()
X = torch.randn(n, d)
Y = X @ W_star.T + 0.5 * torch.randn(n, d) * (0.5 * W0.norm() / d**0.5)

def held_out(W):        # error on new inputs, relative to "do nothing" (W0)
    return ((W - W_star).norm()**2 / (W0 - W_star).norm()**2).item()

for name, layer in [("full", FullLinear(W0)), ("lora r=4", LoRALinear(W0, r=4)),
                    ("qlora r=4", LoRALinear(W0, r=4, quantize=True))]:
    opt = torch.optim.Adam([p for p in layer.parameters() if p.requires_grad], lr=1e-2)
    best = 9.9
    for step in range(300):
        loss = ((layer(X) - Y)**2).mean()
        opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
        W = layer.W if name == "full" else layer.W + layer.s * layer.B @ layer.A
        best = min(best, held_out(W.detach()))
    n_train = sum(p.numel() for p in layer.parameters() if p.requires_grad)
    print(f"{name:10s} trainable {n_train:5d}  train loss {loss.item():.4f}  "
          f"held-out end {held_out(W.detach()):.3f}  best {best:.3f}")
q = nf4_roundtrip(W0)
print("NF4 relative error of W0:", round(((q - W0).norm()**2 / W0.norm()**2).item(), 4))

    Read it like a lab result. Full fine-tuning has the lowest training loss and the highest held-out error: it memorized the noise. This is why we always score on held-out data, and why a lower training loss is never proof of a better method.

    Step 5: sentiment on IMDB

    A review is a document, and we want one label for it. A decoder-only model reads left to right, so its last token’s hidden state has seen the whole review. AutoModelForSequenceClassification adds a new linear layer (the “score” head) on top of that last hidden state \(\mathbf{h}_{last}\), and trains with cross-entropy:

    \[ \mathcal{L}_{cls} = -\log \softmax\!\left(W_s\, \mathbf{h}_{last}\right)_{y}, \qquad W_s \in \R^{2 \times d} \tag{62.8} \]

    Here \(y\) is the true label (0 or 1) and \(W_s\) is the new head. The head is randomly initialized, so it must be trained in all three methods. PEFT does this for you: with task_type="SEQ_CLS" it adds the head (named score in Qwen) to modules_to_save, which means “train this module fully and save it with the adapter” [Certain]. One detail breaks many first runs: the model finds the last real token by looking for the padding token, so model.config.pad_token_id must be set.

    Algorithm 1: one comparison run (same for both tasks)
    1. input: base model, method \(\in\) {full, LoRA, QLoRA}, fixed train and test subsets, seed 0
    2. load the base: FP32 (full), BF16 (LoRA) or NF4 with BF16 compute (QLoRA)
    3. if method is LoRA or QLoRA: freeze the base; add \(A, B\) with \(r = 16\), \(\alpha = 32\) to all linear layers
    4. set the learning rate: \(10^{-5}\) for full, \(2 \times 10^{-4}\) for LoRA and QLoRA // LoRA usually needs a ~10× larger LR [Likely]
    5. reset the GPU peak-memory counter; start a timer
    6. train one epoch: cosine schedule, 3% warmup, BF16, gradient checkpointing
    7. record peak memory, wall time and samples per second
    8. score on the held-out test subset; save the model or the adapter; record the file size

    The shared loader. It is the only place where the three methods differ:

    # common.py: load the base model in one of three ways
import torch
from transformers import BitsAndBytesConfig
from peft import LoraConfig, get_peft_model, prepare_model_for_kbit_training

MODEL = "Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B"

def load(auto_cls, method, task_type, **kw):
    if method == "full":                       # FP32 master weights + BF16 autocast (16 bytes/param)
        model = auto_cls.from_pretrained(MODEL, dtype=torch.float32, **kw)
    elif method == "lora":                     # frozen BF16 base (2 bytes/param)
        model = auto_cls.from_pretrained(MODEL, dtype=torch.bfloat16, **kw)
    elif method == "qlora":                    # frozen NF4 base (about 0.52 bytes/param)
        bnb = BitsAndBytesConfig(load_in_4bit=True, bnb_4bit_quant_type="nf4",
                                 bnb_4bit_use_double_quant=True,
                                 bnb_4bit_compute_dtype=torch.bfloat16)
        model = auto_cls.from_pretrained(MODEL, quantization_config=bnb,
                                         dtype=torch.bfloat16, **kw)
        model = prepare_model_for_kbit_training(model, use_gradient_checkpointing=True)
    if method in ("lora", "qlora"):
        cfg = LoraConfig(task_type=task_type, r=16, lora_alpha=32, lora_dropout=0.05,
                         target_modules="all-linear")
        model = get_peft_model(model, cfg)
        model.print_trainable_parameters()
    return model

def common_args(method):
    return dict(learning_rate=1e-5 if method == "full" else 2e-4,
                num_train_epochs=1, lr_scheduler_type="cosine", warmup_ratio=0.03,
                bf16=True, gradient_checkpointing=True,
                gradient_checkpointing_kwargs={"use_reentrant": False},
                optim="paged_adamw_32bit" if method == "qlora" else "adamw_torch",
                logging_steps=25, save_strategy="no", report_to="none", seed=0)

    The sentiment script. Run it three times: python train_cls.py full, then lora, then qlora.

    # train_cls.py: IMDB sentiment with full FT, LoRA or QLoRA.  Usage: python train_cls.py lora
import sys, time, json, numpy as np, torch
from datasets import load_dataset
from transformers import (AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForSequenceClassification, Trainer,
                          TrainingArguments, DataCollatorWithPadding, set_seed)
from common import MODEL, load, common_args

method = sys.argv[1]; set_seed(0)
tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(MODEL)
if tok.pad_token is None: tok.pad_token = tok.eos_token
ds = load_dataset("stanfordnlp/imdb")
train = ds["train"].shuffle(seed=0).select(range(5000))
test = ds["test"].shuffle(seed=0).select(range(2000))
enc = lambda b: tok(b["text"], truncation=True, max_length=512)
train = train.map(enc, batched=True, remove_columns=["text"])
test = test.map(enc, batched=True, remove_columns=["text"])

model = load(AutoModelForSequenceClassification, method, "SEQ_CLS", num_labels=2)
model.config.pad_token_id = tok.pad_token_id        # the head reads the last non-pad token

def metrics(p):
    return {"accuracy": float((np.argmax(p.predictions, -1) == p.label_ids).mean())}

args = TrainingArguments(output_dir=f"out/cls-{method}", per_device_train_batch_size=8,
                         gradient_accumulation_steps=2, per_device_eval_batch_size=16,
                         **common_args(method))
trainer = Trainer(model=model, args=args, train_dataset=train, eval_dataset=test,
                  data_collator=DataCollatorWithPadding(tok), compute_metrics=metrics)
torch.cuda.reset_peak_memory_stats()
t0 = time.time(); out = trainer.train(); dt = time.time() - t0
res = {"method": method, "accuracy": trainer.evaluate()["eval_accuracy"],
       "peak_GB": torch.cuda.max_memory_allocated() / 1e9,
       "train_minutes": dt / 60, "samples_per_s": out.metrics["train_samples_per_second"]}
print(json.dumps(res)); open(f"cls-{method}.json", "w").write(json.dumps(res))

    Three remarks on this code. First, full fine-tuning loads the model in FP32 and lets bf16=True run the math in BF16. If you load in BF16 and train all weights, the updates (learning rate \(10^{-5}\) times a small gradient) are often smaller than the BF16 step between two numbers near the weight, and they are lost [Likely]. Second, use_reentrant=False makes gradient checkpointing work when the input embeddings are frozen. Third, paged_adamw_32bit is the paged optimizer of the QLoRA paper (Adam with 32-bit states): its states can move to CPU memory when the GPU is briefly full, instead of crashing [Certain].

    Is the difference real? Accuracy on \(n\) test examples is an estimate with a standard error:

    \[ \mathrm{SE}(\hat{p}) = \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}\,(1 - \hat{p})}{n}} \tag{62.9} \]

    With \(\hat{p} = 0.92\) and \(n = 2{,}000\), \(\mathrm{SE} = 0.0061\), so a 95% interval is about \(\pm 1.2\) points. Two methods that differ by less than about one point on 2,000 reviews are not clearly different. Because all methods are scored on the same reviews, a paired comparison is stronger: count the reviews where one method is right and the other is wrong, in each direction, and compare the two counts (McNemar’s test).

    Step 6: summarization on XSum

    Now the model must write. We turn each article into a prompt and its reference summary into the completion. This is supervised fine-tuning (session 54): next-token cross-entropy, but only on the completion tokens \(C\):

    \[ \mathcal{L}_{SFT} = -\frac{1}{|C|} \sum_{t \in C} \log p_\theta\!\left(x_t \mid x_{\lt t}\right) \tag{62.10} \]

    TRL’s SFTTrainer does this when the dataset has prompt and completion columns (completion_only_loss, which is the default for such data), and it adds the end-of-sequence token after the completion [Certain]. That EOS token matters: it is how the model learns to stop after one sentence.

    # train_sum.py: XSum one-sentence summaries with TRL's SFTTrainer.  Usage: python train_sum.py qlora
import sys, time, json, torch
from datasets import load_dataset
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForCausalLM, set_seed
from trl import SFTConfig, SFTTrainer
from common import MODEL, load, common_args

method = sys.argv[1]; set_seed(0)
tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(MODEL)
if tok.pad_token is None: tok.pad_token = tok.eos_token

def to_pc(ex):                                   # prompt-completion format
    doc = ex["document"][:3000]                  # cut the ARTICLE, never the summary
    return {"prompt": f"Summarize the article in one sentence.\n\nArticle:\n{doc}\n\nSummary:",
            "completion": " " + ex["summary"]}  # TRL appends the EOS token
ds = load_dataset("EdinburghNLP/xsum")
train = ds["train"].shuffle(seed=0).select(range(5000)).map(to_pc, remove_columns=ds["train"].column_names)

model = load(AutoModelForCausalLM, method, "CAUSAL_LM")
cfg = SFTConfig(output_dir=f"out/sum-{method}", max_length=1024, completion_only_loss=True,
                per_device_train_batch_size=4, gradient_accumulation_steps=4, **common_args(method))
trainer = SFTTrainer(model=model, args=cfg, train_dataset=train, processing_class=tok)
torch.cuda.reset_peak_memory_stats()
t0 = time.time(); out = trainer.train(); dt = time.time() - t0
trainer.save_model(f"out/sum-{method}/final")   # full model, or only the adapter for LoRA/QLoRA
res = {"method": method, "peak_GB": torch.cuda.max_memory_allocated() / 1e9,
       "train_minutes": dt / 60, "samples_per_s": out.metrics["train_samples_per_second"]}
print(json.dumps(res)); open(f"sum-{method}.json", "w").write(json.dumps(res))

    We cut the article to 3,000 characters ourselves. If you let the trainer cut long rows at max_length, it cuts the end of the row, and the end is the summary: the model then trains on rows with no target at all.

    Then generate summaries for 500 test articles and score them. Note the left padding: in a batch of prompts of different lengths, a decoder model must have all prompts end at the same position, so the padding goes on the left.

    # eval_sum.py: generate summaries for 500 test articles and score them with ROUGE
import sys, json, torch
from datasets import load_dataset
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForCausalLM
from peft import PeftModel
from rouge_score import rouge_scorer
from common import MODEL

method = sys.argv[1]                              # "base", "full", "lora" or "qlora"
tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(MODEL); tok.padding_side = "left"   # left-pad for generation
if tok.pad_token is None: tok.pad_token = tok.eos_token
path = MODEL if method in ("base", "lora", "qlora") else f"out/sum-{method}/final"
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(path, dtype=torch.bfloat16).to("cuda")
if method in ("lora", "qlora"):                   # BF16 base + adapter, merged = the artifact you ship
    model = PeftModel.from_pretrained(model, f"out/sum-{method}/final").merge_and_unload()
model.eval()

test = load_dataset("EdinburghNLP/xsum")["test"].shuffle(seed=0).select(range(500))
prompts = [f"Summarize the article in one sentence.\n\nArticle:\n{d[:3000]}\n\nSummary:" for d in test["document"]]
outs = []
for i in range(0, len(prompts), 16):
    b = tok(prompts[i:i+16], return_tensors="pt", padding=True).to("cuda")
    with torch.no_grad():
        g = model.generate(**b, max_new_tokens=60, do_sample=False, pad_token_id=tok.pad_token_id)
    for row in tok.batch_decode(g[:, b["input_ids"].shape[1]:], skip_special_tokens=True):
        outs.append(row.strip().split("\n")[0])   # keep the first line only

sc = rouge_scorer.RougeScorer(["rouge1", "rouge2", "rougeL"], use_stemmer=True)
scores = [sc.score(r, p) for r, p in zip(test["summary"], outs)]
res = {k: sum(s[k].fmeasure for s in scores) / len(scores) for k in ["rouge1", "rouge2", "rougeL"]}
print(method, json.dumps(res))
json.dump({"method": method, "rouge": res, "preds": outs}, open(f"preds-{method}.json", "w"))

    Run eval_sum.py base as well. The untrained base model is your baseline: every method must beat it clearly, or the training did not work.

    ROUGE

    ROUGE (Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation) counts word overlap between a candidate summary and the reference. ROUGE-N uses n-grams (sequences of \(N\) words), counting each n-gram at most as often as it appears in the other text:

    \[ R_N = \frac{\#\text{matched n-grams}}{\#\text{n-grams in reference}}, \quad P_N = \frac{\#\text{matched n-grams}}{\#\text{n-grams in candidate}}, \quad F_N = \frac{2 P_N R_N}{P_N + R_N} \tag{62.11} \]

    ROUGE-L uses the longest common subsequence (LCS): the longest list of words that appears in both texts in the same order, with gaps allowed.

    \[ R_L = \frac{\mathrm{LCS}}{|\text{ref}|}, \qquad P_L = \frac{\mathrm{LCS}}{|\text{cand}|}, \qquad F_L = \frac{2 P_L R_L}{P_L + R_L} \tag{62.12} \]

    ROUGE is cheap, deterministic and useful for comparing runs of the same system. But it only sees words, not meaning. Figure 5 shows the problem with real numbers.

    We verified the three presets with the rouge_score package. In eval_sum.py we turn stemming on (use_stemmer=True), which is common for summarization and gives slightly different numbers. Always report which setting you used.

    Step 7: an LLM judge

    An LLM judge is a strong instruction-tuned model that reads the article and two summaries and says which is better. Zheng et al. found that strong judges agree with human preferences about as well as humans agree with each other on their benchmarks, but they also found biases [Certain]: position bias (preferring the answer shown first), verbosity bias (preferring longer answers) and self-enhancement bias (preferring answers in their own style). Our script fights the first two directly.

    Algorithm 2: pairwise judging with a position swap
    1. input: test articles, summaries from system \(a\) and system \(b\), a judge model that is not one of the systems
    2. for each article:
    3.   \(v_1 \leftarrow\) judge(article, A = summary of \(a\), B = summary of \(b\)) // greedy decoding: deterministic
    4.   \(v_2 \leftarrow\) judge(article, A = summary of \(b\), B = summary of \(a\)), then map the answer back
    5.   if \(v_1 = v_2\): count that verdict else: count a tie // the verdict depended on the order
    6. return wins \(W\), ties \(T\), losses \(L\), and the win rate (62.13)
    # judge.py: pairwise LLM judge with position swap.  Usage: python judge.py lora full
import sys, json, torch
from datasets import load_dataset
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForCausalLM

a, b = sys.argv[1], sys.argv[2]
JUDGE = "Qwen/Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct"               # any strong instruct model that is NOT one of the contestants
jt = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(JUDGE)
jm = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(JUDGE, dtype=torch.bfloat16, device_map="auto").eval()
test = load_dataset("EdinburghNLP/xsum")["test"].shuffle(seed=0).select(range(500))
pa = json.load(open(f"preds-{a}.json"))["preds"]; pb = json.load(open(f"preds-{b}.json"))["preds"]

RUBRIC = ("You compare two one-sentence summaries of a news article.\n"
          "First check faithfulness: a summary that states something the article does not support loses.\n"
          "Then check coverage of the main point, then clarity. Length is not a quality.\n"
          "Answer with one word on the last line: A, B or TIE.")
def ask(doc, s1, s2):
    msg = [{"role": "system", "content": RUBRIC},
           {"role": "user", "content": f"Article:\n{doc[:3000]}\n\nSummary A: {s1}\n\nSummary B: {s2}"}]
    enc = jt.apply_chat_template(msg, add_generation_prompt=True, return_tensors="pt",
                                 return_dict=True).to(jm.device)
    with torch.no_grad():
        out = jm.generate(**enc, max_new_tokens=200, do_sample=False)
    words = jt.decode(out[0, enc["input_ids"].shape[1]:], skip_special_tokens=True).split()
    last = words[-1].upper().strip(".*:") if words else "TIE"
    return last if last in ("A", "B", "TIE") else "TIE"

win = tie = loss = 0
for doc, x, y in zip(test["document"], pa, pb):
    v1 = ask(doc, x, y)                          # a shown first
    v2 = {"A": "B", "B": "A", "TIE": "TIE"}[ask(doc, y, x)]   # b shown first, mapped back
    v = v1 if v1 == v2 else "TIE"                # disagree after the swap: count as a tie
    win += v == "A"; loss += v == "B"; tie += v == "TIE"
n = win + tie + loss
print(f"{a} vs {b}: win {win}, tie {tie}, loss {loss}, win rate {(win + 0.5 * tie) / n:.3f}")
    \[ \text{win rate}(a, b) = \frac{W + \tfrac12 T}{W + T + L} \tag{62.13} \]

    A win rate of 0.5 means “no difference”. It has noise too. As an illustration with made-up counts: 180 wins, 170 ties and 150 losses over 500 articles give a win rate of \((180 + 85)/500 = 0.53\), and a rough 95% interval of \(\pm 0.044\) (formula 62.9 with \(n = 500\)). That interval includes 0.5, so this result alone would not show that \(a\) is better. Before you trust any judge, read 20 of its verdicts yourself and check that you agree with most of them.

    Watch outDo not use one of the fine-tuned models, or the base model, as the judge. Also keep the rubric fixed across all comparisons: changing the prompt between runs changes the ruler, not the systems.

    Step 8: measure speed and fill the table

    Record three speed numbers per run: wall time, train_samples_per_second from the trainer, and the peak memory. Here is what to expect for compute, from the FLOP rules of session 25. A training step costs about \(2N\) FLOPs per token forward and \(4N\) backward: \(2N\) for gradients of the inputs (to pass the gradient down) and \(2N\) for gradients of the weights. A frozen weight needs no weight gradient. Gradient checkpointing adds one more forward pass:

    \[ C_{\text{full}} \approx (6 + 2)\,N, \qquad C_{\text{LoRA}} \approx (4 + 2)\,N \quad \text{FLOPs per token, with checkpointing} \tag{62.14} \]

    So LoRA can save at most about 25% of the compute here (\(6/8 = 0.75\)); the LoRA matrices themselves add only about \(1.78\%\) of the model’s FLOPs at \(r = 16\). In practice, small models are often limited by memory traffic and kernel overheads rather than FLOPs, so the measured speedup can be smaller [Likely]. QLoRA adds dequantization in every forward pass and in the backward pass, so expect it to be slower than LoRA [Likely]. The big gain of LoRA is memory, which lets you use a larger batch or a longer sequence on the same card. That can make it faster in wall time, even if a single step is not.

    What the papers found, so you know what to look for in your own table:

    • The LoRA paper reports results that match or beat full fine-tuning on RoBERTa, DeBERTa, GPT-2 and GPT-3 for the tasks it tested, with no extra latency after merging [Certain].
    • The QLoRA paper reports that 4-bit NF4 with double quantization matches 16-bit LoRA and 16-bit full fine-tuning on GLUE (RoBERTa-large) and Super-NaturalInstructions (T5 up to 11B; full fine-tuning compared up to 3B), and recovers 16-bit LoRA MMLU results for LLaMA 7B to 65B. It also found that LoRA must be applied to all linear layers to match full fine-tuning; attention-only LoRA fell short [Certain].
    • Biderman et al. (“LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less”) found that on code and math, with large training sets, LoRA is clearly worse than full fine-tuning on the target task, but keeps more of the base model’s other skills [Certain].

    Our tasks are much easier than code and math, and our training sets are small. Whether the methods tie on your table is exactly what you are measuring. Do not assume the answer.

    Also measure forgetting. A fine-tuned model may lose general skills (session 61). A cheap check: compute the perplexity of each final model on a few hundred sentences of general text (for example WikiText) and compare it with the base model. Put it in an extra column.

    Step 9: write down what you would ship

    The last step is a short memo, half a page, as if for your team. A decision without written reasons cannot be reviewed. Use this structure:

    1. Requirement. Example: “Classify reviews at 200 requests per second on one L4; summaries for an internal tool; three more tasks next quarter.”
    2. Results. Your table, with intervals: accuracy \(\pm\) SE, win rates with counts.
    3. Decision. One method, and the artifact you ship: merged weights, or base model plus adapter.
    4. Why. The deciding facts: quality difference versus noise, training cost, serving cost, number of future tasks.
    5. Risks and checks. What you will monitor, and what would make you change the decision.

    Some rules that usually decide it:

    • If the quality difference is inside the noise, pick the cheapest method to train and to serve. For a 0.5B model that is usually LoRA in BF16: it fits easily, and QLoRA saves little memory at this size (Figure 2) while running slower.
    • If you will serve many tasks or customers, LoRA adapters win: one shared base in GPU memory, a small adapter per task (8.8M weights here, about 35 MB in FP32 or 18 MB in BF16), and serving engines that can batch requests for different adapters together [Likely].
    • If the model does not fit in 16-bit on your training GPU, QLoRA is often the only option, and it is a good one.
    • If full fine-tuning is clearly better on a task that matters (beyond the interval), and you can afford it, ship it, but check forgetting first.
    • Evaluate the exact artifact you ship. A QLoRA adapter was trained against the 4-bit base. Merged into the BF16 base, it gives a slightly different model. Our eval_sum.py scores the merged BF16 model for this reason. If you will serve the 4-bit base plus the adapter instead, score that.
    AnalogyChoosing a method is like choosing a car for a company. The fastest car on a test track is not the answer; the answer depends on the roads, the budget and how many drivers share it. The memo lists those facts so someone else can check the choice.

    Common mistakes

    1. Different settings per method. Different data, seeds, steps or sequence lengths make the comparison meaningless. Only the method and its learning rate should change.
    2. The same learning rate for all three. \(2 \times 10^{-4}\) is a common LoRA rate but usually far too high for full fine-tuning; \(10^{-5}\) is often too low for LoRA. Tune each method briefly on a validation split [Likely].
    3. No pad_token_id for classification. The classifier reads the last non-padding token. Without the padding id it cannot find it and fails for batches larger than one.
    4. Truncating away the target. Cutting long rows at the end removes the summary. Cut the article instead.
    5. Right padding at generation time. A decoder model then continues from padding tokens. Use padding_side="left" for batched generation.
    6. Thinking LoRA saves activation memory. It saves gradients and optimizer states of the frozen weights. Activations stay; use gradient checkpointing or a smaller batch.
    7. Trusting ROUGE alone, or a judge alone. ROUGE misses meaning (Figure 5); judges have position and length biases. Use both, swap positions, and read samples yourself.
    8. Evaluating a different artifact than you ship. Score the merged model, or the base plus adapter, in the exact precision you will serve.

    Interview questions

    Why is \(B\) initialized to zero in LoRA, and not \(A\) as well?basic
    With \(B = 0\), the product \(BA\) is zero, so the model at step 0 is exactly the pretrained model and training starts from a known good point. If both were zero, both gradients would be zero (\(\partial L/\partial B\) depends on \(A\mathbf{x}\), and \(\partial L/\partial A\) depends on \(B\)), and nothing would ever train. With random \(A\) and zero \(B\), \(B\) gets a gradient at the first step, and \(A\) starts to move after that.
    How many weights does LoRA train with \(r = 16\) on all linear layers of Qwen2.5-0.5B?core
    Use \(r(d_{in} + d_{out})\) per layer. Per block: q and o are 896 → 896, k and v are 896 → 128, gate and up are 896 → 4,864, down is 4,864 → 896. Sum: \(2 \cdot 1{,}792 + 2 \cdot 1{,}024 + 3 \cdot 5{,}760 = 22{,}912\), times 16 = 366,592 per block, times 24 blocks = 8,798,208, which is 1.78% of 494M. Full fine-tuning trains all 494M.
    Does LoRA reduce activation memory? Explain.core
    Mostly no. The backward pass must send the gradient through every frozen layer down to the lowest adapter, and computing those input gradients needs the stored activations. LoRA removes the gradients, the Adam states and the FP32 master copy of the frozen weights: 14 of the 16 bytes per parameter in mixed precision. Activations are reduced only by gradient checkpointing, a smaller batch, a shorter sequence, or tricks such as chunked loss computation for the large logits tensor.
    In QLoRA, what is stored in 4 bits and what is not? In what precision is the math done?core
    Only the frozen weights of the linear layers are stored in 4-bit NF4, in blocks of 64 with double-quantized scales. Embeddings, norms and usually the output head stay in 16 or 32 bits. The LoRA weights, their gradients and the Adam states are in higher precision. Each block of 4-bit weights is dequantized to BF16 just before its matrix multiplication, so the forward and backward math is BF16, and gradients flow through the dequantized weights into the LoRA matrices and the layers below.
    Why NF4 instead of plain INT4?advanced
    Pretrained weights are roughly normally distributed. INT4 spaces its levels evenly, so many levels sit in the tails where few weights live. NF4 places its 16 levels at quantiles of a normal distribution (after absmax scaling to \([-1, 1]\)), so each level is used about equally often, which gives a lower error for normal data. It also has an exact zero. In Figure 3, blocks of 64 give a relative error of 0.0082 for NF4 versus 0.0109 for INT4.
    What does double quantization save? Compute it.core
    Without it, each block of 64 weights has one FP32 scale: 32/64 = 0.5 bits per weight. With it, the scales are stored as 8-bit numbers, with one FP32 scale per 256 scales: \(8/64 + 32/(64 \cdot 256) = 0.127\) bits per weight. The saving is 0.373 bits per weight, about 0.30 GB for the 6.5B quantized weights of Qwen2.5-7B, and about 3 GB for a 65B model.
    Why is ROUGE not enough to evaluate summaries, and how do you make an LLM judge trustworthy?core
    ROUGE counts word overlap with one reference. A faithful paraphrase can score low and a summary with a wrong fact can score high (0.343 versus 0.842 ROUGE-1 in Figure 5). An LLM judge reads the article and checks meaning. To trust it: use a strong judge that is not one of the systems, a fixed rubric that puts faithfulness first, greedy decoding, both orders of the two answers (count disagreements as ties), a statement that length is not quality, enough examples to make the interval small, and a manual check of a sample of verdicts against your own judgment.
    When does LoRA fall short of full fine-tuning?advanced
    When the task needs a large change of the weights: big domain shifts with a lot of data, such as code or math continued pretraining. Biderman et al. found LoRA clearly worse there, and that full fine-tuning learns weight changes of much higher rank than typical LoRA settings. Fixes: higher rank, LoRA on all linear layers, a tuned learning rate, or full fine-tuning. The flip side: LoRA forgets less of the base model’s other skills.
    How do you serve a LoRA model, and what changes for a QLoRA-trained adapter?core
    Two options. Merge: compute \(W_0 + (\alpha/r)BA\) once and serve a normal model, with no extra latency. Or keep the adapters separate: one base model in memory and many small adapters, with an engine that applies the right adapter per request, which is cheaper when you have many tasks. For a QLoRA adapter, you either serve the 4-bit base plus the adapter (exactly what was trained), or merge into the 16-bit base, which is slightly different from the trained model. Either way, evaluate the artifact you actually serve.
    Your QLoRA run is slower per step than LoRA. Why, and when is it still the right choice?advanced
    Every forward and backward pass must dequantize the 4-bit weights to BF16 before each matrix multiplication, which adds work and memory traffic. It is still right when the 16-bit base plus activations does not fit on the GPU you have (for example a 7B to 70B model on one card), or when the freed memory lets you use a larger batch or longer sequences. For a 0.5B model on a 24 GB GPU, LoRA in BF16 is usually the better choice.
    What does the \(\alpha / r\) scale do? What happens if you double \(r\) and keep \(\alpha\)?basic
    It scales the LoRA update. With \(\alpha\) fixed, doubling \(r\) halves the scale, so the effective step size of the update changes and you may need to re-tune the learning rate. Many people set \(\alpha = 2r\) (as here) or \(\alpha = r\), keeping the scale constant. rsLoRA proposes \(\alpha/\sqrt{r}\) so that larger ranks keep learning well; PEFT supports it with use_rslora=True.

    Key takeaways

    • LoRA (62.1) freezes \(W_0\) and trains \(r(d_{in} + d_{out})\) weights per layer (62.2): 8.8M of 494M for Qwen2.5-0.5B at \(r = 16\) on all linear layers.
    • Memory: full fine-tuning costs 16 bytes per parameter (62.3), LoRA 2 bytes plus 16 per LoRA weight (62.4), QLoRA about 0.52 bytes per quantized weight (62.5). Activations (62.6) are the same for all three, and for small models with big vocabularies the logits can dominate.
    • NF4 (62.7) uses normal quantiles in blocks of 64; double quantization cuts the scale overhead from 0.5 to 0.127 bits per weight. The math still runs in BF16.
    • Fewer trained weights can generalize better on small, noisy data, but a low rank can limit hard tasks (Figure 4, Biderman et al.).
    • Score with held-out data and intervals (62.9). For summaries, use ROUGE (62.11, 62.12) and a position-swapped LLM judge (62.13); ROUGE alone rewards copying words.
    • Ship the cheapest method whose quality is within the noise of the best, and evaluate the exact artifact you serve.

    Key formulas

    \[ \mathbf{h} = W_0\mathbf{x} + \tfrac{\alpha}{r} BA\mathbf{x}, \quad P = r(d_{in} + d_{out}), \quad M_{\text{full}} \approx 16N, \quad M_{\text{LoRA}} \approx 2N + 16P \]
    \[ M_{\text{QLoRA}} \approx 0.516\,N_{lin} + 4N_{oth} + 16P, \quad \mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}, \quad \text{win rate} = \frac{W + T/2}{n} \]
