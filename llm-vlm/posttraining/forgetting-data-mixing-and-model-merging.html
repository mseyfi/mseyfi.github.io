    Forgetting, data mixing and model merging

    When we fine-tune a model on a new task, it often gets worse at things it could already do. This session explains why this catastrophic forgetting happens, how to limit it (mix in general data, use a lower learning rate, train only a LoRA adapter, or pull the weights back), and how to combine several fine-tuned models by doing arithmetic on their weights: averaging, model soups, task vectors, TIES and DARE. It ends with how to measure a regression so that you can trust the result.

    Time: 2.5 to 3 hours (reading 2 hours, exercises 45 minutes)Level: Beginner to interview levelBefore this: Sessions 4, 36, 40, 54 and 57

    What you will learn

    • What catastrophic forgetting is, and why gradient steps for a new task can raise the loss on old skills (first- and second-order view)
    • Four ways to limit it: replay of general data, a lower learning rate or fewer steps, LoRA as a built-in regularizer, and a pull-back penalty such as EWC
    • Why replaying general data and EWC are the same idea for a quadratic loss, and how to choose a mixing ratio
    • Weight interpolation (WiSE-FT), model soups and linear mode connectivity: when averaging weights works and when it fails
    • Task vectors and task arithmetic, plus TIES-Merging and DARE for merging several fine-tuned models
    • How to measure a regression: a fixed evaluation suite, paired comparisons, confidence intervals and how many examples you need

    Why this matters

    In session 54 and session 57 we learned how to teach a model a new skill. This session is about the price. When you fine-tune a model on a narrow task, it often becomes worse at things it could do before: general knowledge, math, other languages, or even following the chat format. This is called catastrophic forgetting. Every team that fine-tunes models meets it, and interviewers ask about it often.

    The name comes from McCloskey and Cohen (1989), who saw that a small neural network trained on a second list of facts lost the first list almost completely. [Certain] Large language models forget too. Luo et al. (2023) fine-tuned models from 1B to 7B parameters on a sequence of tasks and saw forgetting at all sizes; in that range, the larger models even forgot more, partly because they started from a higher level. [Certain] The InstructGPT paper calls the drop in general benchmark scores after alignment the alignment tax, and reduced it by mixing pretraining data into the RL updates (“PPO-ptx”). [Certain]

    This session has three parts:

    • Why forgetting happens, with a simple picture: the weights move to where the new task is good, and that place can be bad for the old skills.
    • How to limit it: mix in general data (replay), use a lower learning rate or fewer steps, use LoRA as a regularizer, or add a penalty that pulls important weights back.
    • Model merging: do arithmetic on the weights of several fine-tuned models. Weight averaging, model soups, task vectors, TIES and DARE. Merging can undo forgetting and combine skills without any extra training.

    And one practical question ties the parts together: how do you know that you have a regression, and not just noise in the evaluation?

    Interview tipCommon questions: “You fine-tuned on medical data and MMLU dropped 5 points. Why, and what do you do?”, “Why does LoRA forget less?”, “What is a model soup?”, “Why can you average two fine-tuned models but not two models trained from scratch?”, “Is a 1-point drop on 1,000 questions real?”. Each one is answered below.

    Real systems use these ideas every day. Llama 3 averaged models trained with different data or hyperparameters at each post-training stage (reward model, SFT and DPO). [Certain] Continual pretraining recipes mix a few percent of old data into the new data to avoid forgetting. [Certain] Open-source model hubs are full of merged models built with tools such as MergeKit, and a merged 7B model built with DARE once ranked first among 7B models on the Open LLM Leaderboard. [Certain]

    Catastrophic forgetting: why fine-tuning can break general skills

    A pretrained model has weights \(\theta_0\). These weights are good at many things at once, because pretraining saw a huge, mixed dataset. Fine-tuning then runs gradient descent on a new loss \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}\) only. Nothing in that loss says “stay good at the old things”. The weights move to wherever the new loss is low, and the old skills are only safe if that place happens to be good for them too.

    We measure forgetting on a held-out set of “general” evaluations that the fine-tuning data does not cover:

    \[ F = A_{\text{gen}}(\theta_0) - A_{\text{gen}}(\theta_{\text{ft}}) \tag{61.1} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(A_{\text{gen}}(\theta)\)
    the score (for example accuracy) of the model with weights \(\theta\) on general evaluations: knowledge, math, code, other languages, chat behavior
    \(\theta_0\), \(\theta_{\text{ft}}\)
    the weights before and after fine-tuning
    \(F\)
    forgetting: how many points the general score dropped. \(F \gt 0\) is a regression; \(F/A_{\text{gen}}(\theta_0)\) is the relative drop

    The first-order view: gradients that disagree

    Take one small step of size \(\eta\) on the task loss: \(\Delta\theta = -\eta \nabla \mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}\). A Taylor expansion of the general loss gives its change:

    \[ \Delta \mathcal{L}_{\text{gen}} \approx \nabla \mathcal{L}_{\text{gen}}^{\top} \Delta\theta = -\eta \, \nabla \mathcal{L}_{\text{gen}}^{\top} \nabla \mathcal{L}_{\text{task}} \tag{61.2} \]

    If the two gradients point in similar directions (positive dot product), the task step also helps the general skills. If they point in opposite directions (negative dot product), the task step hurts the general skills. This is called gradient interference. The same weights store many skills, so the directions often overlap.

    The second-order view: moving away from a good place

    There is a catch. A well-pretrained model sits near a minimum of the general loss, so \(\nabla \mathcal{L}_{\text{gen}}(\theta_0) \approx 0\) and formula (61.2) says almost nothing. The real damage comes from the distance travelled. Near a minimum, the general loss grows like a bowl:

    \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{gen}}(\theta_0 + \Delta\theta) - \mathcal{L}_{\text{gen}}(\theta_0) \approx \tfrac{1}{2}\, \Delta\theta^{\top} H_{\text{gen}}\, \Delta\theta \tag{61.3} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\Delta\theta\)
    the total change of the weights during fine-tuning, \(\theta_{\text{ft}} - \theta_0\)
    \(H_{\text{gen}}\)
    the Hessian (matrix of second derivatives) of the general loss at \(\theta_0\). It says how fast the loss grows in each direction: large in “sharp” directions, small in “flat” ones

    Formula (61.3) gives us the whole story of this section. Forgetting is large when the weights move far (large \(\Delta\theta\)) and in sharp directions of the general loss. So every fix does one of two things: move less, or move in flat directions.

    AnalogyA city has a good bus network. A new hospital opens, and you redesign the routes only to serve the hospital. The hospital is now easy to reach, but old routes that many people used are gone. Small changes to quiet routes cost little (flat directions); changing the main lines costs a lot (sharp directions). A good planner changes less, and changes mainly the quiet routes.

    Why LLM fine-tuning is especially at risk

    • The data is narrow. A domain set (legal, medical, code in one language) covers a tiny part of what pretraining covered. Nothing in it reminds the model of the rest.
    • Many steps on few examples. Several epochs on a small set move the weights far in the directions that only this set cares about.
    • The learning rate is high relative to the change we need. A pretraining-size learning rate, or rewarming it to a high peak in continual pretraining, moves the weights a lot. Ibrahim et al. (2024) found that a higher rewarmed peak learning rate gives more adaptation and more forgetting. [Certain]
    • Format and behavior are fragile. Fine-tuning on plain question–answer pairs without the chat template can break the chat behavior of an instruct model, even when its knowledge is fine. Some of what looks like lost knowledge is lost format, and a little training on the old format brings it back. [Likely]

    Figure 1 makes formula (61.3) concrete with two weights. The general loss is a narrow valley: flat along \(\theta_1\), sharp along \(\theta_2\). The task loss is a round bowl with its minimum somewhere else. Plain fine-tuning walks straight to the task minimum and climbs the steep side of the general valley.

    Fix 1: mix in general data (replay)

    The most direct fix is to keep reminding the model of the old skills. Instead of training on task data only, each batch contains a share \(\alpha\) of general data: a sample of pretraining text, or of the general SFT mix that made the model an assistant. This is called replay or data mixing. In expectation, the model minimizes a weighted sum of the two losses:

    \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{mix}}(\theta) = (1 - \alpha)\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}(\theta) + \alpha\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{gen}}(\theta) \tag{61.4} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\alpha\)
    the mixing ratio: the share of each batch (in tokens or examples) that comes from general data. \(\alpha = 0\) is plain fine-tuning
    \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{gen}}\)
    the loss on the replayed general data: next-token loss on pretraining text, or the SFT loss on general chats

    Now the gradient of every step contains a part that says “stay good at general things”. In Figure 1 the path bends: it moves along the flat direction, where the general loss barely rises, and avoids the sharp one. For each \(\alpha\) the end point is the minimum of (61.4); all these minima together form the grey curve of best trade-offs.

    How much to mix

    • Continual pretraining (more pretraining on new text, such as a new language or new code): Ibrahim et al. (2024) used 5% replay of the old data for a weak shift (English to English data) and 25% for a strong shift (English to German), together with rewarming and re-decaying the learning rate, and this matched retraining from scratch on all the data. They tested this at 405M and 10B parameters. [Certain]
    • SFT for a domain: mix the domain examples with the general instruction data of the original model. Llama 3’s final SFT mix was 52.66% general English examples, with the rest covering skills such as code, reasoning and tool use. [Certain] Luo et al. (2023) found that a general instruction-tuning stage before the narrow fine-tune also reduces forgetting. [Certain]
    • RL and preference training: InstructGPT’s PPO-ptx adds a pretraining next-token loss to the RL objective. [Certain]

    The general data should be close to what the model was trained on before. A random web crawl is a poor stand-in for the original SFT mix of an assistant. [Likely] If you do not have the original data (common for open-weight models), you can use an open instruction dataset of similar style, or generate replay data with the original model itself. [Likely]

    Worked example: the replay budget

    You continue pretraining on 10B tokens of new data with 5% replay, measured as a share of all tokens. The new data is 95% of the total, so the total is \(10/0.95 = 10.53\)B tokens, and replay adds 0.53B tokens: about 5% more compute. With 25% replay, replay adds \(10/0.75 - 10 = 3.33\)B tokens, a third more.

    For SFT: 40,000 domain examples and \(\alpha = 0.2\) means \(40000/0.8 = 50{,}000\) examples per epoch, so 10,000 of them general. Replay is cheap insurance compared with the cost of a model that forgets.

    Fix 2: move less (lower learning rate, fewer steps, pull-back)

    Formula (61.3) says forgetting grows with the size of \(\Delta\theta\). The simplest ways to move less are a lower learning rate, fewer steps (fewer epochs) and early stopping. They help, but they are blunt: they slow down movement in all directions, including the directions the task needs. In Figure 1 the low-learning-rate path stops early on the straight line toward the task minimum. It forgets less than plain fine-tuning, but it is worse than mixing on both losses at once.

    A smarter version slows down only the directions that matter for the old skills. Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) (Kirkpatrick et al., 2017) adds a penalty that pulls each weight back toward its old value, with a strength that depends on how important the weight was for the old task: [Certain]

    \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{EWC}}(\theta) = \mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}(\theta) + \frac{\lambda}{2} \sum_{i} F_i \,(\theta_i - \theta_{0,i})^2 \tag{61.5} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\theta_{0,i}\)
    weight \(i\) before fine-tuning
    \(F_i\)
    the importance of weight \(i\) for the old skills: the diagonal of the Fisher information, \(F_i = \E\big[(\partial \log p_\theta(y \mid x) / \partial \theta_i)^2\big]\), averaged over old data. Near a minimum it approximates the diagonal of \(H_{\text{gen}}\)
    \(\lambda\)
    the pull-back strength. With all \(F_i = 1\) this is plain L2 regularization toward the starting point (sometimes called L2-SP)

    Here is a useful connection. If the general loss is exactly the bowl of (61.3) and \(F\) equals its Hessian, then the EWC penalty is the general loss times \(\lambda\). Dividing (61.4) by \(1 - \alpha\) shows that mixing with ratio \(\alpha\) is the same objective as EWC with

    \(\lambda = \alpha / (1 - \alpha)\),

    so \(\alpha = 0.5\) matches \(\lambda = 1\). That is why Figure 1 ends at the same point for both. In real networks the two differ: replay measures the true general loss, while EWC uses a local, diagonal approximation that becomes wrong once the weights move far. This is one reason replay is the default for LLMs, and EWC is rarely used at LLM scale. [Likely] EWC also needs the Fisher values for every weight, which is one more copy of the model in memory.

    Fix 3: LoRA as a regularizer

    LoRA (session 57) freezes the pretrained weight matrix \(W_0\) and learns only a low-rank update:

    \[ W = W_0 + \Delta W, \qquad \Delta W = \frac{\alpha_{\text{LoRA}}}{r}\, B A, \qquad B \in \R^{d \times r},\ A \in \R^{r \times k} \tag{61.6} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(W_0\)
    the frozen pretrained matrix, of size \(d \times k\)
    \(B\), \(A\)
    the two small trained matrices; \(B\) starts at zero, so training starts exactly at the pretrained model
    \(r\)
    the rank, much smaller than \(d\) and \(k\) (often 8 to 64)
    \(\alpha_{\text{LoRA}}\)
    a fixed scale (not the mixing ratio \(\alpha\) of formula 61.4)

    The rank limit is a strong constraint on \(\Delta\theta\): each matrix can change only inside an \(r\)-dimensional space of directions, and all other directions stay exactly where pretraining put them. Biderman et al. (2024) compared LoRA and full fine-tuning on code and math, for both instruction tuning and continued pretraining. Their title says it: LoRA learns less and forgets less. LoRA reached lower scores on the target domain but kept more of the base model’s skills outside it, and it forgot less than common regularizers such as weight decay and dropout. [Certain] They also found that full fine-tuning learns weight changes with a rank 10 to 100 times higher than typical LoRA settings. [Certain]

    Worked example: how small is the LoRA space?

    One attention projection in a 7B-class model is \(4096 \times 4096 = 16{,}777{,}216\) weights. LoRA with \(r = 16\) trains \(r(d + k) = 16 \times 8192 = 131{,}072\) numbers, which is 0.78% of the matrix. Every \(\Delta W\) it can produce has rank at most 16, out of a possible 4,096.

    Two warnings. First, LoRA does not make forgetting impossible: a large rank, a high learning rate and many epochs can still move the model far, and in our PyTorch example below a rank-2 LoRA still forgets a lot on a toy task. Second, if your task truly needs a big change (a new language, a lot of new knowledge), the rank limit hurts learning; then full fine-tuning plus replay is usually the better choice. [Likely]

    A useful bonus: a LoRA adapter can be switched off. The base model is still there, unchanged, so for requests outside the domain you can serve the base model and lose nothing. [Certain]

    Model merging, part 1: averaging weights

    Now a surprising idea. Instead of training one model that is good at everything, train several models and average their weights. Not their outputs (that would be an ensemble, which costs \(K\) forward passes), but the numbers inside the weight matrices, giving one model with the normal cost.

    \[ \theta(\beta) = (1 - \beta)\,\theta_0 + \beta\,\theta_{\text{ft}}, \qquad \theta_{\text{soup}} = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \theta_k \tag{61.7} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\beta\)
    the interpolation weight: 0 gives the pretrained model, 1 the fine-tuned model, values in between lie on the straight line between them
    \(\theta_k\)
    \(K\) fine-tuned models, all started from the same pretrained weights (for example with different learning rates, seeds or data orders)
    \(\theta_{\text{soup}}\)
    a uniform model soup: the plain average of the \(K\) models

    When does averaging work? Linear mode connectivity

    Averaging weights sounds like it should fail. A neural network has many symmetries: you can swap two hidden units (and their weights in the next layer) and get the exact same function. Two networks trained from different random starts may store the same feature in different units, so the average mixes unrelated units and the result is broken.

    The key fact is that this problem mostly goes away when both models start from the same weights. Frankle et al. (2020) showed that networks become “stable to SGD noise” early in training: after that point, two runs that differ only in data order end in minima connected by a straight line with no rise in error between them. This is called linear mode connectivity. [Certain] Fine-tuning from a shared pretrained model is the extreme case: all fine-tuned models start from the same point and usually stay in the same wide basin. [Likely] Ainsworth et al. (Git Re-Basin) showed that even independently trained ResNets on CIFAR-10 can be merged without a loss barrier if you first permute the units of one network to match the other. [Certain]

    AnalogyTwo hikers start at the same hut in a wide valley and walk to two good camping spots. The straight line between their spots stays in the valley, so the midpoint is also a good spot. Two hikers who start in different valleys may each find a good spot, but the straight line between them crosses a mountain.

    WiSE-FT: interpolate the base and the fine-tuned model

    The first formula in (61.7) is WiSE-FT (Wortsman et al., 2022). They fine-tuned zero-shot models such as CLIP and then moved part of the way back toward the original weights. On ImageNet, this improved accuracy under distribution shift by 4 to 6 points over prior work while also raising ImageNet accuracy by 1.6 points, at no extra cost in training or inference. [Certain] For LLMs, the same trick trades task skill against general skill after training, with one knob \(\beta\) and no retraining.

    Model soups

    The second formula is a model soup (Wortsman et al., 2022). The usual practice is to run a hyperparameter sweep and keep the best model. A soup averages the models of the sweep instead. A uniform soup averages all of them. A greedy soup sorts the models by validation score and adds each one to the soup only if the averaged model gets better on held-out data. With ViT-G, a soup reached 90.94% top-1 accuracy on ImageNet. [Certain] Llama 3 used the same idea in post-training, averaging models from runs with different data or hyperparameters at the reward model, SFT and DPO stages. [Certain]

    Figure 2 trains three small networks live in your browser and walks along straight lines between them. You can see both effects: the trade-off of WiSE-FT, and the barrier between two independently trained networks.

    Model merging, part 2: task vectors and their arithmetic

    Ilharco et al. (2023) looked at fine-tuning as a vector. The task vector of a task is the change that fine-tuning made to the weights:

    \[ \tau_t = \theta_t - \theta_0, \qquad \theta_{\text{merged}} = \theta_0 + \lambda \sum_{t=1}^{T} \tau_t \tag{61.8} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\theta_t\)
    the model fine-tuned on task \(t\), started from \(\theta_0\)
    \(\tau_t\)
    its task vector: one number per weight, the same shape as the model
    \(\lambda\)
    a scaling factor, tuned on validation data. \(\lambda = 1/T\) is plain averaging of the \(T\) models

    They showed three operations, across several models and tasks: [Certain]

    • Addition: \(\theta_0 + \lambda(\tau_A + \tau_B)\) gives one model that is good at both tasks, without training on both.
    • Negation: \(\theta_0 - \lambda \tau_A\) makes the model worse at task A while other tasks are mostly unaffected. One use is to remove an unwanted behavior (for example toxic text) by negating the task vector of a model fine-tuned to produce it.
    • Analogies: if tasks are related as “A is to B as C is to D”, then \(\tau_C + (\tau_B - \tau_A)\) can improve task D without any data for D.

    Note that WiSE-FT is task arithmetic with one task: \(\theta(\beta) = \theta_0 + \beta\,\tau\).

    Interference: why plain addition loses information

    When we average \(T\) task vectors, each one is divided by \(T\). If only one task changed a weight, that change is shrunk to \(1/T\) of its size. If two tasks changed the same weight in opposite directions, they cancel, and neither task gets what it needed. TIES-Merging (Yadav et al., 2023) names these two problems: redundant small values and sign disagreement. [Certain] It fixes them in three steps:

    \[ \hat\tau_t = \mathrm{top}_k(\tau_t), \qquad \gamma_p = \mathrm{sgn}\Big(\sum_t \hat\tau_{t,p}\Big), \qquad \tau_{m,p} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}_p|} \sum_{t \in \mathcal{A}_p} \hat\tau_{t,p} \tag{61.9} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\mathrm{top}_k(\tau_t)\)
    trim: keep the \(k\%\) of entries of \(\tau_t\) with the largest magnitude and set the rest to 0
    \(p\)
    one weight (parameter) position
    \(\gamma_p\)
    elect the sign: the sign with the larger total movement over all tasks at position \(p\)
    \(\mathcal{A}_p\)
    the tasks whose trimmed value at \(p\) is non-zero and has the sign \(\gamma_p\)
    \(\tau_{m,p}\)
    disjoint merge: average only over the agreeing tasks. The merged model is \(\theta_0 + \lambda \tau_m\)

    When no validation data is available, the paper’s recipe keeps the top 20% of each task vector and uses \(\lambda = 1\). [Certain]

    DARE: drop most of the delta and rescale

    Yu et al. (2024) observed that SFT task vectors of LLMs are tiny and very redundant: the changes usually stay within 0.002 in absolute value. [Certain] DARE (Drop And REscale) randomly drops a share \(p\) of each task vector and scales up the rest so the expected value is unchanged:

    \[ \hat\tau_t = \frac{(1 - m_t) \odot \tau_t}{1 - p}, \qquad m_{t,i} \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(p) \tag{61.10} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(m_{t,i}\)
    1 if entry \(i\) of task vector \(t\) is dropped (probability \(p\)), else 0
    \(\odot\)
    element-wise product
    \(1/(1-p)\)
    the rescale factor; it keeps \(\E[\hat\tau_t] = \tau_t\)

    They report that DARE can drop 90% or even 99% of the delta with little loss, and that it helps when several models are merged. [Certain] In practice it is used as a first step before another merge method, such as task arithmetic or TIES. [Likely] It has a clear limit: after continued pretraining the deltas can reach about 0.03, and then DARE fails; for a code model built on extra code pretraining, removing just 10% of the delta dropped HumanEval and MBPP to 0. [Certain] Merging methods assume small, compatible task vectors from the same base.

    Other methods you will meet in merge tools: SLERP (spherical interpolation between two models, which keeps the norm of the weights), and Fisher merging (a weighted average where each weight counts in proportion to its Fisher importance, like EWC). [Likely]

    Worked example: TIES by hand on Figure 3

    Task vectors (entries 1 to 8): \(\tau_1 = (0.9, -0.1, 0.05, 0.6, -0.7, 0.02, 0.3, -0.05)\), \(\tau_2 = (0.8, 0.5, -0.04, -0.6, -0.45, 0.01, -0.02, 0.4)\), \(\tau_3 = (-0.1, 0.6, 0.03, -0.5, 0.06, -0.8, 0.25, 0.02)\).

    Average: \((0.533, 0.333, 0.013, -0.167, -0.363, -0.257, 0.177, 0.123)\).

    Trim (keep 4 largest per task): \(\hat\tau_1 = (0.9, 0, 0, 0.6, -0.7, 0, 0.3, 0)\), \(\hat\tau_2 = (0.8, 0.5, 0, -0.6, -0.45, 0, 0, 0)\), \(\hat\tau_3 = (0, 0.6, 0, -0.5, 0, -0.8, 0.25, 0)\).

    Elect: sums \((1.7, 1.1, 0, -0.5, -1.15, -0.8, 0.55, 0)\), so signs \((+, +, 0, -, -, -, +, 0)\).

    Disjoint mean: \(\tau_m = (0.85, 0.55, 0, -0.55, -0.575, -0.8, 0.275, 0)\). Its length is 1.54, close to the length of a single task vector (1.15 to 1.33), while the plain average has length only 0.82: averaging shrinks every task’s change.

    How to measure regressions

    All the fixes above need a measurement: did the general skills drop, and by how much? This is harder than it looks, because an evaluation score is itself a noisy estimate. A drop from 70.0% to 68.0% can be a real regression or just noise, depending on the number of questions and on how you compare.

    Build a regression suite before you fine-tune

    • Cover the skills you must keep, not only the target task: knowledge (MMLU-style), math (GSM8K-style), code (HumanEval-style), instruction following, multi-turn chat, safety (harmful requests refused, harmless ones not over-refused), long context and other languages if you serve them (session 40, session 155).
    • Check behavior, not only scores: does the model still stop at the end-of-turn token, use the chat template, keep its answer length, and call tools in the right format? Format breaks are among the most common regressions after fine-tuning. [Likely]
    • Fix everything except the weights: same prompts, same chat template, same decoding settings (greedy or fixed seeds), same scoring code. Otherwise you measure the change of the setup, not of the model.
    • Compare against two references: the model you started from, and the model currently in production.
    • Decontaminate: the fine-tuning data must not contain the evaluation questions.

    Is the drop real? Confidence intervals

    For accuracy \(p\) on \(n\) independent questions, the standard error (SE) is \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\). If we treat the two models as independent samples, the SE of the difference is:

    \[ \mathrm{SE}_{\text{unpaired}}(\hat p_B - \hat p_A) = \sqrt{\frac{\hat p_A(1 - \hat p_A) + \hat p_B(1 - \hat p_B)}{n}} \tag{61.11} \]

    But both models answer the same questions. Most questions are easy or hard for both; only the questions where they disagree carry information about the difference. The paired SE uses this:

    \[ \mathrm{SE}_{\text{paired}} = \sqrt{\frac{d - (\hat p_B - \hat p_A)^2}{n}}, \qquad d = b + c,\quad \hat p_A - \hat p_B = b - c \tag{61.12} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\hat p_A\), \(\hat p_B\)
    accuracy of the old and the new model on the same \(n\) questions
    \(b\)
    share of questions the old model got right and the new model got wrong (regressions)
    \(c\)
    share the old model got wrong and the new model got right (improvements)
    \(d\)
    share of questions where the two models disagree

    A 95% confidence interval is the difference \(\pm 1.96\,\mathrm{SE}\). If it does not include 0, the change is unlikely to be noise. The paired SE is usually much smaller, because \(d\) is much smaller than \(2p(1-p)\). For a paired test on the counts \(b\) and \(c\), this is closely related to McNemar’s test. [Certain] A paired bootstrap over questions gives the same kind of interval for any metric, including judge scores. [Certain]

    Worked example: 70% to 68% on 1,000 questions

    The old model scores 70.0%, the new one 68.0%, on \(n = 1000\) questions. The SE of one score is \(\sqrt{0.7 \times 0.3 / 1000} = 1.45\) points. Unpaired, the SE of the difference is \(\sqrt{(0.21 + 0.2176)/1000} = 2.07\) points, so the 95% interval is \(-2.0 \pm 4.05\), from −6.05 to +2.05 points: we cannot tell.

    Now pair the answers. The models disagree on 10% of questions: 6% were right and became wrong, 4% the other way. Then \(\mathrm{SE}_{\text{paired}} = \sqrt{(0.10 - 0.0004)/1000} = 1.00\) point, and the interval is from −3.96 to −0.04 points. With the same data, the paired view says this is (just) a real regression.

    How many questions do you need to detect a 1-point change? With \(d = 10\%\) and the paired test, the interval half-width \(1.96\sqrt{0.0999/n}\) reaches 1 point at \(n \approx 3{,}838\). Unpaired, at \(p = 0.7\), you would need about 16,135.

    Two more traps. First, many comparisons: if you check 20 benchmarks at 95% confidence, about one will look significant by chance. Look for patterns (several benchmarks of the same skill moving together) or use a stricter threshold. [Certain] Second, seed noise: two fine-tunes with different seeds can differ by more than the effect you study. If you can, train with two or three seeds and compare the spread. [Likely]

    Putting it together

    Algorithm 1: fine-tune with replay and a regression gate
    1. input: base \(\theta_0\), task data \(D_{\text{task}}\), general data \(D_{\text{gen}}\), mixing ratio \(\alpha\), regression suite \(S\), budget \(\epsilon\)
    2. score the base on \(S\) and on the task validation set; save per-question results // needed for paired tests
    3. for each step: build a batch with share \(\alpha\) from \(D_{\text{gen}}\) and \(1 - \alpha\) from \(D_{\text{task}}\) // formula (61.4)
    4.   take an optimizer step with a low learning rate (or train a LoRA adapter, 61.6)
    5. at each checkpoint: score on \(S\) with the same prompts, template and decoding
    6.   for each benchmark: paired difference and its 95% interval // formula (61.12)
    7. if some benchmark drops by more than \(\epsilon\) with an interval below 0: raise \(\alpha\), lower the learning rate or the rank, or interpolate back (61.7)
    8. return the checkpoint with the best task score among those that pass the gate
    Algorithm 2: merge \(T\) fine-tuned models (TIES, optionally after DARE)
    1. input: base \(\theta_0\), fine-tuned models \(\theta_1, \dots, \theta_T\) from the same base, keep share \(k\), drop rate \(p\), scale \(\lambda\)
    2. for each task \(t\): \(\tau_t \leftarrow \theta_t - \theta_0\) // task vector, (61.8)
    3.   optional DARE: drop each entry with probability \(p\), divide the rest by \(1 - p\) // (61.10)
    4.   trim: keep the top \(k\%\) of entries by magnitude, set the rest to 0 // (61.9)
    5. for each parameter \(p\): \(\gamma_p \leftarrow \mathrm{sgn}(\sum_t \hat\tau_{t,p})\) // elect
    6.   \(\tau_{m,p} \leftarrow\) mean of the non-zero \(\hat\tau_{t,p}\) with sign \(\gamma_p\) // disjoint merge
    7. \(\theta \leftarrow \theta_0 + \lambda\,\tau_m\); choose \(\lambda\) (and \(k\)) on validation data, or use \(k = 20\%\), \(\lambda = 1\)
    8. run the regression suite on \(\theta\) as in Algorithm 1 // merges can regress too

    The PyTorch code below shows every idea of this session on a small model that runs in a few seconds on a CPU. A network learns a “general” skill, then is fine-tuned on a new task in four ways (plain, low learning rate, 20% replay, LoRA). Then it interpolates the weights (WiSE-FT) and adds two task vectors. The printed numbers are the real output.

    import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F
torch.manual_seed(0)
D, C, H = 16, 4, 64

# 1) three "skills": each is a labeling rule on its own region of input space
def make_task(seed, shift, n):
    W = torch.randn(D, C, generator=torch.Generator().manual_seed(seed))
    def sample(m, s):
        x = torch.randn(m, D, generator=torch.Generator().manual_seed(s)) + shift
        return x, (x @ W).argmax(1)
    return sample(n, seed + 1), sample(2000, seed + 2)          # (train, test)
def region(k):
    v = torch.zeros(D); v[k] = 6.0; return v
GEN = make_task(10, 0.0, 4000)        # the "general" skill, learned in pretraining
TA  = make_task(20, region(0), 1000)  # new task A
TB  = make_task(30, region(1), 1000)  # new task B

def net(): return nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(D, H), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(H, H), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(H, C))
def acc(m, task):
    x, y = task[1]
    with torch.no_grad(): return (m(x).argmax(1) == y).float().mean().item()
def clone(m):
    c = net(); c.load_state_dict(m.state_dict()); return c

def train(m, sets, steps, lr, mix=None, seed=0):
    """Mini-batches of 32 rows; each row comes from set j with probability mix[j], formula (61.4)."""
    g = torch.Generator().manual_seed(seed)
    opt = torch.optim.AdamW([p for p in m.parameters() if p.requires_grad], lr=lr, weight_decay=0.0)
    mix = torch.tensor(mix or [1.0] * len(sets))
    for _ in range(steps):
        src = torch.multinomial(mix, 32, replacement=True, generator=g)
        xs, ys = [], []
        for j, (x, y) in enumerate(sets):
            i = torch.randint(0, len(x), (int((src == j).sum()),), generator=g)
            xs.append(x[i]); ys.append(y[i])
        loss = F.cross_entropy(m(torch.cat(xs)), torch.cat(ys))
        opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    return m

# 2) LoRA: freeze W, learn a rank-r update B @ A, formula (61.6)
class LoRA(nn.Module):
    def __init__(s, lin, r=2, alpha=4):
        super().__init__(); s.lin, s.scale = lin, alpha / r
        for p in s.lin.parameters(): p.requires_grad = False
        s.A = nn.Parameter(torch.randn(r, lin.in_features) / lin.in_features ** 0.5)
        s.B = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(lin.out_features, r))     # starts as an exact copy of the base
    def forward(s, x): return s.lin(x) + s.scale * (x @ s.A.T @ s.B.T)
def with_lora(m):
    m = clone(m)
    for i in (0, 2, 4): m[i] = LoRA(m[i])
    return m

base = train(net(), [GEN[0]], 3000, 3e-3)                       # "pretraining"
g0 = acc(base, GEN)
print(f"base       general {g0:.3f}  task A {acc(base, TA):.3f}")

# 3) fine-tune on task A: plain, low learning rate, 20% general replay, LoRA
runs = {"plain FT": train(clone(base), [TA[0]], 600, 3e-3),
        "low LR":   train(clone(base), [TA[0]], 600, 3e-4),
        "20% mix":  train(clone(base), [TA[0], GEN[0]], 600, 3e-3, mix=[0.8, 0.2]),
        "LoRA r=2": train(with_lora(base), [TA[0]], 600, 3e-3)}
for name, m in runs.items():   # forgetting = general accuracy before minus after, formula (61.1)
    print(f"{name:10s} general {acc(m, GEN):.3f}  task A {acc(m, TA):.3f}  forgetting {g0 - acc(m, GEN):+.3f}")

# 4) WiSE-FT: walk on the straight line from base to the fine-tuned weights, formula (61.7)
ft, sb = runs["plain FT"], base.state_dict()
for a in (0.25, 0.5, 0.75):
    m = clone(base); m.load_state_dict({k: (1 - a) * sb[k] + a * ft.state_dict()[k] for k in sb})
    print(f"alpha {a:.2f} general {acc(m, GEN):.3f}  task A {acc(m, TA):.3f}")

# 5) task arithmetic: base + lam * (tau_A + tau_B), formula (61.8)
ftB = train(clone(base), [TB[0]], 600, 3e-3)
tau = lambda m: {k: m.state_dict()[k] - sb[k] for k in sb}
tA, tB = tau(ft), tau(ftB)
for lam in (0.5, 1.0):
    m = clone(base); m.load_state_dict({k: sb[k] + lam * (tA[k] + tB[k]) for k in sb})
    print(f"merge lam {lam:.1f} general {acc(m, GEN):.3f}  A {acc(m, TA):.3f}  B {acc(m, TB):.3f}")
print(f"only B     general {acc(ftB, GEN):.3f}  A {acc(ftB, TA):.3f}  B {acc(ftB, TB):.3f}")

    Read the output like a regression report. Plain fine-tuning learns task A (94.6%) but loses 44.9 points of the general skill. A ten times lower learning rate forgets less but also learns less. Replay (20% general data) keeps most of the general skill (−7.6 points) and still learns the task (92.7%): the best trade-off here. LoRA with rank 2 learns less (78.1%) and forgets less than plain fine-tuning, as Biderman et al. found, but on this toy it is not as good as replay. WiSE-FT gives a smooth trade-off between the two ends. The last lines are a warning: on this small network, adding the two task vectors keeps only part of each skill. Each fine-tune moved the weights far, and the two moves interfere. Task arithmetic works best for large pretrained models whose task vectors are small, the setting where DARE measured deltas within 0.002. [Likely] Always measure a merge before you trust it.

    Common mistakes

    • Measuring only the target task. The task score goes up and nobody checks the rest. Run a fixed general suite on the base before training and on every candidate after.
    • Fine-tuning an instruct model without its chat template, or with a different one. The model “forgets” how to chat, stop and follow the system prompt. This is often a format regression, not lost knowledge (session 9).
    • A pretraining-size learning rate and many epochs on a small domain set. Formula (61.3): far moves cause forgetting. Start low (around \(10^{-5}\) or below for full fine-tuning) and use 1 to 3 epochs. [Likely]
    • Replay data that does not match the original training data, such as random web text for an assistant. It does not protect the skills you care about.
    • Merging models that do not share a base, or that use different tokenizers or architectures. Weight averaging only makes sense within one basin; two independently trained models give a broken average (Figure 2).
    • Using DARE or TIES on large deltas, such as a model after continued pretraining. DARE fails when deltas are large. Check the size of the task vectors first.
    • Calling a 1-point change a regression without a confidence interval. Use paired comparisons on the same questions, report the interval, and be careful with many benchmarks at once.
    • Comparing runs with different evaluation settings (decoding, prompt format, number of shots). The difference you see may come from the setup.

    Interview questions

    What is catastrophic forgetting, and why does it happen in fine-tuning?basic
    It is the loss of earlier skills when a model is trained on new data. Fine-tuning minimizes only the new task loss, so the weights move to wherever that loss is low; nothing keeps them in the region that is good for the old skills. Near the pretrained minimum the general loss grows like \(\tfrac12 \Delta\theta^\top H \Delta\theta\) (61.3), so the damage is large when the weights move far, and in directions where the general loss is sharp. Narrow data, many epochs and high learning rates all make the move larger.
    You fine-tuned an 8B model on medical QA and MMLU dropped 5 points. What do you do?core
    First check that the drop is real and not a setup change: same template, prompts and decoding; compute a paired confidence interval. Check formatting (does the model still answer in the expected format, stop correctly?). Then reduce the move: mix 10% to 30% general instruction data into the training mix, lower the learning rate and epochs, or train a LoRA adapter instead of all weights. You can also interpolate the fine-tuned weights back toward the base (WiSE-FT) and choose \(\beta\) on a validation set of both medical and general questions. Re-run the full regression suite on each candidate, and choose the best medical score among those that pass.
    Why does mixing in general data help, and how is it related to EWC?core
    Replay makes the loss (61.4) a weighted sum of task and general loss, so every gradient step also keeps the general loss low; the path bends toward directions where the general loss is flat. EWC (61.5) adds \(\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\sum_i F_i(\theta_i - \theta_{0,i})^2\), a quadratic approximation of the general loss around the old weights with diagonal Fisher importance. If the general loss were exactly that quadratic, mixing with ratio \(\alpha\) would equal EWC with \(\lambda = \alpha/(1-\alpha)\). In practice replay measures the true loss and works far from \(\theta_0\), while EWC is a local, diagonal approximation; for LLMs replay is much more common.
    Why does LoRA forget less than full fine-tuning?core
    LoRA freezes \(W_0\) and learns \(\Delta W = BA\) of rank at most \(r\). This limits the weight change to a small subspace per matrix and keeps all other directions exactly as pretrained, so \(\Delta\theta\) is restricted and the general loss cannot rise as much. Biderman et al. (2024) found that LoRA learns less on the target domain (code, math) but keeps more of the base model's other skills, more than weight decay or dropout do, and that full fine-tuning learns changes with 10 to 100 times higher rank. It is not a guarantee: large ranks, high learning rates and long training still forget. And because the base is unchanged, the adapter can be turned off for out-of-domain requests.
    What is a model soup, and how is it different from an ensemble?basic
    A model soup averages the weights of several models fine-tuned from the same pretrained model with different hyperparameters (61.7). A uniform soup averages all of them; a greedy soup adds models in order of validation score and keeps each only if the held-out score improves. An ensemble averages the outputs of the models and costs \(K\) forward passes; a soup is a single model with the normal cost. Soups work because fine-tuned models from one base lie in one connected basin. With ViT-G a soup reached 90.94% on ImageNet; Llama 3 averaged models at each post-training stage.
    Why can you average two fine-tunes of the same model but not two models trained from scratch?advanced
    Neural networks have permutation symmetries: hidden units can be reordered without changing the function. Two models trained from different random starts may use different units for the same feature, and there is usually a high loss barrier on the straight line between them, so the average is bad. Models fine-tuned from one pretrained checkpoint start at the same point and stay in one basin, so the line between them has no barrier (linear mode connectivity; Frankle et al. showed that runs become stable to SGD noise early in training). Git Re-Basin shows that permuting units to align two independent models can remove the barrier in some cases.
    What is a task vector, and what can you do with it?core
    A task vector is \(\tau = \theta_{\text{ft}} - \theta_0\), the change fine-tuning made to the weights. Task arithmetic (61.8) edits a model by adding scaled task vectors: adding several gives a multi-task model without joint training; negating one removes a behavior (for example toxic generation) while leaving other tasks mostly intact; analogies such as \(\tau_C + \tau_B - \tau_A\) can improve a task with no data. WiSE-FT is the one-task case. The scale \(\lambda\) is tuned on validation data.
    Explain TIES-Merging. Which problems of plain averaging does it fix?advanced
    Plain averaging divides each task's change by \(T\) and lets opposite changes cancel. TIES (61.9) first trims each task vector to its largest-magnitude entries (for example top 20%), removing small redundant changes. It then elects one sign per parameter, the sign with the larger total movement, and finally averages only the entries that agree with that sign. Changes made by one task survive at full size, and conflicting tasks do not cancel each other. The no-validation recipe uses top 20% and \(\lambda = 1\).
    What does DARE do, and when does it fail?advanced
    DARE drops each entry of a task vector with probability \(p\) and multiplies the remaining entries by \(1/(1-p)\), so the expected task vector is unchanged (61.10). Because SFT deltas are small (usually within 0.002) and redundant, \(p = 0.9\) or more often works; sparser deltas interfere less when several models are merged, so DARE is a common step before TIES or averaging. It fails when deltas are large, for example after continued pretraining (deltas around 0.03): for a code model built on extra code pretraining, dropping only 10% gave 0 on HumanEval and MBPP.
    Your new model scores 68.0% vs 70.0% on 1,000 questions. Is it a regression?core
    Unpaired, the SE of the difference is about 2.07 points, so the 95% interval (−6.05 to +2.05) includes 0. But both models answered the same questions, so use a paired test: if they disagree on 10% of questions (6% right to wrong, 4% wrong to right), the paired SE is \(\sqrt{(0.10 - 0.0004)/1000} \approx 1.0\) point and the interval (−3.96 to −0.04) excludes 0, a borderline real regression. Also check that the setup was identical, look at which questions regressed, check several benchmarks of the same skill, and remember that with many benchmarks some will look significant by chance.
    How would you add a new skill to a deployed assistant without losing the old ones?advanced
    Options from cheap to expensive. (1) Train a LoRA adapter for the skill and route only relevant requests to it, so the base is untouched. (2) Fine-tune with replay of the assistant's original post-training mix (or a close stand-in), at a low learning rate, then interpolate toward the old weights if the regression suite fails. (3) Train a specialist from the same base and merge it with the current model (TIES or DARE plus averaging), tuning \(\lambda\) on validation data of both skills. (4) Redo the last post-training stage with the new data added to the full mix. In all cases, gate the release on a regression suite with paired confidence intervals, including safety and format checks.

Divide (61.4) by \(1 - \alpha\): \(\lambda = \alpha/(1 - \alpha) = 0.2/0.8 = 0.25\). 3. \(r(d + k) = 8 \times (4096 + 11008) = 120{,}832\) numbers, against \(4096 \times 11008 = 45{,}088{,}768\) in the matrix: 0.27%. 4. It becomes \(0.001/(1 - 0.9) = 0.01\).     Key takeaways

    • Fine-tuning minimizes only the new loss, so the weights can leave the region that was good for old skills. Forgetting grows with how far the weights move and how sharp the general loss is in those directions (61.3).
    • Replay is the main fix: mix 5% to 30% general data, close to the original training data, into every batch (61.4). For a quadratic loss it equals EWC with \(\lambda = \alpha/(1-\alpha)\) (61.5).
    • A lower learning rate and fewer steps reduce forgetting but also learning. LoRA limits the change to a low-rank space (61.6): it learns less and forgets less, and the base stays intact.
    • Models fine-tuned from the same base can be averaged: WiSE-FT, model soups (61.7) and task arithmetic (61.8). Models from different random starts cannot, because of a loss barrier.
    • TIES (61.9) trims, elects signs and averages only agreeing values; DARE (61.10) drops and rescales small deltas. Both assume small task vectors from one base.
    • A regression is real only if its confidence interval excludes 0. Compare on the same questions with a paired test (61.12), keep the setup fixed, and cover all skills you must keep.

    Key formulas

    \[ F = A_{\text{gen}}(\theta_0) - A_{\text{gen}}(\theta_{\text{ft}}), \qquad \Delta \mathcal{L}_{\text{gen}} \approx \tfrac12 \Delta\theta^\top H_{\text{gen}} \Delta\theta \]
    \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{mix}} = (1-\alpha)\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}} + \alpha \mathcal{L}_{\text{gen}}, \qquad \mathcal{L}_{\text{EWC}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{task}} + \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\textstyle\sum_i F_i(\theta_i - \theta_{0,i})^2 \]
    \[ \theta(\beta) = (1-\beta)\theta_0 + \beta\theta_{\text{ft}}, \qquad \tau_t = \theta_t - \theta_0, \qquad \theta = \theta_0 + \lambda \textstyle\sum_t \tau_t \]
    \[ \hat\tau_t = \frac{(1 - m_t)\odot\tau_t}{1-p}, \qquad \mathrm{SE}_{\text{paired}} = \sqrt{\frac{d - (\hat p_B - \hat p_A)^2}{n}} \]
