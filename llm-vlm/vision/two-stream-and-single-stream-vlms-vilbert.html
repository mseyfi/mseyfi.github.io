    Two-stream and single-stream VLMs: ViLBERT, LXMERT, UNITER

    Before CLIP and before LLaVA, vision-language models were BERT-style encoders that read object-detector regions and words together. ViLBERT and LXMERT keep two streams that talk through co-attention; UNITER puts everything in one stream. This session shows how regions become tokens, what the pretraining losses (masked words, masked regions, image–text matching) compute, what the papers measured, and why this design faded.

    Time: 3 to 4 hours (reading 2 hours, exercises 1 to 2 hours)Level: Beginner to interview levelBefore this: Sessions on BERT-style masked language modeling, the Vision Transformer (164) and CLIP (166)

    What you will learn

    • How a Faster R-CNN detector turns an image into 10 to 36 region tokens, each a 2048-d feature plus a 5-d or 7-d box vector
    • The co-attention layer of ViLBERT and the cross-modality layer of LXMERT, and how they differ from one joint stream (UNITER)
    • Why a two-stream layer pair and a single-stream layer compute the same number of attention scores, (T + K)² = T² + K² + 2TK
    • The pretraining losses: masked language modeling with the image, three kinds of masked region modeling, image–text matching, image QA and word–region alignment
    • What the ablations measured: conditional masking, more data and in-domain data, and the ViLBERT single-stream baseline
    • Why these models faded: about 900 ms per image for region features, under 10M training pairs, one forward pass per image–text pair for retrieval, and no text generation
    • Which ideas survive in ALBEF, BLIP, BLIP-2 and modern VLM rerankers

    Why this matters

    In 2018, BERT showed that one large encoder, pretrained on masked words and then fine-tuned with a small head, beats task-specific models on almost every text task. In 2019, several groups asked the obvious next question: can we do the same for images and text together? The answers were ViLBERT, LXMERT, VisualBERT, VL-BERT and, a few months later, UNITER. They are the first generation of pretrained vision-language models.

    They all share one recipe. An object detector finds 10 to 100 regions in the image (a person, a horse, a hat). Each region becomes one visual token, just like a word is one text token. A transformer reads the words and the regions and learns, from image–caption pairs, which words go with which regions. Then the same model is fine-tuned for visual question answering (VQA), visual commonsense reasoning (VCR), referring expressions and image–text retrieval.

    The models differ in one main design choice: where the two modalities meet. ViLBERT and LXMERT use two streams, one transformer per modality, that exchange information through special co-attention layers. UNITER (and VisualBERT, VL-BERT) use one stream: the words and regions are joined into one sequence and every layer is ordinary self-attention.

    Interview tipTypical questions: “Single-stream vs dual-stream: which is better, and why?”, “What is image–text matching and how are negatives made?”, “Why did region-based models disappear?”, “Why can a CLIP-style model do retrieval over millions of images but UNITER cannot?”. Each one is answered below with numbers from the papers.

    Why study models from 2019 in a course about modern VLMs? Three reasons. First, their pretraining losses are still used: ALBEF and BLIP (session 173) keep masked language modeling and image–text matching, and BLIP-2’s Q-Former uses image–text matching too. Second, the two-stream versus one-stream question did not go away: Flamingo-style cross-attention versus LLaVA-style “just put image tokens in the sequence” is the same question at a larger scale (session 179). Third, the reasons these models faded (a slow detector, small paired data, no text generation) explain the design of everything that came after: CLIP’s huge web data (session 166), ViT patches instead of regions (session 164), and decoder LLMs that write answers.

    Keep one difference from CLIP in mind for the whole session. CLIP is a dual encoder: image and text are encoded separately and compared only at the end with a dot product. The models here are fusion encoders: image and text tokens interact inside the network, many layers deep. Fusion gives finer reasoning; separate encoders give speed. Both facts matter below.

    From pixels to visual tokens: region features

    Words come in a natural unit: the token. Images do not. In 2019, the best unit available was the object region, because of one very successful earlier paper: “bottom-up attention” (Anderson et al., 2018). It trained a Faster R-CNN detector (a two-stage object detector: a region proposal network suggests boxes, then a head classifies and refines each box) with a ResNet-101 backbone on Visual Genome, a dataset with about 1,600 object classes and attributes per box [Certain]. For every detected box, the detector also gives a 2048-dimensional feature vector: the average of the backbone’s feature map inside the box (RoI pooling). These features had already pushed VQA and captioning forward, so ViLBERT, LXMERT and UNITER all reused them.

    AnalogyA student reads a picture book with a friend who has already circled the important things on every page and written a short note next to each circle. The student never looks at the raw page, only at the circles and notes. That is fast to learn from, but if the friend missed something, the student cannot see it.

    So the image becomes a set of regions \(\{(\mathbf{f}_i, \mathbf{b}_i)\}_{i=1}^{K}\): a feature \(\mathbf{f}_i \in \R^{2048}\) and a box \(\mathbf{b}_i = (x_1, y_1, x_2, y_2)\) in pixels. How many regions? ViLBERT keeps boxes whose class confidence is above a threshold, but never fewer than 10 or more than 36 [Certain]. LXMERT always keeps exactly 36, so every image has the same length and no padding is wasted [Certain]. UNITER also uses Faster R-CNN features pretrained on Visual Genome, with a variable number of boxes [Certain].

    Where is the region? The location vector

    Words have an order, so BERT adds a position embedding. Regions have no order; what matters is where they are. So each model builds a small location vector from the box. ViLBERT uses 5 numbers: the box corners divided by the image size, and the fraction of the image the box covers [Certain]:

    \[ \mathbf{p}_i^{\text{ViLBERT}} = \left( \frac{x_1}{W},\ \frac{y_1}{H},\ \frac{x_2}{W},\ \frac{y_2}{H},\ \frac{(x_2 - x_1)(y_2 - y_1)}{W H} \right) \in \R^{5} \tag{172.1} \]

    UNITER uses 7 numbers: the 4 normalized corners, the normalized width and height, and their product (the area) [Certain]:

    \[ \mathbf{p}_i^{\text{UNITER}} = \left( \hat x_1,\ \hat y_1,\ \hat x_2,\ \hat y_2,\ \hat w,\ \hat h,\ \hat w \hat h \right), \qquad \hat w = \hat x_2 - \hat x_1,\ \ \hat h = \hat y_2 - \hat y_1 \tag{172.2} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(x_1, y_1, x_2, y_2\)
    the top-left and bottom-right corners of the box, in pixels
    \(W, H\)
    the image width and height in pixels
    \(\hat x_1, \hat y_1, \dots\)
    the same corners divided by \(W\) or \(H\), so they lie between 0 and 1
    \(\hat w, \hat h\)
    box width and height as a fraction of the image width and height

    One embedding per region

    The feature and the location live in different spaces (2048-d and 5-d or 7-d), so each gets its own linear layer into the model width \(d\), and the results are added. UNITER then applies layer normalization, and adds a modality embedding (like BERT’s segment embedding) so the model can tell an image token from a word [Certain]:

    \[ \mathbf{v}_i = \operatorname{LN}\!\big( W_f \mathbf{f}_i + W_p \mathbf{p}_i + \mathbf{e}_{\text{img}} \big) \in \R^{d} \tag{172.3} \]
    \[ \mathbf{t}_j = \operatorname{LN}\!\big( E_{\text{word}}[w_j] + E_{\text{pos}}[j] + \mathbf{e}_{\text{txt}} \big) \in \R^{d} \tag{172.4} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\mathbf{f}_i\)
    the 2048-d detector feature of region \(i\)
    \(W_f, W_p\)
    learned matrices of size \(d \times 2048\) and \(d \times 7\) (or \(d \times 5\))
    \(\mathbf{e}_{\text{img}}, \mathbf{e}_{\text{txt}}\)
    two learned vectors that mark “this is a region” or “this is a word”
    \(E_{\text{word}}, E_{\text{pos}}\)
    BERT’s word-piece and position embedding tables
    \(\operatorname{LN}\)
    layer normalization

    LXMERT does almost the same, but normalizes the two parts separately and averages them: \(\mathbf{v}_i = \tfrac12\big(\operatorname{LN}(W_F\mathbf{f}_i + \mathbf{b}_F) + \operatorname{LN}(W_P\mathbf{p}_i + \mathbf{b}_P)\big)\) [Certain]. ViLBERT also adds a special IMG token at the start of the region sequence: the mean of all region features, with the location of the whole image [Certain]. It plays the role that [CLS] plays for text.

    Worked example: one box, two location vectors

    An image is 640 × 480 pixels. The detector finds a horse with box \((160, 120, 480, 360)\). Normalized corners: \(160/640 = 0.25\), \(120/480 = 0.25\), \(480/640 = 0.75\), \(360/480 = 0.75\). The box is 320 × 240 pixels, so it covers \(76{,}800 / 307{,}200 = 0.25\) of the image.

    ViLBERT, formula (172.1): \((0.25, 0.25, 0.75, 0.75, 0.25)\). UNITER, formula (172.2): \(\hat w = 0.5\), \(\hat h = 0.5\), so \((0.25, 0.25, 0.75, 0.75, 0.5, 0.5, 0.25)\). Note that the area is the same in both, and that two boxes with the same size but different places differ only in the first four numbers.

    The caption “a man rides a horse on the beach” has 8 words. With 6 regions, a single-stream input has \(1\ (\text{IMG}) + 6 + 1\ (\text{CLS}) + 8 + 1\ (\text{SEP}) = 17\) tokens. Check it in Figure 1.

    Two streams: ViLBERT’s co-attention

    ViLBERT (Lu et al., 2019) starts from a simple argument: words and regions are different kinds of data. A word embedding is low-level and needs many layers of context. A region feature is already the output of a 101-layer CNN and a detector head, so it is high-level and needs less processing [Certain]. So ViLBERT keeps two transformers: a text stream initialized from BERT-base (12 layers, width 768, 12 heads) and a new visual stream (width 1024, 8 heads) [Certain]. The text stream runs several ordinary layers first; then the two streams meet in co-attentional transformer layers (Co-TRM), each followed by ordinary layers (TRM) in both streams. The best depth was 6 such Co-TRM→TRM blocks [Certain].

    A co-attention layer is ordinary multi-head attention with one change: each stream takes its queries from itself but its keys and values from the other stream.

    \[ \begin{aligned} H_W' &= H_W + \operatorname{MHA}\big(\underbrace{H_W W^Q_{w}}_{\text{queries: words}},\ \underbrace{H_V W^K_{w}}_{\text{keys: regions}},\ \underbrace{H_V W^V_{w}}_{\text{values: regions}}\big) \\ H_V' &= H_V + \operatorname{MHA}\big(H_V W^Q_{v},\ H_W W^K_{v},\ H_W W^V_{v}\big) \end{aligned} \tag{172.5} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(H_W \in \R^{T \times d_w}\)
    the hidden states of the \(T\) text tokens
    \(H_V \in \R^{K \times d_v}\)
    the hidden states of the \(K\) region tokens
    \(W^Q, W^K, W^V\)
    learned projection matrices; each stream has its own set
    \(\operatorname{MHA}\)
    multi-head attention with an output projection; after it come layer normalization and a feed-forward block, as in any transformer layer

    Read the first line as: “every word asks a question, and only the regions answer”. The result for word \(j\) is a mixture of region values: image-conditioned text. The second line is the reverse: text-conditioned regions. After a co-attention layer, each stream keeps its own length and its own width; only the content has been mixed.

    AnalogyTwo teams work in two rooms, one on the text and one on the picture. At fixed times, each team sends questions to the other room and gets answers back, then goes on working alone. They never sit at one table.

    LXMERT: three encoders

    LXMERT (Tan and Bansal, 2019) uses the same idea with a cleaner layout: a language encoder of 9 self-attention layers, an object-relationship encoder of 5 self-attention layers over the regions, and a cross-modality encoder of 5 layers, all with width 768 [Certain]. Inside each cross-modality layer, two cross-attention sub-layers run first (language to vision and vision to language), then a self-attention sub-layer in each stream, then a feed-forward sub-layer in each stream [Certain]. The authors count a single-modality layer as half a cross-modality layer: \((9 + 5)/2 + 5 = 12\), the depth of BERT-base [Certain]. Unlike ViLBERT, LXMERT trains everything from scratch; loading BERT weights made results worse in their tests [Certain].

    One stream: UNITER

    The single-stream answer is simpler. Embed the words with (172.4), embed the regions with (172.3), concatenate them into one sequence of length \(T + K\), and run a standard BERT encoder. Every layer is plain self-attention, so from the first layer every word can attend to every region and every region to every word. VisualBERT and VL-BERT did this in 2019 [Certain]; UNITER (Chen et al., 2020) did it with more data and better losses, in two sizes: UNITER-base (12 layers, width 768, 12 heads, 86M parameters) and UNITER-large (24 layers, width 1024, 16 heads, 303M parameters) [Certain].

    Watch the tokens talk: one stream versus two streams

    The two layouts differ in one place: which keys each query may read in each layer. Everything else is the same attention formula. To see what that changes, take a tiny example you can follow by hand: 4 words (man, rides, horse, beach) and 3 regions (man, horse, sand), each a 5-number vector. The first four numbers mean roughly “person, animal, place, action”. The fifth is the modality embedding: \(+m\) for words, \(-m\) for regions, like \(\mathbf{e}_{\text{txt}}\) and \(\mathbf{e}_{\text{img}}\) in (172.3) and (172.4). To keep the numbers readable, the toy uses no learned projections (\(W^Q = W^K = W^V = I\)), no feed-forward block, and averages the residual with \(\tfrac12\) instead of layer normalization. One toy layer is then:

    \[ \mathbf{h}_i' = \tfrac12\Big(\mathbf{h}_i + \sum_{j \in J(i)} a_{ij}\, \mathbf{h}_j\Big), \qquad a_{ij} = \frac{\exp\!\big(\mathbf{h}_i^\top \mathbf{h}_j / \sqrt{d}\big)}{\sum_{j' \in J(i)} \exp\!\big(\mathbf{h}_i^\top \mathbf{h}_{j'} / \sqrt{d}\big)} \tag{172.5a} \]

    The set \(J(i)\) is the whole difference. Single-stream layer: \(J(i)\) is all \(T + K\) tokens. Co-TRM: \(J(i)\) is the other modality only. TRM: \(J(i)\) is the same modality only. To measure how much image information a token carries, follow its image share \(\rho_i\): it starts at 0 for a word and 1 for a region, and mixes with the same weights:

    \[ \rho_i' = \tfrac12\Big(\rho_i + \sum_{j \in J(i)} a_{ij}\, \rho_j\Big), \qquad \rho_i^{(0)} = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{word} \\ 1 & \text{region} \end{cases} \tag{172.5b} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\mathbf{h}_i\)
    the state of token \(i\) before the layer; \(\mathbf{h}_i'\) after it. All tokens are updated at the same time from the old states
    \(J(i)\)
    the keys token \(i\) may attend to in this layer
    \(a_{ij}\)
    attention weight from query \(i\) to key \(j\); the weights over \(J(i)\) sum to 1
    \(d\)
    the vector size, 5 here, so the scores are divided by \(\sqrt{5} \approx 2.236\)
    \(\rho_i\)
    the fraction of token \(i\)’s state that came from region inputs

    Is two-stream cheaper? Count the attention scores

    A common belief is that two streams save compute. Count the attention scores (the entries of \(QK^\top\)) per layer. A single-stream layer over \(T\) words and \(K\) regions computes \((T + K)^2\) scores. A ViLBERT Co-TRM layer computes \(TK\) word-to-region scores plus \(KT\) region-to-word scores. The TRM layer after it computes \(T^2\) word-to-word plus \(K^2\) region-to-region scores. Add them:

    \[ \underbrace{(T + K)^2}_{\text{one single-stream layer}} \;=\; \underbrace{T^2 + K^2}_{\text{TRM: within each stream}} \;+\; \underbrace{2TK}_{\text{Co-TRM: across streams}} \tag{172.6} \]

    So one Co-TRM→TRM pair computes exactly as many scores as one single-stream layer; the two-stream model just splits the big attention matrix into four blocks and computes them in two steps. The same holds for one LXMERT cross-modality layer. The real differences are elsewhere: two streams have separate weights for each modality (so more parameters: ViLBERT-style models are larger), they can use different widths and depths per modality, and the modalities meet only at chosen layers instead of in every layer.

    What did the experiments say? ViLBERT’s own paper trained a single-stream baseline on the same data and features, and it lost on every task, for example 68.85 versus 70.55 on VQA test-dev [Certain]. But UNITER-base, a single-stream model with more and cleaner data and better losses, reached 72.70 on VQA test-dev [Certain]. A later controlled study (Bugliarello et al., 2021) re-trained several of these models with the same data and hyper-parameters and found that most of the reported differences came from training data and hyper-parameters, not from one-stream versus two-stream [Certain]. The honest summary: the fusion layout matters less than the data and the losses.

    Pretraining tasks

    All three models are pretrained on image–caption pairs with a mix of self-supervised tasks, then fine-tuned. The tasks are BERT’s tasks, extended to two modalities.

    Masked language modeling with the image (MLM)

    Mask about 15% of the words (80% become [MASK], 10% a random word, 10% unchanged, as in BERT) and predict them from the other words and all regions [Certain]:

    \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{MLM}} = -\,\E_{(w, v)} \sum_{j \in \mathcal{M}} \log P_\theta\big(w_j \mid w_{\setminus \mathcal{M}},\, v\big) \tag{172.7} \]
    Read the symbols
    \((w, v)\)
    one caption (words) and its image (regions)
    \(\mathcal{M}\)
    the set of masked word positions
    \(w_{\setminus \mathcal{M}}\)
    the words that are not masked

    If the caption is “a man rides a [MASK] on the beach”, the text alone allows horse, bike or camel. The region of a horse makes “horse” likely. This is how the model is forced to look at the image.

    Masked region modeling (MRM)

    Mask about 15% of the regions by setting their features to zero, and ask the model to recover them from the other regions and the words [Certain]. A region is a continuous 2048-d vector, not a word from a vocabulary, so “recover” needs a definition. UNITER tested three [Certain]:

    \[ \text{MRFR:}\quad \mathcal{L} = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{M}_v} \big\lVert g_\theta(\mathbf{h}_i) - \mathbf{f}_i \big\rVert_2^2 \tag{172.8} \]
    \[ \text{MRC:}\quad \mathcal{L} = -\sum_{i \in \mathcal{M}_v} \log q_\theta(c_i^{*} \mid \mathbf{h}_i), \qquad c_i^{*} = \argmax_c\, \tilde{\mathbf{c}}_i[c] \tag{172.9} \]
    \[ \text{MRC-kl:}\quad \mathcal{L} = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{M}_v} \KL\big(\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_i \,\big\|\, q_\theta(\cdot \mid \mathbf{h}_i)\big) \tag{172.10} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\mathcal{M}_v\)
    the set of masked regions
    \(\mathbf{h}_i\)
    the model’s output vector at masked region \(i\)
    \(g_\theta\)
    a linear layer back to 2048 dimensions; MRFR regresses the original feature \(\mathbf{f}_i\)
    \(\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_i\)
    the detector’s class distribution for that box (for example 1,600 Visual Genome classes)
    \(q_\theta\)
    the model’s predicted class distribution (a linear layer and softmax)
    \(c_i^{*}\)
    the detector’s top class, used as a hard label in MRC

    ViLBERT uses only the KL version (it zeroes masked region features 90% of the time and leaves them unchanged 10%) and argues that regression would be hard to balance against the text loss [Certain]. LXMERT uses both regression and detected-label classification [Certain]. Note what all three variants do: the target comes from the detector. The model learns to agree with Faster R-CNN, not with the real image.

    Worked example: MRC versus MRC-kl on one region

    The detector is unsure about a masked box: \(\tilde{\mathbf{c}} = (0.7, 0.2, 0.1)\) over (horse, cow, dog). The model predicts \(q = (0.5, 0.3, 0.2)\).

    MRC uses the hard label “horse”: loss \(-\ln 0.5 = 0.693\). MRC-kl compares full distributions: \(\KL(\tilde{\mathbf{c}} \,\|\, q) = 0.7\ln\tfrac{0.7}{0.5} + 0.2\ln\tfrac{0.2}{0.3} + 0.1\ln\tfrac{0.1}{0.2} = 0.085\). The KL is small because the model already has the right shape; it would be 0 only if \(q = \tilde{\mathbf{c}}\). MRC would keep pushing \(q(\text{horse})\) toward 1, even though the detector itself gives “cow” 20%. In UNITER’s ablation, MRC-kl gave a higher total score than MRC (397.09 vs 393.97) [Certain].

    Why does MRC keep pushing? Look at the gradient. For a softmax output \(q = \softmax(\mathbf{z})\), the gradient of the cross-entropy against any target distribution \(\mathbf{t}\) with respect to the logits \(\mathbf{z}\) is \(q - \mathbf{t}\). MRC uses a one-hot target, MRC-kl the detector’s distribution (the KL and the cross-entropy differ only by a constant, the entropy of \(\tilde{\mathbf{c}}\)):

    \[ \nabla_{\mathbf{z}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{MRC}} = q - \mathbf{e}_{c^*}, \qquad \nabla_{\mathbf{z}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{MRC-kl}} = q - \tilde{\mathbf{c}}, \qquad \mathbf{z} \leftarrow \mathbf{z} - \eta\, \nabla_{\mathbf{z}} \mathcal{L} \tag{172.10a} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\mathbf{z}\)
    the logits of the region-classification head at one masked region
    \(\mathbf{e}_{c^*}\)
    the one-hot vector of the detector’s top class
    \(\eta\)
    the step size (learning rate)

    The MRC gradient is zero only when \(q\) is one-hot, which needs infinite logits, so it never stops. The MRC-kl gradient is zero exactly when \(q = \tilde{\mathbf{c}}\). Figure 5 runs both losses on the worked example, step by step.

    Image–text matching (ITM)

    Give the model a caption and an image, and ask one yes/no question: do they belong together? Half the time (LXMERT: probability 0.5) the caption or the image is replaced with one from another pair [Certain]. UNITER puts a linear layer and a sigmoid on the [CLS] output [Certain]; ViLBERT multiplies the IMG and CLS outputs element by element first [Certain]:

    \[ s_\theta(w, v) = \sigma\big(\mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{h}_{\text{CLS}}\big), \qquad \mathcal{L}_{\text{ITM}} = -\,\E\big[\, y \log s_\theta + (1 - y) \log(1 - s_\theta) \,\big] \tag{172.11} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\mathbf{h}_{\text{CLS}}\)
    the output at the [CLS] token, a summary of the whole pair (ViLBERT uses \(\mathbf{h}_{\text{IMG}} \odot \mathbf{h}_{\text{CLS}}\))
    \(\mathbf{u}\)
    a learned vector; \(\sigma\) is the sigmoid
    \(y\)
    1 if the pair is real, 0 if one side was swapped

    This is a binary cross-entropy, like SigLIP’s loss (session 167), with one big difference: here the score comes out of a deep fusion network that sees both inputs together, so it must be recomputed for every pair. Random negatives are easy (a beach caption with a kitchen image), so the task becomes easy quickly. ALBEF later fixed this with hard negatives chosen by a contrastive model (session 173).

    Extra tasks: image QA and word–region alignment

    LXMERT adds image question answering to pretraining: about one third of its sentences are questions from VQA v2, GQA and Visual Genome QA, and the model predicts the answer from a table of 9,500 candidates [Certain]. UNITER adds word–region alignment (WRA) with optimal transport: it finds a soft matching \(\mathbf{T}\) between the \(T\) words and \(K\) regions that moves all “mass” from words to regions at the lowest total cosine distance, and uses that cost as a loss [Certain]:

    \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{WRA}} = \min_{\mathbf{T} \in \Pi(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b})} \sum_{j=1}^{T} \sum_{i=1}^{K} T_{ji}\, \big(1 - \cos(\mathbf{h}_{w_j}, \mathbf{h}_{v_i})\big) \tag{172.12} \]

    Here \(\Pi(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b})\) is the set of non-negative \(T \times K\) matrices whose rows sum to the word weights \(\mathbf{a}\) and columns to the region weights \(\mathbf{b}\). UNITER solves it approximately with a few matrix-vector iterations (the IPOT algorithm) [Certain]. In words: each word should have some region close to it, and each region some word.

    Conditional masking

    ViLBERT and LXMERT mask words and regions at the same time. That creates a problem. If “horse” is masked and the horse region is also masked, there is no information left to recover either one; the model is asked to guess. UNITER masks one modality at a time: an MLM batch masks only words, an MRM batch masks only regions [Certain]. It also samples one task per mini-batch, so each update uses one loss [Certain]. Turning conditional masking off lowered UNITER’s total score from 399.97 to 396.51 [Certain]. Figure 6 shows how often the clash happens.

    Which tasks helped? The UNITER ablation

    UNITER pretrained a base model with many task mixes and fine-tuned each one on four benchmarks: VQA, Flickr30K image retrieval (IR) and text retrieval (TR), each the average of R@1, R@5 and R@10, NLVR2 (does a sentence describe a pair of images?) and RefCOCO+ with detected boxes (find the box a phrase refers to). The sum of these five scores is called Meta-Sum [Certain]. Figure 7 shows the table.

And ViLBERT showed that more pretraining data helps steadily: with 0%, 25%, 50% and 100% of Conceptual Captions, VQA test-dev went 68.93, 69.82, 70.30, 70.55, and zero-shot Flickr30K image retrieval R@1 went 0, 20.40, 26.76, 31.86 [Certain].

    The PyTorch version of the core pieces, with toy sizes. It builds the embeddings (172.3) and (172.4), a ViLBERT co-attention layer (172.5), a UNITER-style single stream, and the losses (172.7), (172.8), (172.10) and (172.11) with conditional masking and one task per mini-batch.

    Algorithm 1: turning one image and caption into model inputs
    1. input: image, caption, frozen detector (Faster R-CNN, Visual Genome classes), \(K_{\min}, K_{\max}\), threshold \(\tau\)
    2. run the detector once; get boxes \(\mathbf{b}_i\), class distributions \(\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_i\), RoI features \(\mathbf{f}_i \in \R^{2048}\) // about 0.8 s per image, cached on disk before training
    3. keep boxes with top confidence \(\ge \tau\); if fewer than \(K_{\min}\), take the top \(K_{\min}\); if more than \(K_{\max}\), keep the top \(K_{\max}\)
    4. \(\mathbf{p}_i \leftarrow\) location vector, formula (172.1) or (172.2)
    5. \(\mathbf{v}_i \leftarrow \operatorname{LN}(W_f\mathbf{f}_i + W_p\mathbf{p}_i + \mathbf{e}_{\text{img}})\) // formula (172.3)
    6. tokenize the caption into word pieces; \(\mathbf{t}_j \leftarrow \operatorname{LN}(E_{\text{word}}[w_j] + E_{\text{pos}}[j] + \mathbf{e}_{\text{txt}})\) // formula (172.4)
    7. single stream: return \([\mathbf{t}_{\text{CLS}}, \mathbf{t}_1, \dots, \mathbf{t}_T, \mathbf{t}_{\text{SEP}}, \mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_K]\); two streams: return the two lists separately, with IMG first in the region list
    Algorithm 2: one UNITER pretraining step
    1. input: batch of (caption, regions, detector distributions), task list {MLM, MRFR, MRC-kl, ITM, WRA}
    2. task \(\leftarrow\) sample one task for this mini-batch
    3. if MLM: mask 15% of words only; loss \(\leftarrow\) cross-entropy at masked words, formula (172.7)
    4. if MRFR or MRC-kl: zero 15% of region features only; loss \(\leftarrow\) formula (172.8) or (172.10) at masked regions
    5. if ITM: with probability 0.5 replace the caption (or image) by one from another pair; loss \(\leftarrow\) binary cross-entropy on [CLS], formula (172.11)
    6. if WRA: compute the transport plan between word and region outputs (IPOT); loss \(\leftarrow\) formula (172.12)
    7. backward, AdamW step // one objective per update
    import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F
torch.manual_seed(0)
V, C, FEAT, D, K, T = 100, 10, 64, 32, 6, 8     # vocab, detector classes, RoI dim, width, regions, words
MASK, CLS, SEP = 1, 2, 3

class Embed(nn.Module):                          # formulas (172.3) and (172.4)
    def __init__(s):
        super().__init__()
        s.feat, s.loc = nn.Linear(FEAT, D), nn.Linear(7, D)
        s.word, s.pos, s.mod = nn.Embedding(V, D), nn.Embedding(64, D), nn.Embedding(2, D)
        s.ln_v, s.ln_w = nn.LayerNorm(D), nn.LayerNorm(D)
    def forward(s, f, loc, w):
        v = s.ln_v(s.feat(f) + s.loc(loc) + s.mod.weight[1])            # regions: no order, only location
        pos = torch.arange(w.shape[1])
        t = s.ln_w(s.word(w) + s.pos(pos) + s.mod.weight[0])            # words: position embedding
        return t, v

class CoAttention(nn.Module):                    # ViLBERT co-TRM, formula (172.5)
    def __init__(s):
        super().__init__()
        s.a_w = nn.MultiheadAttention(D, 4, batch_first=True)
        s.a_v = nn.MultiheadAttention(D, 4, batch_first=True)
    def forward(s, t, v):
        t2 = t + s.a_w(t, v, v, need_weights=False)[0]                  # words ask, regions answer
        v2 = v + s.a_v(v, t, t, need_weights=False)[0]                  # regions ask, words answer
        return t2, v2

class SingleStream(nn.Module):                   # UNITER-style: one sequence, one encoder
    def __init__(s):
        super().__init__()
        s.emb = Embed()
        layer = nn.TransformerEncoderLayer(D, 4, 4 * D, dropout=0.0, batch_first=True)
        s.enc = nn.TransformerEncoder(layer, 2)
        s.mlm, s.mrfr, s.mrc, s.itm = nn.Linear(D, V), nn.Linear(D, FEAT), nn.Linear(D, C), nn.Linear(D, 1)
    def forward(s, f, loc, w):
        t, v = s.emb(f, loc, w)
        h = s.enc(torch.cat([t, v], dim=1))                             # (B, T + K, D)
        return h[:, :w.shape[1]], h[:, w.shape[1]:]

def batch(B=4):                                  # fake detector output + captions
    f = torch.randn(B, K, FEAT).relu()
    xy = torch.rand(B, K, 2) * 0.5; wh = torch.rand(B, K, 2) * 0.5
    loc = torch.cat([xy, xy + wh, wh, wh[..., :1] * wh[..., 1:]], -1)  # 7-d, formula (172.2)
    det = F.softmax(torch.randn(B, K, C) * 2, -1)                      # detector class distribution
    w = torch.randint(4, V, (B, T)); w[:, 0] = CLS; w[:, -1] = SEP
    return f, loc, det, w

def step(model, task, f, loc, det, w, p=0.15):
    B = w.shape[0]
    if task == "mlm":                            # mask words only (conditional masking)
        m = (torch.rand(B, T) < p); m[:, [0, -1]] = False; m[0, 1] = True
        ht, _ = model(f, loc, w.masked_fill(m, MASK))
        return F.cross_entropy(model.mlm(ht[m]), w[m])                  # formula (172.7)
    if task in ("mrfr", "mrc_kl"):               # mask regions only
        m = (torch.rand(B, K) < p); m[0, 0] = True
        _, hv = model(f.masked_fill(m[..., None], 0.0), loc, w)
        if task == "mrfr":
            return ((model.mrfr(hv[m]) - f[m]) ** 2).sum(-1).mean()     # formula (172.8)
        return F.kl_div(F.log_softmax(model.mrc(hv[m]), -1), det[m], reduction="batchmean")  # (172.10)
    y = (torch.rand(B) < 0.5).float()            # ITM: half the captions are swapped
    w_in = torch.where(y[:, None].bool(), w, w.roll(1, dims=0))
    ht, _ = model(f, loc, w_in)
    return F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(model.itm(ht[:, 0]).squeeze(-1), y)  # formula (172.11)

model = SingleStream()
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
tasks = ["mlm", "mrfr", "mrc_kl", "itm"]
for it in range(8):                              # one task per mini-batch, as in UNITER
    task = tasks[it % 4]
    loss = step(model, task, *batch())
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    print(f"step {it} {task:7s} loss {loss.item():.3f}")

co = CoAttention()
t, v = model.emb(*batch()[:2], batch()[3])
t2, v2 = co(t, v)
print(tuple(t2.shape), tuple(v2.shape))          # (4, 8, 32) (4, 6, 32): each stream keeps its length

    The script is deterministic (same output on every run) and runs in about a second on a CPU. The losses are on random data, so they do not go down in 8 steps; the point is the shapes and which positions each loss reads. Two details to notice: the MLM branch never masks regions and the region branches never mask words (conditional masking), and the ITM negatives come from rolling the batch, so each wrong caption belongs to another image in the same batch.

    Why they faded

    By 2022, almost no new model used this recipe. Four reasons, each with numbers.

    1. The detector is slow. ViLT (Kim et al., 2021) timed the visual input step on one GPU (a P40) with 800 × 1,333 images: the ResNet-101 backbone takes about 75 ms, and the region steps (proposals, RoI Align, non-maximum suppression and RoI heads) about 810 ms, so about 900 ms per image before the transformer even starts; the transformer itself takes about 15 ms [Certain]. Per-class non-maximum suppression over 1,600 classes alone takes more than 500 ms [Certain]. During pretraining you can cache the features on disk, but at inference every new image pays the full cost. ViLT replaced the detector with a linear patch embedding (about 0.4 ms) and reached similar accuracy at about 15 ms in total [Certain].

    2. The data is small and hand-made. The biggest pretraining sets here have about 9–10M pairs, mostly from COCO and Visual Genome, whose images also appear in the test benchmarks. CLIP trained on 400M web pairs [Certain], about 42 times UNITER’s full set. Region features also tie you to the detector’s 1,600 classes: things outside that list are hard to represent.

    3. Retrieval is quadratic. A fusion encoder needs one full forward pass for each (image, text) pair:

    \[ \text{passes}_{\text{fusion}} = N_I \cdot N_T, \qquad \text{passes}_{\text{dual encoder}} = N_I + N_T \tag{172.13} \]

    On the Flickr30K test set (1,000 images, 5,000 captions), that is 5,000,000 passes against 6,000 for a CLIP-style model: about 833 times more. On a web-scale index it is impossible. ViLBERT already cached the text stream before the first co-attention layer to save time [Certain], but each pair still needs its own fusion layers.

    4. No generation. These are bidirectional encoders. They classify (pick an answer from 3,129 or 9,500 candidates), score, and point at boxes, but they do not write a sentence. Modern VLMs answer open questions in free text with a decoder LLM (session 177).

    The ideas that survived: MLM and ITM as losses, ITM as a reranker on top of a fast dual encoder (ALBEF, BLIP), cross-attention as a fusion layer (Flamingo, the BLIP-2 Q-Former), and the lesson that in-domain data and careful masking matter. The ideas that did not: frozen detector features as the image input. A middle step worth knowing: Oscar (2020) added the detector’s object tags as extra words, and VinVL (2021) trained a much better detector; VinVL-base reached 75.95 on VQA test-dev [Certain], the peak of the region era, just as CLIP and ViLT appeared.

    Common mistakes

    • Calling CLIP a “two-stream model like ViLBERT”. CLIP’s streams never interact before the final dot product (a dual encoder). ViLBERT’s streams interact in 6 co-attention layers (a fusion encoder). The costs and abilities are very different.
    • Thinking two streams save attention compute. One Co-TRM→TRM pair computes \(T^2 + K^2 + 2TK = (T + K)^2\) scores, the same as one single-stream layer (172.6). Two streams cost more parameters, not fewer scores.
    • Ignoring the detector in latency estimates. The transformer is about 15 ms; the region step is about 900 ms. Caching features hides this during training, not in a product.
    • Masking a word and its region together and then wondering why the masked-region loss is noisy. Use conditional masking: one modality per batch.
    • Regressing raw 2048-d features with an unscaled L2 loss next to a cross-entropy loss. The regression term can be orders of magnitude larger (in the toy code above it is around 50 while the others are around 1); weight or normalize it, or use the KL target.
    • Easy ITM negatives only. A random caption is almost always obviously wrong, so the ITM loss falls quickly and teaches little. Mine hard negatives from a contrastive model (ALBEF).
    • Comparing papers that used different data. LXMERT, UNITER and ViLBERT were trained on different sets; a controlled re-training showed most gaps come from data and hyper-parameters (Bugliarello et al.).
    • Test images inside pretraining data. COCO and Visual Genome images appear in many benchmarks. UNITER removed downstream validation and test images (and Flickr30K overlaps) from pretraining; do the same.

    Interview questions

    What is the difference between a single-stream and a two-stream vision-language model?basic
    A single-stream model (VisualBERT, VL-BERT, UNITER) concatenates word tokens and region tokens into one sequence and runs one transformer, so every layer mixes both modalities through ordinary self-attention. A two-stream model (ViLBERT, LXMERT) keeps one transformer per modality, with its own weights and sizes, and lets them exchange information only in co-attention or cross-attention layers, where queries come from one modality and keys and values from the other. Both are fusion encoders; neither is a CLIP-style dual encoder.
    How does an image become a sequence of tokens in ViLBERT or UNITER?basic
    A frozen Faster R-CNN (ResNet-101, trained on Visual Genome) detects boxes. ViLBERT keeps 10 to 36 boxes above a confidence threshold, LXMERT exactly 36. Each box gives a 2048-d RoI feature and a location vector (5-d in ViLBERT: normalized corners and area fraction; 7-d in UNITER: corners, width, height, area). Two linear layers map both to the model width, they are summed and layer-normalized, plus a modality embedding. ViLBERT adds an IMG token with the mean feature of all regions.
    Write the co-attention layer. How does it differ from self-attention and from a decoder’s cross-attention?core
    Text update: \(H_W' = H_W + \operatorname{MHA}(H_W W^Q, H_V W^K, H_V W^V)\); region update: the same with the roles swapped. Self-attention takes Q, K and V from the same sequence. A decoder’s cross-attention (or Flamingo’s) is one-directional: text queries read image keys and values, and the image side is not updated. Co-attention is bidirectional and symmetric: both streams are updated in the same layer, each with its own weights.
    Is a two-stream model cheaper than a single-stream one?core
    Not in attention scores. For \(T\) words and \(K\) regions, a single-stream layer computes \((T+K)^2\). A Co-TRM layer computes \(2TK\), and the TRM layer after it \(T^2 + K^2\); the sum is the same. With \(T = 20\), \(K = 36\): 3,136 either way. Two streams have separate weights per modality, so they usually have more parameters (ViLT’s table, which counts the visual embedder too, lists about 274M for ViLBERT and about 155M for UNITER-base), but they can use a narrower or shallower stream for one modality.
    Explain the three masked region modeling variants. Why not just regress the feature?core
    MRFR regresses the zeroed 2048-d RoI feature with L2. MRC predicts the detector’s top class with cross-entropy. MRC-kl matches the detector’s full class distribution with KL divergence. Regression targets are continuous and high-dimensional, much of their detail cannot be inferred from language, and the L2 loss has a different scale from the text cross-entropy, so it is hard to balance (ViLBERT’s argument). MRC-kl keeps the detector’s uncertainty. In UNITER’s ablation, combining MRC-kl and MRFR worked best.
    What is image–text matching, how are negatives made, and what is its weakness?core
    A binary classifier on the fused summary ([CLS], or \(\mathbf{h}_{\text{IMG}} \odot \mathbf{h}_{\text{CLS}}\) in ViLBERT) predicts whether the caption matches the image, trained with binary cross-entropy. Negatives replace the caption or the image with one from another pair, with probability 0.5 in LXMERT. Weakness: random negatives are too easy, so the model can solve the task from coarse topic cues. ALBEF fixed this by sampling hard negatives using contrastive similarity.
    What is conditional masking and why does it help?core
    Mask only one modality per training example (words for MLM, regions for MRM) instead of both at once. With joint masking, a word and the region it describes can both be hidden, so neither can be recovered and the gradient is noise. At \(p = 0.15\) and three linked pairs, this happens in \(1 - (1 - 0.15^2)^3 \approx 6.6\%\) of captions. UNITER’s ablation: removing conditional masking lowered Meta-Sum from 399.97 to 396.51.
    Why can CLIP search a billion images but UNITER cannot?core
    CLIP encodes each image once and each query once, and compares vectors with a dot product, so it costs \(N_I + N_T\) encoder passes plus a fast nearest-neighbor search. UNITER’s score depends on both inputs inside the network, so it needs one forward pass per pair: \(N_I \cdot N_T\). For Flickr30K’s 1,000 images and 5,000 captions, that is 5,000,000 passes versus 6,000. The practical fix is two stages: dual encoder for the top-k, then a fusion head to rerank them (ALBEF, BLIP).
    Give four reasons region-based VLP models faded.basic
    (1) Detector latency: about 810 ms of region work plus 75 ms of backbone per image in ViLT’s measurement, versus about 15 ms for the transformer. (2) Small, curated data: under 10M pairs, versus 400M for CLIP, and a closed set of about 1,600 detector classes. (3) Fusion scoring is quadratic for retrieval. (4) Bidirectional encoders do not generate text, so open-ended answers and captions need another model. Also, the detector is frozen, so the visual features never improve with the language task.
    ViLBERT’s paper found two streams better than one. UNITER, a single stream, beat ViLBERT. How do you reconcile this?advanced
    ViLBERT compared against its own single-stream baseline with the same data (Conceptual Captions) and losses, and won (VQA 70.55 vs 68.85). UNITER changed many things at once: more and in-domain data (COCO and Visual Genome, about 9.6M pairs with CC and SBU), MRFR + MRC-kl + WRA, conditional masking. Bugliarello et al. re-trained several models in one controlled framework and found that data and hyper-parameters explain most reported gaps; the layout itself mattered little. Lesson: compare architectures only under the same data and budget.
    Why did LXMERT not initialize its language encoder from BERT, while ViLBERT did?advanced
    LXMERT’s authors tried loading BERT weights and got worse results. Their explanation: BERT already performs well on the text side, so the model can solve masked language modeling without learning the cross-modal connections. Training from scratch on 9.18M image–sentence pairs, about one third of them questions, forced it to use the image. ViLBERT kept BERT-base because it trained on noisier web captions and wanted BERT’s language knowledge. Both choices are reasonable; they depend on the data.
    Which ideas from this era are still in modern VLMs?advanced
    MLM conditioned on the image (ALBEF, BLIP), ITM as a loss and as a reranker (ALBEF, BLIP, BLIP-2’s Q-Former), cross-attention as a fusion layer (Flamingo, Llama 3.2 Vision-style adapters, the Q-Former), and the single-stream idea of putting visual tokens directly in the sequence (LLaVA and most decoder VLMs). The detector input, fixed answer vocabularies and encoder-only design were replaced by ViT patches, web-scale data and decoder LLMs.

    Key takeaways

    • First-generation VLMs turn an image into 10–36 detector regions: a 2048-d RoI feature plus a 5-d or 7-d location vector (172.1, 172.2), embedded like words (172.3, 172.4).
    • Two streams (ViLBERT, LXMERT) meet through co-attention (172.5); one stream (UNITER) concatenates everything. Both compute the same attention scores per layer pair (172.6); the layout matters less than data and losses.
    • Pretraining: masked words with the image (172.7), masked regions with detector targets (172.8–172.10), image–text matching (172.11), plus image QA (LXMERT) and word–region alignment (172.12). Conditional masking avoids hiding a word and its region together.
    • They faded because the detector costs about 900 ms per image, the data was under 10M pairs, fusion retrieval needs \(N_I N_T\) passes (172.13), and encoders cannot generate text.
    • MLM, ITM reranking and cross-attention fusion live on in ALBEF, BLIP, BLIP-2 and Flamingo-style VLMs.

    Key formulas

    \[ \mathbf{v}_i = \operatorname{LN}(W_f \mathbf{f}_i + W_p \mathbf{p}_i + \mathbf{e}_{\text{img}}), \quad H_W' = H_W + \operatorname{MHA}(H_W W^Q, H_V W^K, H_V W^V) \]
    \[ (T+K)^2 = T^2 + K^2 + 2TK, \quad \mathcal{L}_{\text{ITM}} = -\E[y \log s + (1-y)\log(1-s)], \quad \text{passes} = N_I N_T \text{ vs } N_I + N_T \]
