    In this lab you run CLIP, BLIP and BLIP-2 on the same 500 COCO photos and measure them on retrieval, captioning and visual question answering. You build two-stage retrieval with a contrastive shortlist and a matching reranker, score captions with CIDEr and with a VLM judge asked in both orders, and score answers with the VQA metric and a blind baseline. Then you explain every failure by its architecture, its loss or its data.

    Why this matters

    You have met three famous model families in this track. Session 166 learns one vector per image and one per sentence. Session 173 adds a matching head that reads image and text together, and a decoder that writes captions. Session 181 and session 178 showed BLIP-2, which plugs a frozen image encoder into a frozen large language model (LLM) through a small Q-Former. In this lab you run all three on the same 500 photos and measure them on three tasks: retrieval (find the right photo for a sentence and the right sentence for a photo), captioning (write a sentence for a photo) and visual question answering (VQA: answer a question about a photo).

    The goal is not a leaderboard. The goal is to learn to read results the way a senior engineer does: every number comes with a reason in the architecture (what can talk to what inside the model), the loss (what the model was rewarded for) and the data (what it has already seen). You will see a dual encoder fail at VQA below a blind baseline, two models score above a held-out human caption on CIDEr, a small judge that mostly picks a position instead of a caption, and a VQA score that is high partly because the model was trained on the very questions we test. Every number on this page comes from our own run of the code below, unless it is tagged as coming from a paper.

    The short version of what we measured: for retrieval, the matching heads of BLIP and BLIP-2 lift text-to-image recall@1 from 0.591 (CLIP) to about 0.86. For captioning, CLIP can only copy a caption of another photo (CIDEr 61.8), BLIP writes short COCO-style captions (97.0), and BLIP-2, never fine-tuned on COCO, writes longer ones and gets the best score, 140.8. For VQA, CLIP scores 4.2%, a blind “most common answer” rule 32.2%, BLIP-2 zero-shot 55.2% and BLIP, which was fine-tuned on these questions, 83.0%.

    Interview tipA common question is “Which model would you use for image search, for alt-text, and for a visual assistant, and why?” A strong answer names the architecture limit first (a dual encoder cannot generate; a matching head is too slow to scan a whole gallery; a frozen LLM brings knowledge and fluency but follows its own style), then the evaluation you would run, then the data risks (was the test set in training?).

    AnalogyThink of three job candidates. CLIP is a librarian with a perfect card catalogue: very fast at matching, but she can only hand you an existing card, never write a new one. BLIP is a specialist trained in-house on this exact company’s files: excellent on the familiar format, but each skill is a separate person. BLIP-2 is a well-read generalist (the LLM) wearing borrowed glasses (the frozen image encoder), with a short note-taker (the Q-Former) in between: broad and fluent, but he answers in his own style.

    Step 1: retrieval with ITC, then ITM

    All three families can score an image–text pair in a cheap way. Image and text are encoded separately into unit vectors (vectors of length 1), and the score is their dot product, the cosine. This is the image–text contrastive (ITC) score. Because the two encoders never see each other, you can embed a whole gallery once, offline, and search it with one matrix product. CLIP and BLIP use one vector per image. BLIP-2’s Q-Former gives 32 vectors per image (one per learned query) and keeps the best one:

    \[ s_{\text{ITC}}(I, T) = \mathbf{u}(I)^\top \mathbf{t}(T) \quad \text{(CLIP, BLIP)}, \qquad s_{\text{ITC}}(I, T) = \max_{q = 1, \dots, 32} \; \mathbf{z}_q(I)^\top \mathbf{t}(T) \quad \text{(BLIP-2)} \tag{176.1} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\mathbf{u}(I)\)
    unit image vector: CLIP’s projected image embedding (512 numbers), or BLIP’s projected [CLS] token of the image encoder (256 numbers)
    \(\mathbf{t}(T)\)
    unit text vector from the text encoder’s [CLS] (or end-of-text) token, projected to the same size
    \(\mathbf{z}_q(I)\)
    the output of query \(q\) of BLIP-2’s Q-Former, projected to 256 numbers and normalized; the BLIP-2 paper takes “the highest one as the image-text similarity” [Certain]

    BLIP and BLIP-2 also have an expensive score. The image–text matching (ITM) head reads the text with cross-attention to the image tokens (each text token can look at every image patch) and outputs a binary logit: “is this a match?”. It sees the words and the image together, so it can check details that one pooled vector loses. But it needs a full forward pass for every pair: a gallery of a million images would need a million passes per query. The standard answer, used in both papers, is a two-stage search: ITC picks the top \(k\) candidates, ITM reranks only those [Certain]. In the authors’ evaluation code the final score of a candidate is the ITM logit plus the ITC cosine [Certain]:

    \[ \text{score}(I, T) = \begin{cases} \ell_{\text{ITM}}(I, T) + s_{\text{ITC}}(I, T) & \text{if } I \text{ is in the ITC top-}k \text{ for } T \\ \text{ITC order, after all reranked candidates} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \tag{176.2} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\ell_{\text{ITM}}(I, T)\)
    the “match” logit of the ITM head; BLIP reads it from the [CLS] position, BLIP-2 averages it over the 32 query outputs
    \(k\)
    the rerank depth: how many ITC candidates get the expensive pass; the papers use \(k = 256\) (BLIP on COCO) and \(k = 128\) (BLIP-2); we use 16
    Algorithm 1: two-stage retrieval (ITC shortlist, ITM rerank)
    1. offline: for every gallery image \(I\): keep its unit ITC vector(s) and its image tokens // ITM needs the tokens, not only the vector
    2. for each query text \(T\):
    3.   \(\mathbf{t} \leftarrow\) unit text vector; \(s_I \leftarrow s_{\text{ITC}}(I, T)\) for all \(I\) // one matrix product, formula (176.1)
    4.   \(C \leftarrow\) the \(k\) images with the largest \(s_I\)
    5.   for \(I \in C\): \(\text{score}_I \leftarrow \ell_{\text{ITM}}(I, T) + s_I\) // k cross-attention passes, batched
    6.   return \(C\) sorted by score, then the rest in ITC order // formula (176.2)
    7. image → text is the same with the roles swapped (top \(k\) captions per image)

    The code below is a compact version of our ret.py (which batches the same calls). Note what each model needs to keep: for BLIP the 577 image tokens of 768 numbers, for BLIP-2 the 257 frozen ViT-g tokens of 1,408 numbers per image. The ITC vector alone is not enough for ITM.

    # ret.py (core): ITC for all pairs, ITM rerank of the top-K. 500 images, 2,500 captions; caption c is of image c // 5
import torch, torch.nn.functional as F
from transformers import BlipForImageTextRetrieval, BlipProcessor, Blip2ForImageTextRetrieval, Blip2Processor
torch.set_grad_enabled(False); dev = "mps"                  # or "cuda"
ones = lambda e: torch.ones(e.shape[:2], dtype=torch.long, device=dev)

# ---- BLIP: image tokens once; ITC = projected [CLS] vectors; ITM = text encoder with cross-attention ----
m = BlipForImageTextRetrieval.from_pretrained("Salesforce/blip-itm-base-coco").to(dev).eval()
p = BlipProcessor.from_pretrained("Salesforce/blip-itm-base-coco")
emb = torch.cat([m.vision_model(pixel_values=p(images=images[i:i + 25], return_tensors="pt")["pixel_values"].to(dev))
                 .last_hidden_state for i in range(0, len(images), 25)])           # (500, 577, 768)
U = F.normalize(m.vision_proj(emb[:, 0]), dim=-1)                                  # (500, 256)
tok = p.tokenizer(caps, padding="max_length", truncation=True, max_length=35, return_tensors="pt").to(dev)
h = m.text_encoder(input_ids=tok["input_ids"], attention_mask=tok["attention_mask"]).last_hidden_state
T = F.normalize(m.text_proj(h[:, 0]), dim=-1)                                      # (2500, 256); batch this on small GPUs
itc = U @ T.T                                                                      # (500, 2500), formula (176.1)
def blip_itm(ii, tt):                                                              # index tensors of images and captions
    e = emb[ii]
    h = m.text_encoder(input_ids=tok["input_ids"][tt], attention_mask=tok["attention_mask"][tt],
                       encoder_hidden_states=e, encoder_attention_mask=ones(e)).last_hidden_state
    return m.itm_head(h[:, 0])[:, 1]                                                # the "match" logit

# ---- BLIP-2 stage 1: frozen ViT-g tokens, 32 Q-Former queries; heads kept in float32 ----
m2 = Blip2ForImageTextRetrieval.from_pretrained("Salesforce/blip2-itm-vit-g", dtype=torch.float16).to(dev).eval()
for mod in (m2.vision_projection, m2.text_projection, m2.itm_head): mod.float()     # fp16 scores create ties
p2 = Blip2Processor.from_pretrained("Salesforce/blip2-itm-vit-g")
emb2 = torch.cat([m2.vision_model(pixel_values=p2(images=images[i:i + 10], return_tensors="pt")["pixel_values"]
                  .to(dev, torch.float16)).last_hidden_state for i in range(0, len(images), 10)])   # (500, 257, 1408)
q = m2.query_tokens.expand(len(images), -1, -1)
z = m2.qformer(query_embeds=q, encoder_hidden_states=emb2, encoder_attention_mask=ones(emb2)).last_hidden_state
Z = F.normalize(m2.vision_projection(z.float()), dim=-1)                          # (500, 32, 256)
tok2 = p2.tokenizer(caps, padding="max_length", truncation=True, max_length=35, return_tensors="pt").to(dev)
h = m2.qformer(query_embeds=m2.embeddings(input_ids=tok2["input_ids"]), query_length=0,
               attention_mask=tok2["attention_mask"]).last_hidden_state
T2 = F.normalize(m2.text_projection(h[:, 0].float()), dim=-1)                     # (2500, 256)
itc2 = torch.einsum("iqd,td->itq", Z, T2).max(-1).values                          # max over the 32 queries
def blip2_itm(ii, tt):
    e = emb2[ii]; qq = m2.query_tokens.expand(len(ii), -1, -1)
    am = torch.cat([ones(qq), tok2["attention_mask"][tt]], 1)                      # queries and text attend to each other
    h = m2.qformer(query_embeds=m2.embeddings(input_ids=tok2["input_ids"][tt], query_embeds=qq), query_length=32,
                   attention_mask=am, encoder_hidden_states=e, encoder_attention_mask=ones(e)).last_hidden_state
    return m2.itm_head(h[:, :32].float())[:, :, 1].mean(1)                         # mean "match" logit over queries

# ---- rerank (176.2), text -> image; image -> text is the same with itc.T ----
def rerank_t2i(itc, itm, K=16):
    top = itc.T.topk(K, dim=1).indices                                            # (2500, K) candidate images
    tt = torch.arange(itc.shape[1], device=dev)[:, None].expand(-1, K)
    s = torch.cat([itm(top.flatten()[i:i + 128], tt.flatten()[i:i + 128]) for i in range(0, top.numel(), 128)])
    s = s.view(-1, K) + itc.T.gather(1, top)                                       # ITM logit + ITC cosine
    return top.gather(1, s.argsort(1, descending=True))                            # reranked top-K per caption

    Read the symbols
    \(Q\)
    the queries: 2,500 captions (text → image) or 500 images (image → text)
    \(r_q\)

    How deep should you rerank? Each extra candidate costs one more ITM pass per query. Figure 3 shows our measured R@1 for every depth from 1 (no rerank) to 16.

    Cost, in our run (rough, on a shared GPU): computing all BLIP ITC vectors took 30.7 s; the 48,000 ITM passes (16 per query in both directions) took 954 s, about 50 pairs per second. For BLIP-2, the ITC stage took 62.4 s (we expect most of it in the 1-billion-parameter ViT-g, but did not time the parts), and the ITM passes 366.7 s. This is the asymmetry that shapes every production system: ITC cost grows with the gallery once, offline; ITM cost grows with queries × depth, online.

    A word-order probe. Is a single pooled vector a “bag of words”? Yuksekgonul et al. showed that contrastive VLMs often ignore word order [Certain]. We test it directly: for each of the 2,500 captions we make a negative with the same words in a random order (“man on moped helmet a with a a small red A dirt on road”), and count how often the model scores the true caption higher for its own image. Chance is 0.5.

    Two results stand out. The ITC heads of BLIP and BLIP-2 prefer the true caption only 66.4% and 65.3% of the time: their pooled vectors are close to a bag of words. The ITM heads of the same models, which read the words with cross-attention to the image, reach 76.3% and 78.8%. Word order needs interaction. The surprise is CLIP at 85.0%, better than both ITM heads. We can only guess why [Speculation]: BLIP and BLIP-2 train ITC next to ITM and mine hard negatives for ITM with ITC, so the fine-grained work may move into the ITM head, while CLIP has only its contrastive loss and a very large batch of web text. A shuffled sentence is also not a realistic negative; benchmarks such as ARO swap only the roles of two words, which is much harder.

    Step 2: captioning, and how to score a sentence

    Now the models must write. Here the architecture decides what is even possible:

    • CLIP has no decoder. It cannot write a word. The best it can do is captioning by retrieval: return the closest caption from a pool. We use one caption of each of the 82,783 COCO Karpathy train images, so the true captions of our test images are never in the pool.
    • BLIP has a text decoder that cross-attends to the image tokens, fine-tuned on COCO captions with the prompt “a picture of” [Certain]. We use the paper’s decoding: beam search with 3 beams and a maximum length of 20 tokens [Certain], then remove the prompt.
    • BLIP-2 turns the image into 32 Q-Former outputs, projects them to OPT’s 2,560-number embeddings, and puts them in front of the text as soft visual prompts (input vectors that are not word embeddings). The frozen OPT-2.7B then continues the text. We give it no prompt, and use 5 beams and at most 30 new tokens.
    # gen.py (captioning part): one caption per image from each system

    Scoring with CIDEr. There is no single correct caption, so the standard metric compares a candidate with the 5 human references of its image. CIDEr (Consensus-based Image Description Evaluation, Vedantam et al.) counts shared word n-grams (runs of 1 to 4 words), but weights each n-gram by how rare it is across all images. “a” and “on a” appear in almost every reference and carry little weight; “dirt road” appears in few and carries a lot. This weighting is TF-IDF (term frequency times inverse document frequency):

    \[ g_\omega(s) = h_\omega(s) \cdot \log \frac{N}{\max\big(1,\; \mathrm{df}(\omega)\big)} \tag{176.4} \]

    COCO evaluation uses CIDEr-D, which adds two guards against gaming the metric: a candidate n-gram count is clipped at the reference count, and a Gaussian penalty punishes length differences:

    \[ \text{CIDEr-D}(c, R) = \frac{10}{4\,|R|} \sum_{n=1}^{4} \sum_{r \in R} \frac{\sum_{\omega} \min\big(g_\omega(c), g_\omega(r)\big)\, g_\omega(r)}{\lVert \mathbf{g}^n(c) \rVert\, \lVert \mathbf{g}^n(r) \rVert}\; e^{-\frac{(\ell_c - \ell_r)^2}{2\sigma^2}} \tag{176.5} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\omega\)
    an n-gram; \(h_\omega(s)\) is how often it occurs in sentence \(s\)
    \(N, \mathrm{df}(\omega)\)
    the number of images in the evaluation set, and in how many of them \(\omega\) occurs in at least one reference: the weights depend on the test set itself
    \(\mathbf{g}^n(s)\)
    the vector of TF-IDF weights of all n-grams of length \(n\) in \(s\)
    \(R\)
    the references of the image (5 here); \(\ell_c, \ell_r\) are the lengths of candidate and reference, \(\sigma = 6\)
    \(10\)
    a scale factor from the reference code; papers then multiply by 100 again, so “CIDEr 97.0” means 0.970 from the code

    We use the pycocoevalcap CIDEr scorer. Its standard tokenizer needs Java, so we replace it by a simple one (lower case, keep letters, digits and apostrophes). We also wrote our own CIDEr-D in 25 lines to check our understanding: it agrees with pycocoevalcap to 15 digits (0.9697293142528624 against …625 for BLIP).

    # metrics.py (CIDEr part)
import re
from pycocoevalcap.cider.cider import Cider
def tok(s): return " ".join(re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+(?:'[a-z]+)?", s.lower()))   # stand-in for the PTB tokenizer
def cider(cands, refs):                                    # cands: list of str; refs: list of lists of str
    gts = {i: [tok(r) for r in rs] for i, rs in enumerate(refs)}
    res = {i: [tok(c)] for i, c in enumerate(cands)}
    mean, per_image = Cider().compute_score(gts, res)        # CIDEr-D, sigma = 6; IDF from these refs
    return 100 * mean, per_image

    A second opinion: a VLM as judge. CIDEr only rewards overlap with five sentences. A different check is to ask a stronger model which caption is better. We use Qwen2-VL-2B-Instruct, a small modern VLM that is not related to the three models, show it the image and two captions, and read the probability of the next token “A” against “B”. Judges have a known position bias (a preference for the first or second option, Zheng et al.) [Certain], so we ask twice with the order swapped and count a win only when both orders agree:

    \[ \text{win}(x \succ y) = \mathbb{1}\big[\, p_{xy}(A) \gt \tfrac12 \;\text{ and }\; p_{yx}(A) \lt \tfrac12 \,\big] \tag{176.6} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(p_{xy}(A)\)
    the judge’s probability of answering “A” when caption \(x\) is shown as A and \(y\) as B (renormalized over the two letters)
    inconsistent
    both orders pick the same position: the judge followed the slot, not the caption; we count it as no decision
    Algorithm 2: pairwise judging with order swap
    1. for each image \(I\) and caption pair \((x, y)\):
    2.   \(p_1 \leftarrow P(\text{“A”} \mid I, \text{A} = x, \text{B} = y)\) // one forward pass, read two logits
    3.   \(p_2 \leftarrow P(\text{“A”} \mid I, \text{A} = y, \text{B} = x)\) // same question, order swapped
    4.   if \(p_1 \gt 0.5\) and \(p_2 \lt 0.5\): \(x\) wins; elif \(p_1 \lt 0.5\) and \(p_2 \gt 0.5\): \(y\) wins; else: inconsistent
    5. report wins of \(x\), wins of \(y\) and the inconsistent count // a high count means the judge is unreliable here

    Step 3: visual question answering

    VQA needs the question and the image to interact: “What color is the train?” asks for one property of one object. Each system gets the question in a different way:

    • CLIP can only score text against an image. We give it 1,000 candidate answers (the most frequent answers in VQAv2 val) and score the text “question: {q} answer: {a}” for each, keeping the best. The question and the image never interact inside the model; they only meet in the final dot product.
    • BLIP (the VQA checkpoint) encodes the question with cross-attention to the image, and a separate decoder writes the answer. The paper ranks 3,128 candidate answers with the decoder [Certain]; the Hugging Face model generates freely, which we use (3 beams, at most 10 tokens).
    • BLIP-2 gets the 32 visual tokens and then the text “Question: {q} Answer:”, the paper’s prompt for OPT, with 5 beams and length penalty −1 to favour short answers [Certain]. We keep the text up to the first line break.
    • A blind baseline never looks at the image: for each question type (VQAv2 labels each question with a type such as “what color is the” or “how many”), answer the most common answer of that type on the other val images.

    The VQA accuracy. Each question has 10 human answers, and people disagree (“8”, “9”, “10” sheep). The VQA metric gives full credit if at least 3 people gave your answer, and partial credit below that. The official script averages this over the 10 ways to leave one annotator out [Certain]:

    \[ \mathrm{Acc}(a) = \frac{1}{10} \sum_{j=1}^{10} \min\!\Big(1,\; \frac{\#\{\, i \ne j : a_i = a \,\}}{3}\Big) \tag{176.7} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(a\)
    the model’s answer after normalization: lower case, punctuation removed, number words to digits (“two” → “2”), articles (“a”, “an”, “the”) removed
    \(a_i\)
    the normalized answer of annotator \(i\); the sum over \(j\) leaves annotator \(j\) out, so each subset has 9 humans
    Worked example: “How many sheep are there?”

    The 10 humans answered “8” five times, “9” three times and “10” twice. BLIP answered “10”. In the 2 subsets that leave out a “10” annotator, 1 of the other 9 says “10”: credit \(\min(1, 1/3) = 0.333\). In the other 8 subsets, 2 say “10”: credit \(2/3 = 0.667\). So \(\mathrm{Acc} = (2 \times 0.333 + 8 \times 0.667)/10 = 0.600\). The answer “8” would get 1.0 and “9” would get \((3 \times 0.667 + 7 \times 1)/10 = 0.900\). The metric rewards agreeing with the crowd, which is fair for colors and hard for counts of many objects.

    # metrics.py (VQA part): a close copy of the official normalization, then (176.7)
manual = {"none": "0", "zero": "0", "one": "1", "two": "2", "three": "3", "four": "4", "five": "5",
          "six": "6", "seven": "7", "eight": "8", "nine": "9", "ten": "10"}
punct = [";", "/", "[", "]", '"', "{", "}", "(", ")", "=", "+", "\\", "_", "-", ">", "<", "@", "`", ",", "?", "!"]
period_strip = re.compile(r"(?!<=\d)(\.)(?!\d)"); comma_strip = re.compile(r"(\d)(,)(\d)")
def norm_answer(s):
    s = s.replace("\n", " ").strip().lower(); out = s
    for p in punct:
        out = out.replace(p, "") if (p + " " in s or " " + p in s or comma_strip.search(s)) else out.replace(p, " ")
    out = period_strip.sub("", out)
    return " ".join(manual.get(w, w) for w in out.split() if w not in {"a", "an", "the"})
def vqa_acc(pred, answers):
    a, g = norm_answer(pred), [norm_answer(x) for x in answers]
    return sum(min(1.0, sum(x == a for x in g[:j] + g[j + 1:]) / 3) for j in range(len(g))) / len(g)

    Three lessons carry over to every VLM you will evaluate:

    • Check what the model has seen. BLIP’s 83.0% VQA accuracy is not a zero-shot number; on the official test-dev split, which it did not train on, the BLIP paper reports 77.54 for the base model with 14M images [Certain]. BLIP-2’s VQA number is zero-shot. Comparing them as equals would be a mistake. For captions the story is subtler: BLIP was fine-tuned on Karpathy train and BLIP-2 OPT was not, yet BLIP-2 wins. Its pretraining mix contains COCO’s own human captions and synthetic captions written by BLIP-large [Certain], a captioner that BLIP fine-tunes on COCO for CapFilt [Likely], so “pre-trained only” does not mean “never saw COCO-style text”.
    • A metric is a model of “good”, with its own biases. CIDEr rewards the consensus wording of the five references and punishes length differences; in our run it scored both BLIP and BLIP-2 above a held-out human caption. The judge has its own bias (position) and its own blind spots. Report two views and how they disagree.
    • Interaction decides what a model can do. If question and image only meet in a final dot product (CLIP), the model cannot answer “what color is the train?”: it can only say what the image is about. Cross-attention (BLIP’s ITM and VQA encoder, BLIP-2’s Q-Former) lets the text pick which part of the image to read. That is why every modern VLM (session 177) feeds visual tokens into the language model instead of pooling them into one vector.

    Common mistakes

    • Comparing a fine-tuned number with a zero-shot number. BLIP’s VQA model trained on VQAv2 val; scoring it on VQAv2 val and putting it next to BLIP-2’s zero-shot score is not a fair comparison. Always write down, per checkpoint, which datasets and splits it was trained on.
    • Comparing your CIDEr with a paper’s CIDEr. The value depends on the tokenizer, on the set the IDF is computed from (ours moved from 97.0 to 92.1 for BLIP when the IDF came from 5,000 images instead of 500) and on post-processing. Compare systems only inside one evaluation run.
    • Leaving the prompt in the caption. BLIP generates “a picture of a person riding a motorcycle”. The authors’ code removes the prompt; if you forget, our BLIP CIDEr goes from 97.0 to 104.9, because the longer caption gets a smaller length penalty. A “better” score from a bug.
    • Running ITM over the whole gallery, or skipping it. ITM costs one cross-attention pass per pair: 50 pairs per second in our run. Use it only to rerank an ITC shortlist, and do not call ITC-only numbers “BLIP retrieval” without saying so.
    • A probe with a hidden second difference. Our first word-order probe kept the final period on the true captions and dropped it from the shuffled ones. That alone moved BLIP-2 ITC from 0.653 to 0.550 and BLIP-2 ITM from 0.788 to 0.844. A controlled test must change exactly one thing.
    • Right-padding a decoder-only LLM in batched generation. BLIP-2 with OPT must pad on the left; with right padding, pad tokens sit between the prompt and the first generated token and the answers change [Likely].
    • Trusting a single-order judge. Ask twice with the order swapped and report the inconsistent cases (176.6); a judge that follows the slot is measuring itself, not your models.
    • No blind baseline for VQA. Without the question-type prior (32.2% here) you cannot tell how much of a VQA score needs the image at all.

    Interview questions

    What is the difference between ITC and ITM, and why do retrieval systems use both?basic
    ITC (image–text contrastive) encodes image and text separately and scores a pair with a cosine (176.1). Gallery vectors are computed once, so a query costs one matrix product over the whole gallery. ITM (image–text matching) reads the text with cross-attention to the image tokens and outputs a match logit; it sees details and word order, but needs one full pass per pair. So systems use ITC to shortlist the top \(k\) and ITM to rerank them (176.2). In our run BLIP went from 0.822 to 0.861 text → image R@1 with \(k = 16\), and BLIP-2 from 0.816 to 0.857, at a cost of 16 ITM passes per query.
    Why can CLIP not do captioning or VQA directly? What did it score when forced?basic
    CLIP has no decoder, so it cannot produce new text; and its image and text towers only meet in a final dot product, so a question cannot change which part of the image is read. Forced work-arounds: captioning by retrieval from 82,783 train captions gave CIDEr 61.8, because the copied caption is about a similar scene but rarely this one. VQA by ranking 1,000 answers with “question: q answer: a” gave 4.2%, below a blind question-type baseline (32.2%): CLIP picked words that describe the image (“suzuki”, “train”) and almost never “yes” or “no”, which have no visual content.
    How does BLIP-2 connect a frozen image encoder to a frozen LLM, and what is trained?core
    A Q-Former with 32 learned query vectors cross-attends to the frozen ViT-g features (257 tokens of 1,408 numbers at 224 px) and outputs 32 vectors of 768. A linear layer maps them to OPT’s 2,560-dimensional input space, and they are prepended to the text as soft visual prompts. Stage 1 trains the Q-Former with ITC, ITM and image-grounded generation; stage 2 trains it with the LLM’s language-modeling loss. Only the Q-Former and projection are trained: 107.1 M of 3.745 B parameters in our loaded model (2.9%), matching the paper’s 107M.
    Define CIDEr. Why can a model score higher than a human caption?core
    CIDEr represents captions as TF-IDF vectors of 1- to 4-grams, where the IDF is computed over the evaluation set’s references (176.4), and averages the cosine with each reference over n and references; CIDEr-D clips counts and applies a Gaussian length penalty (176.5). It rewards the consensus wording. A model fine-tuned on COCO learns that consensus style, while a single human caption is one individual’s wording. In our run a held-out human caption scored 91.6 against the other 4 references, while BLIP scored 97.0 and BLIP-2 140.8 against all 5. A higher CIDEr does not mean a better description.
    Your BLIP VQA model scores 83% on VQAv2 val. What do you check before you report it?core
    Whether the model trained on that split. The BLIP paper fine-tunes VQA on VQAv2 train and val plus Visual Genome, and reports 77.54 on test-dev for the base model [Certain]; our 83.0% on val is inflated by contamination. Also check: the answer normalization and the 10-annotator formula (176.7), the generation vs candidate-ranking difference with the paper, and a blind baseline (32.2% here) to see how much is language prior. Report per answer type: BLIP gets 96.5% on yes/no but 67.7% on number questions.
    How does the VQA accuracy metric work? Give the score of an answer that 2 of 10 annotators gave.basic
    Normalize all answers, then for each of the 10 subsets of 9 annotators give \(\min(1, \text{matches}/3)\) and average (176.7). If 2 of 10 gave the answer: in the 2 subsets that drop one of them there is 1 match (1/3); in the other 8 there are 2 matches (2/3). The score is \((2/3 + 16/3)/10 = 0.6\). Three or more matching humans in every subset give 1.0.
    Why did BLIP-2 not beat BLIP on retrieval in this lab, despite a 1-billion-parameter vision encoder?advanced
    Three reasons, two of them about data. The BLIP checkpoint was fine-tuned on COCO Karpathy train with ITC and ITM, so our test photos and captions are in-domain for it. The public BLIP-2 retrieval checkpoint is (most likely) the stage-1 pretrained Q-Former, not the COCO fine-tuned model of the paper’s table [Likely]. And BLIP-2 sees the image at 224 px through a frozen encoder, while BLIP fine-tunes its ViT at 384 px. Result: ITC R@1 0.822 against 0.816, and 0.861 against 0.857 after ITM. To compare architectures you must hold the fine-tuning data fixed.
    Is a CLIP-style embedding a bag of words? How would you test it?advanced
    Build a controlled negative: the same words in a random order, and count how often the true caption scores higher for its own image (chance 0.5). In our run (true captions and shuffles both without the final period): CLIP 0.850, BLIP ITC 0.664 and ITM 0.763, BLIP-2 ITC 0.653 and ITM 0.788. The ITC heads of BLIP and BLIP-2 are close to bag-of-words; the ITM heads, which read the text with cross-attention to the image, are far less so. For a fuller test use benchmarks built for it, such as the ARO suite of Yuksekgonul et al., which also proposes training with word-order hard negatives.
    How would you use an LLM or VLM as a caption judge without fooling yourself?core
    Use pairwise comparisons, read the answer from token probabilities rather than parsing free text, and ask both orders; count a win only when both agree (176.6) and report the inconsistent share. In our run with Qwen2-VL-2B, the judge picked the second slot in both orders for 300 of the 500 BLIP-versus-BLIP-2 images; only 197 decisions survived the swap, and those favoured BLIP-2 171 to 26. Also check the judge against humans on a sample, keep the judge different from the systems being compared (a BLIP-family judge would favour BLIP-style text), and report it next to a reference-based metric such as CIDEr.
    Design an evaluation to choose between a BLIP-style and a BLIP-2-style model for product alt-text.advanced
    Collect a few hundred of your own product images with 3 to 5 references written in your house style, never used for training. Measure CIDEr (one run, one tokenizer, IDF on your set), a judge with order swap, and a human check of factual errors (invented colors, brands, counts) on a sample. Slice by product type and by text-on-product. Add speed and memory: BLIP base is 224 M parameters, BLIP-2 OPT-2.7B is 3.7 B (about 7.5 GB in fp16). If BLIP-2 wins on the judge but loses on CIDEr, the style differs; a light fine-tune of the Q-Former on your captions can fix style without touching the frozen parts.
    What do the three families tell you about modern VLM design?core
    CLIP shows that contrastive pretraining gives excellent, cheap, general image features, which is why most VLMs still start from a CLIP- or SigLIP-type encoder. BLIP shows that cross-attention between text and image is needed for matching details and that a decoder is needed to write, and that bootstrapped captions clean noisy web data. BLIP-2 shows that a small trainable bridge can connect frozen experts, and that the LLM brings fluency and knowledge but also its own style and priors. LLaVA-style models keep the frozen-encoder idea but replace the Q-Former by a simple projector and feed many visual tokens to an instruction-tuned LLM (session 178).

    Exercises

    1. One annotator out of 10 gave the answer “gravel”; nobody else did. What VQA accuracy does the answer “gravel” get with (176.7)?
    2. A gallery has 1 million images and your service gets 1,000 text queries per second. How many ITM passes per second do you need with \(k = 16\), and how many if you ran ITM on every pair?
    3. In our 500 images, the unigram “a” occurs in the references of 499 images and the bigram “dirt road” in 1. Compute their IDF \(\log(N / \mathrm{df})\) and the ratio. What does this say about which words drive CIDEr?
    4. BLIP-2 OPT-2.7B has 3,744.8 M parameters, of which the Q-Former (105.2 M) and the projection (2.0 M) are trained. What fraction is trained? How many visual tokens does OPT see per image, compared with BLIP’s image encoder output at 384 px with 16-pixel patches?
    5. Our first word-order probe compared captions that end with a period against shuffles without one. Why is that a confound, and how would it bias the result?
    6. Run the VQA step again with BLIP-2 and the prompt “Question: {} Short answer:”. Which answer types change most? Explain the change with the length of the answers.
    Answerscheck
    1. In the 1 subset that drops that annotator, 0 matches: 0. In the other 9 subsets, 1 match: 1/3. The score is \(9 \times (1/3) / 10 = 0.3\). 2. With \(k = 16\): \(1{,}000 \times 16 = 16{,}000\) passes per second (for example 320 GPUs at our measured 50 pairs per second, or far fewer with a fast GPU and batching). On every pair: \(1{,}000 \times 10^6 = 10^9\) passes per second, impossible. 3. \(\log(500/499) = 0.002\) and \(\log(500/1) = 6.215\); the ratio is about 3,100. CIDEr is driven by rare, content-bearing n-grams; function words count for almost nothing, so a caption that misses the key object or scene word scores near zero even if it is fluent. 4. \(107.1 / 3{,}744.8 = 2.86\%\). OPT sees 32 visual tokens; BLIP’s ViT-B/16 at 384 px outputs \((384/16)^2 + 1 = 577\) tokens, 18 times more. (BLIP-2’s frozen ViT-g gives 257 tokens, which the Q-Former compresses 8 times.) 5. A model can prefer the true caption just because of the period (punctuation is a token), not because of word order; this would inflate every score, more for models whose tokenizer and training data treat the final period as informative. The fix is to strip it from both sides, as in our final probe. 6. Open-ended. Expect the share of one-word answers to change; because VQA accuracy needs an exact match after normalization, longer answers such as “a man on a horse” score 0 even when correct, so the answer style can move the score by several points without any change in what the model sees.

    Key takeaways

    • Run every model on the same images with the same metric code; a number is only meaningful next to the numbers it is compared with.
    • Retrieval: ITC is cheap and scales to a gallery (176.1); ITM reranking of the top \(k\) (176.2) added about 4 points of R@1 for BLIP and BLIP-2. Most of the gain came by \(k = 4\).
    • CLIP cannot write and cannot condition on a question: captioning by retrieval gave CIDEr 61.8 and VQA by ranking 4.2%, below a blind 32.2% baseline.
    • CIDEr (176.4, 176.5) rewards the consensus style and depends on tokenizer, IDF set and post-processing; BLIP-2 (140.8) and BLIP (97.0) both scored above a held-out human caption (91.6), and BLIP’s decoding settings alone moved it by 12 points. Pair it with a judge that is asked in both orders (176.6).
    • VQA accuracy (176.7) gives partial credit for answers that fewer than 3 humans gave; always report per answer type and a blind baseline.
    • Check training data before comparing: BLIP’s 83.0% VQA is on questions it trained on; BLIP-2’s 55.2% is zero-shot.
    • Cross-attention is what lets text read the image: on shuffled captions the ITM heads beat the ITC heads of the same family by a wide margin.

    \[ s_{\text{ITC}} = \mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{t}, \qquad s^{\text{BLIP-2}}_{\text{ITC}} = \max_q \mathbf{z}_q^\top \mathbf{t}, \qquad \text{score} = \ell_{\text{ITM}} + s_{\text{ITC}} \;\; (\text{top-}k \text{ only}) \]
    \[ \mathrm{R@}k = \tfrac{1}{|Q|} \textstyle\sum_q \mathbb{1}[r_q \le k], \qquad g_\omega(s) = h_\omega(s) \log \frac{N}{\max(1, \mathrm{df}(\omega))} \]
    \[ \text{CIDEr-D} = \frac{10}{4|R|} \sum_{n, r} \frac{\sum_\omega \min(g_\omega(c), g_\omega(r))\, g_\omega(r)}{\lVert \mathbf{g}^n(c) \rVert \lVert \mathbf{g}^n(r) \rVert} e^{-\frac{(\ell_c - \ell_r)^2}{2\sigma^2}}, \qquad \mathrm{Acc}(a) = \frac{1}{10} \sum_j \min\!\Big(1, \frac{\#\{i \ne j : a_i = a\}}{3}\Big) \]
