    Images as data and a CNN refresher

    Before a vision-language model can talk about a picture, the picture must become a tensor of numbers with the exact shape, scale and colour order that the vision encoder was trained on. This session shows how that happens (pixels, channels, normalization, resizing and aspect ratio), then refreshes convolutional networks: the sliding filter, the receptive field, pooling, ResNet and transfer learning. It ends with what CNNs do well and what they miss, which is the reason the next session moves to the Vision Transformer.

    Time: 3 to 4 hours (reading 2 hours, exercises 1 to 2 hours)Level: Beginner to interview levelBefore this: Sessions 3 to 5 (math, optimization, PyTorch)

    What you will learn

    • How an image is stored (uint8, HWC vs CHW, RGB vs BGR) and how much memory images and video frames take
    • Why every encoder needs its own normalization (ImageNet, CLIP, SigLIP statistics) and what goes wrong when it does not match
    • Three ways to fit any aspect ratio into a square input (squash, crop, pad) and what each one loses
    • Convolution as a sliding dot product: output size, parameter count and multiply-adds of a layer, computed by hand
    • Receptive field growth through depth, stride and pooling, and why the effective receptive field is smaller
    • From LeNet to ResNet: residual blocks, the ResNet-50 stage layout, and transfer learning with frozen or fine-tuned backbones
    • What the CNN assumptions (locality, translation equivariance) give you, and where CNNs fail: aliasing, texture bias, limited global context

    Why this matters

    A vision-language model (VLM) never sees a picture. It sees a tensor: a block of numbers with a fixed shape, a fixed value range and a fixed colour order. A vision encoder turns that tensor into vectors, and only then can the language model read them. If the numbers are prepared in the wrong way, the model still answers, fluently and wrongly.

    This session starts the vision track (Track 7). It has two parts. The first part is images as data: how a photo or a video frame becomes a tensor, and the small preprocessing choices (normalization, resizing, cropping, padding) that silently decide what the model can see. These choices come back in every later session: CLIP and SigLIP have their own normalization, and high-resolution VLMs are mostly about handling aspect ratio well (session 180).

    The second part is a refresher on convolutional neural networks (CNNs). From 2012 to about 2020, CNNs were the standard way to turn images into features, and they are still everywhere: in detection and segmentation backbones, on phones, in video pipelines, and as the image encoder of some VLMs. The Vision Transformer of the next session (session 164) is easiest to understand as an answer to the limits of CNNs. So we build the CNN up from one filter, measure it with numbers, and end with what it does well and what it misses.

    Interview tipTypical questions: “What is the output size and parameter count of this conv layer?”, “What is a receptive field?”, “Why do residual connections help?”, “Freeze or fine-tune the backbone?”, “Are CNNs translation invariant?”, “Why did ViTs replace CNNs?”. Every one of them is answered below with a formula and a number.

    An image is a grid of numbers

    A digital colour image is a grid of pixels (picture elements). Each pixel stores three numbers, one per channel: red, green and blue (RGB). In a normal photo file (JPEG, PNG) each number is an 8-bit unsigned integer (uint8) from 0 (no light) to 255 (full light). So an image of height \(H\) and width \(W\) is an array of \(H \times W \times 3\) integers.

    • Layout. Image libraries (PIL, OpenCV, NumPy) store the array as \((H, W, C)\): channels last. PyTorch models expect \((B, C, H, W)\): a batch dimension first and channels before height and width. The conversion is one permute(2, 0, 1).
    • Colour order. PIL and torchvision give RGB. OpenCV’s cv2.imread gives BGR (blue first). [Certain] A model trained on RGB that receives BGR sees red and blue swapped.
    • Size. A 224 × 224 RGB image is \(224 \cdot 224 \cdot 3 = 150{,}528\) numbers: 150,528 bytes as uint8, four times that (602,112 bytes) as float32. One 1920 × 1080 video frame is 6,220,800 bytes, and one second of 30 fps video is about 187 MB before compression. This is why video systems sample frames and shrink them first (session 201).
    • Grayscale and others. A grayscale image has one channel. Some images have a fourth alpha channel (transparency) that must be dropped or blended before an RGB model sees it.
    AnalogyA colour image is three spreadsheets of the same size laid on top of each other: one sheet for how much red light each cell has, one for green, one for blue. A CNN reads all three sheets at once, a small window at a time.

    Normalization

    Neural networks train best when their inputs are centred near 0 with a spread near 1 (the same reason we normalize hidden states, see session 22). So each channel \(c\) is first scaled to \([0, 1]\), then shifted by a mean \(\mu_c\) and divided by a standard deviation \(\sigma_c\):

    \[ \tilde{x}_{c,i,j} = \frac{x_{c,i,j} / 255 - \mu_c}{\sigma_c} \tag{163.1} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(x_{c,i,j}\)
    the raw uint8 value of channel \(c\) at row \(i\), column \(j\)
    \(\mu_c, \sigma_c\)
    a mean and a standard deviation per channel, fixed by whoever trained the encoder
    \(\tilde{x}_{c,i,j}\)
    the number the network actually receives

    The important point: \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) are part of the model. They are not computed on your data. Three common choices [Certain]:

    Encoder family\(\mu\) (R, G, B)\(\sigma\) (R, G, B)Range of \(\tilde{x}\)
    ImageNet CNNs (torchvision ResNet, many others)0.485, 0.456, 0.4060.229, 0.224, 0.225about −2.1 to 2.6
    OpenAI CLIP0.4815, 0.4578, 0.40820.2686, 0.2613, 0.2758about −1.8 to 2.1
    SigLIP (Hugging Face config)0.5, 0.5, 0.50.5, 0.5, 0.5exactly −1 to 1

    The ImageNet numbers are the average colour of the ImageNet training photos. CLIP measured its own statistics on its own data. SigLIP simply maps \([0, 1]\) to \([-1, 1]\). Figure 1 lets you push a few pixel colours through each recipe.

    Worked example: pure white and pure black, ImageNet recipe

    White is (255, 255, 255), so \(x/255 = 1\) in every channel. Red channel: \((1 - 0.485)/0.229 = 2.249\). Green: \((1 - 0.456)/0.224 = 2.429\). Blue: \((1 - 0.406)/0.225 = 2.640\).

    Black is (0, 0, 0): red \(-0.485/0.229 = -2.118\), green \(-2.036\), blue \(-1.804\). So the ImageNet-normalized range is about \([-2.1, 2.6]\), not \([-1, 1]\). With SigLIP’s recipe the same two pixels become exactly \(+1\) and \(-1\).

    Resizing and aspect ratio

    Most vision encoders take a fixed square input: 224 × 224 for classic CNNs and many ViTs, 336 × 336 for CLIP ViT-L/14-336, 384 or 448 for others. Real images are not square: a phone photo is 4:3, a video frame is 16:9, a receipt can be 1:3. The aspect ratio is width divided by height. There are three standard ways to fit a \(W \times H\) image into an \(S \times S\) square:

    1. Squash: resize width and height separately to \(S\). Nothing is lost, but shapes are stretched: a circle becomes an ellipse.
    2. Resize and center crop: scale the shorter side to \(S\), then cut the central \(S \times S\) square. Shapes are correct, but the edges of the long side are thrown away. This is the default for ImageNet CNNs and for CLIP. [Certain]
    3. Pad (letterbox): scale the longer side to \(S\), then fill the rest with a constant colour. Nothing is lost and nothing is stretched, but part of the input is empty, and the content gets fewer pixels.
    \[ s_{\text{crop}} = \frac{S}{\min(W, H)}, \qquad s_{\text{pad}} = \frac{S}{\max(W, H)}, \qquad \text{kept (crop)} = \text{content (pad)} = \frac{\min(W, H)}{\max(W, H)} \tag{163.2} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(W, H\)
    width and height of the original image in pixels
    \(S\)
    side of the square input of the encoder
    \(s\)
    the scale factor applied to both sides (so shapes are not distorted)
    kept, content
    the fraction of the original image area that survives the crop; the same number is the fraction of the padded square that holds real content

    For a 1920 × 1080 frame and \(S = 224\): the crop keeps \(1080/1920 = 56.25\%\) of the frame, and the pad leaves \(43.75\%\) of the square empty. Either way the encoder sees only \(224^2 / (1920 \cdot 1080) = 2.4\%\) as many pixels as the original. Small text, a distant face or a road sign may simply vanish. That single fact drives a large part of modern VLM design: tiling, dynamic resolution and native aspect ratio (session 180).

    Watch outResizing also needs anti-aliasing: when you shrink an image a lot, fine patterns must be blurred first or they turn into false patterns (moiré). PIL applies it by default; torch.nn.functional.interpolate only does it with antialias=True. Different libraries give slightly different pixels for the “same” resize, which can shift accuracy a little. [Likely] Use the exact preprocessing the encoder was trained with.

    Convolution: a sliding filter

    Now the network. The simplest idea would be to flatten the 150,528 input numbers and use a fully connected layer. With only 1,000 output units, that one layer would already need 150 million weights, and it would treat a cat in the top-left corner as a completely different thing from the same cat in the bottom-right. Images have two properties we can use instead:

    • Locality. Nearby pixels belong together. An edge, a corner, a patch of fur: each is visible inside a small window.
    • Same pattern, any place. An edge detector that works in one corner should work everywhere.

    A convolution uses both. It takes a small grid of weights, the filter or kernel (for example 3 × 3), slides it over the image, and at every position computes a dot product between the filter and the pixels under it. The same filter weights are used at every position: this is called weight sharing. The result is a new grid, the feature map, that is large where the image looks like the filter. With several input channels the filter has a depth too, and with several output channels we use several filters:

    \[ Y_{o,i,j} = b_o + \sum_{c=1}^{C_{\text{in}}} \sum_{u=0}^{k-1} \sum_{v=0}^{k-1} W_{o,c,u,v} \; X_{c,\; s i + u - p,\; s j + v - p} \tag{163.3} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(X\)
    input with \(C_{\text{in}}\) channels; positions outside the image count as 0 (zero padding)
    \(W_{o,c,u,v}\)
    weight of filter \(o\), input channel \(c\), offset \((u, v)\) inside the \(k \times k\) window
    \(b_o\)
    one bias per output channel
    \(s\)
    stride: how many pixels the window jumps between positions
    \(p\)
    padding: how many rows and columns of zeros are added around the input
    \(Y_{o,i,j}\)
    output channel \(o\) at output position \((i, j)\)

    Strictly, (163.3) is a cross-correlation: a mathematical convolution flips the filter first. Deep learning libraries do not flip, and since the filter is learned, the difference does not matter. [Certain] Each output is just a dot product, so a whole conv layer can be written as one big matrix multiply (the PyTorch code below checks this with unfold).

    AnalogyA filter is a small stencil with a shape cut into it. You slide the stencil across a page and, at each spot, measure how well the ink underneath matches the cut-out. The feature map is the map of “how well it matched” for every spot.

    Output size, parameters and compute

    Along each side, the number of window positions is

    \[ H_{\text{out}} = \left\lfloor \frac{H_{\text{in}} + 2p - k}{s} \right\rfloor + 1 \tag{163.4} \]

    The ResNet first layer has \(k = 7\), \(s = 2\), \(p = 3\) on a 224 input: \(\lfloor (224 + 6 - 7)/2 \rfloor + 1 = 112\). A 3 × 3 conv with \(p = 1\), \(s = 1\) keeps the size (“same” padding). The cost of a layer is

    \[ \text{params} = k^2 C_{\text{in}} C_{\text{out}} + C_{\text{out}}, \qquad \text{MACs} = k^2 C_{\text{in}} C_{\text{out}} \, H_{\text{out}} W_{\text{out}} \tag{163.5} \]

    A MAC is one multiply-accumulate (about 2 FLOPs). Notice the difference: parameters do not depend on image size (weight sharing), but compute grows with the number of output positions.

    Worked example: one ResNet-style layer

    A 3 × 3 conv from 64 to 128 channels with an output of 56 × 56. Parameters: \(9 \cdot 64 \cdot 128 + 128 = 73{,}856\). Multiply-adds: \(9 \cdot 64 \cdot 128 \cdot 56 \cdot 56 = 231{,}211{,}008\), about 0.23 billion.

    Now a 3 × 3 conv from 128 to 256 channels at 28 × 28 (one stage deeper: half the size, twice the channels). Parameters: 295,168, four times more. Multiply-adds: \(9 \cdot 128 \cdot 256 \cdot 784 = 231{,}211{,}008\), exactly the same. Halving the resolution while doubling the channels keeps the compute per layer constant. This is the design rule of VGG and ResNet. [Certain]

    Figure 3 runs formula (163.3) on a tiny one-channel 8 × 8 image: dark on the left (value 1), bright on the right (value 7), with a small bright block in the dark half.

    Algorithm 1: forward pass of one conv layer (formula 163.3)
    1. input: \(X\) of shape \((C_{\text{in}}, H, W)\), weights \(W\) of shape \((C_{\text{out}}, C_{\text{in}}, k, k)\), bias \(b\), stride \(s\), padding \(p\)
    2. pad \(X\) with \(p\) zeros on every side
    3. \(H_{\text{out}}, W_{\text{out}} \leftarrow\) formula (163.4)
    4. for \(o = 1 \dots C_{\text{out}}\), \(i = 0 \dots H_{\text{out}}-1\), \(j = 0 \dots W_{\text{out}}-1\):
    5.   \(\text{win} \leftarrow X[:, si : si + k, sj : sj + k]\) // a \(C_{\text{in}} \times k \times k\) block
    6.   \(Y[o, i, j] \leftarrow b_o + \sum \text{win} \odot W[o]\) // one dot product of length \(k^2 C_{\text{in}}\)
    7. apply a nonlinearity (ReLU) to \(Y\), usually after a normalization layer
    8. return \(Y\) of shape \((C_{\text{out}}, H_{\text{out}}, W_{\text{out}})\) // real kernels batch all windows into one GEMM

    Two special kernel sizes are worth knowing. A 1 × 1 convolution looks at one position only: it is a linear layer applied to the channel vector of each pixel, used to change the number of channels cheaply. A depthwise convolution uses one filter per channel (no mixing between channels) and is the cheap building block of MobileNet and ConvNeXt. [Certain]

    Receptive field and pooling

    One output of a 3 × 3 conv depends on a 3 × 3 patch of its input. Its receptive field is the region of the original image that can affect it. Stack a second 3 × 3 conv on top: each of its outputs sees a 3 × 3 patch of the first map, which covers a 5 × 5 patch of the image. A stride or a pooling layer makes every later step count double. The rule, from the input upwards, is

    \[ r_\ell = r_{\ell-1} + (k_\ell - 1)\, j_{\ell-1}, \qquad j_\ell = j_{\ell-1}\, s_\ell, \qquad r_0 = j_0 = 1 \tag{163.6} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(r_\ell\)
    receptive field (in input pixels, along one side) of a unit after layer \(\ell\)
    \(j_\ell\)
    the jump: how many input pixels apart two neighbouring units of layer \(\ell\) are (the product of all strides so far)
    \(k_\ell, s_\ell\)
    kernel size and stride of layer \(\ell\) (pooling layers count as layers too)

    So \(L\) stacked 3 × 3 convs with stride 1 reach \(1 + 2L\) pixels: 3, 5, 7, 9, … Growth is only linear, which is why every CNN also downsamples. With strides the jump doubles each time, and the receptive field grows fast. Applying (163.6) to all 13 conv and 5 pooling layers of VGG-16 gives 212 pixels; applying it to the torchvision ResNet-50 gives 427 pixels, larger than the 224 input. [Certain, computed]

    A large theoretical receptive field does not mean the unit uses all of it. Luo et al. showed that the effective receptive field, the part that really changes the output, has a roughly Gaussian shape and covers only a fraction of the theoretical one. [Certain] The centre pixels matter much more than the border ones. This is one reason CNNs struggle with relations between far-apart parts of an image.

    Pooling

    Pooling summarizes each small window with one number and has no weights. Max pooling keeps the largest value (the strongest detection in the window); average pooling keeps the mean. Global average pooling (GAP) averages over the whole feature map and turns a \(C \times H \times W\) tensor into a vector of \(C\) numbers:

    \[ \text{maxpool}(X)_{c,i,j} = \max_{0 \le u, v \lt k} X_{c,\, si+u,\, sj+v}, \qquad \text{GAP}(X)_c = \frac{1}{HW} \sum_{i=1}^{H} \sum_{j=1}^{W} X_{c,i,j} \tag{163.7} \]

    GAP is how a ResNet turns its final 7 × 7 × 2048 map into one 2048-number image vector. Because GAP works for any \(H\) and \(W\), a CNN can process images of any size, unlike a fully connected layer.

    What convolution assumes: locality and equivariance

    The design of (163.3) builds two assumptions, called inductive biases, into the network: locality (a unit sees only a small window) and translation equivariance. Equivariance means: if you shift the input, the output shifts by the same amount and otherwise stays the same. With \(T_\delta\) meaning “shift by \(\delta\) pixels”,

    \[ \operatorname{conv}(T_\delta\, x) = T_\delta \operatorname{conv}(x) \qquad \text{(stride 1)}, \qquad \text{but in general} \quad \operatorname{pool}_{s=2}(T_1\, x) \ne T_{1/2} \operatorname{pool}_{s=2}(x) \tag{163.8} \]

    The first part is exact for stride-1 convolution (up to the borders). Invariance (output does not change at all) would be a stronger property, and CNNs only get it approximately, from pooling and GAP at the end. The second part is the catch: any layer with stride 2 keeps only every second position. A shift by an odd number of pixels cannot be expressed on the coarser grid, so the output changes in a way that is not a clean shift. This is aliasing. Zhang (2019) showed that common CNNs are surprisingly sensitive to one-pixel shifts and that a small blur before each downsampling (“BlurPool”) helps both stability and accuracy. [Certain] Figure 5 shows it on a 1-D signal.

    From filters to backbones: LeNet to ResNet

    A CNN backbone is a deep stack of conv layers that turns an image into feature maps; a small head on top turns the features into the answer (class scores, boxes, masks). The history is short and worth knowing for interviews:

    ModelYearWhat it added
    LeNet-51998Conv, subsampling, conv, then fully connected layers, trained with backprop to read handwritten digits. [Certain]
    AlexNet2012A much bigger CNN trained on GPUs on ImageNet (about 1.28 million images, 1,000 classes), with ReLU and dropout. It won ILSVRC 2012 by a large margin and started the deep learning era. [Certain]
    VGG2014Only 3 × 3 convs, stacked 16 to 19 layers deep. Two 3 × 3 layers see 5 × 5 and three see 7 × 7 (formula 163.6), with fewer weights and more nonlinearities than one big filter. [Certain]
    ResNet2015Residual connections, which made 50, 101 and 152 layers trainable. An ensemble reached 3.57% top-5 error on the ImageNet test set and won ILSVRC 2015. [Certain]
    ConvNeXt2022A ResNet modernized with ViT-era choices (large depthwise kernels, LayerNorm, fewer activations); its paper reports 87.8% ImageNet top-1, competitive with transformers. [Certain]

    Why three 3 × 3 layers instead of one 7 × 7? Both have a 7 × 7 receptive field. With \(C\) channels in and out, the stack has \(3 \cdot 9 C^2 = 27C^2\) weights and the single layer \(49C^2\): the stack uses 55% of the weights and adds two more ReLUs.

    Residual connections

    Before ResNet, adding layers to a deep CNN eventually made training error go up, not only test error. That is an optimization problem, not overfitting: a deeper network could in principle copy the shallower one and set the extra layers to the identity, but gradient descent did not find that solution. He et al. made the identity the default. Each block learns only a residual, a correction added to its input:

    \[ \mathbf{y} = \operatorname{ReLU}\big(\mathbf{x} + \mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x})\big), \qquad \frac{\partial \mathbf{y}}{\partial \mathbf{x}} \approx I + \frac{\partial \mathcal{F}}{\partial \mathbf{x}} \tag{163.9} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\mathbf{x}\)
    input feature map of the block
    \(\mathcal{F}\)
    two or three conv layers with BatchNorm and ReLU; if the shape changes, the skip path uses a 1 × 1 conv with stride 2
    \(I\)
    the identity: the gradient always has a direct path back through the skip connection (ignoring the final ReLU)

    If \(\mathcal{F}\) learns nothing useful, it can output near 0 and the block passes \(\mathbf{x}\) through. The gradient flows back through the “+” without being multiplied by many weight matrices. This is exactly the residual stream of every transformer (session 22). ResNets also use BatchNorm: each channel is normalized with the mean and variance of the current batch during training, and with stored running averages at test time. [Certain]

    AnalogyA residual block is an editor, not a writer. It receives a draft and only marks changes. If the draft is already good, it changes nothing. A stack of 50 editors cannot ruin a good draft as easily as a chain of 50 people who each rewrite it from scratch.

    Inside ResNet-50

    ResNet-50 uses bottleneck blocks: a 1 × 1 conv reduces the channels by 4×, a 3 × 3 conv works on the small tensor, and a 1 × 1 conv expands again. Here is the shape of the tensor for a 224 × 224 input, measured on the torchvision model:

    StageWhatOutput shape \((C, H, W)\)Parameters
    stem7 × 7 conv, stride 2; 3 × 3 max pool, stride 264 × 56 × 569,536
    conv2_x3 bottleneck blocks256 × 56 × 56215,808
    conv3_x4 blocks, first with stride 2512 × 28 × 281,219,584
    conv4_x6 blocks, first with stride 21024 × 14 × 147,098,368
    conv5_x3 blocks, first with stride 22048 × 7 × 714,964,736
    headglobal average pool, linear 2048 → 100010002,049,000
    total50 weight layers25,557,032

    The total downsampling is 32×: a 224 input gives a 7 × 7 grid, so each final position summarizes a 32 × 32 pixel cell. Keep this 7 × 7 grid in mind: it is the CNN version of the 14 × 14 grid of patch tokens in a ViT-B/16 (next session), and early VLMs fed such grids to the language model. The paper reports 3.8 billion FLOPs (multiply-adds) for ResNet-50. [Certain] Counting every conv and the final linear layer of the torchvision version gives 4.09 billion multiply-adds. [Certain, computed]

    Transfer learning

    Training ResNet-50 from scratch needs a large labeled dataset and days of GPU time. Most real projects instead reuse a backbone pretrained on a large dataset and adapt it. This works because features are layered: early layers learn edges, colours and textures that are useful for almost any image, and only the last layers become specific to the training classes. Yosinski et al. measured this: features move from general to specific with depth. [Certain]

    The simplest form is the linear probe: keep the backbone \(h\) frozen and train only a linear layer on its output vector:

    \[ \hat{\mathbf{y}} = \softmax\big( A\, h_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}) + \mathbf{c} \big), \qquad \theta \text{ frozen}, \quad A \in \R^{K \times d} \text{ trained} \tag{163.10} \]

    For ResNet-50, \(d = 2048\). A probe for 10 classes has \(2048 \cdot 10 + 10 = 20{,}490\) trainable numbers, less than 0.1% of the backbone. Linear probes are also the standard way to compare vision encoders, because they measure how much useful information the frozen features already hold (session 171).

    Algorithm 2: choosing and running a transfer-learning recipe
    1. input: pretrained backbone \(h_\theta\), its preprocessing (resize, crop, \(\mu\), \(\sigma\)), your labeled data with \(K\) classes
    2. preprocess every image exactly as in pretraining // formulas (163.1), (163.2)
    3. replace the old head with a new linear layer \(A \in \R^{K \times d}\) // formula (163.10)
    4. if little data or very similar domain: freeze \(\theta\), train only \(A\) // linear probe; keep BatchNorm in eval mode
    5. else if medium data: first train \(A\) with \(\theta\) frozen, then unfreeze the last stage(s)
    6. else (much data or a very different domain, e.g. X-rays): fine-tune everything
    7. use a small learning rate for the backbone (often 10× smaller than for the head) // protects pretrained features
    8. evaluate on a held-out set with the same preprocessing
    Watch outBatchNorm behaves differently in train() and eval() mode. If you “freeze” a backbone but leave it in train mode, its running statistics still change with every batch, and with small batches they become noisy. Call backbone.eval() (or freeze the BatchNorm layers) when the backbone is frozen.

    What CNNs are good at, and what they miss

    The inductive biases are a trade. They help a lot when data is limited, and they limit the model when data is huge.

    Good atWhy
    Learning from modest dataLocality and weight sharing are built in, so the network does not need to discover them from examples
    Any input sizeConvolution and global pooling work on any \(H \times W\); compute grows linearly with the number of pixels
    Dense, local tasksFeature maps keep a spatial grid, which suits detection, segmentation and edge-level work
    Speed on small devicesConvolutions are very well optimized on phones and embedded chips; MobileNet-style models run in real time [Certain]
    MissesWhy
    Long-range relations (“is the cup to the left of the laptop?”)Information travels only through local windows; the effective receptive field is small and Gaussian [Certain]
    Shape over textureImageNet-trained CNNs rely strongly on texture: a cat shape with elephant skin texture is usually called an elephant (Geirhos et al.) [Certain]
    Exact shift invarianceStride and pooling cause aliasing (Figure 5)
    Scaling with dataWith very large pretraining sets, Vision Transformers match or beat CNNs of similar cost, because they assume less and learn more from data [Certain for the ViT paper’s results; the general trend is Likely]
    A token interfaceA CNN outputs a grid of feature vectors, not a sequence of tokens with positions; VLMs prefer the ViT format, which plugs straight into a transformer

    These gaps are the motivation for the next session (session 164): cut the image into patches, treat each patch as a token, and let self-attention connect any two patches in one layer. CNNs did not disappear: CLIP was released with ResNet image encoders next to its ViTs [Certain], detection and segmentation still often use CNN backbones, and some VLMs use ConvNeXt encoders for high-resolution input. [Likely]

    The PyTorch version

    All five ideas in one deterministic script: preprocessing, a hand-written convolution checked against F.conv2d, the output-size and receptive-field formulas, a residual block, and a frozen-backbone transfer-learning step. It runs on CPU in a few seconds and needs no download.

    import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F, torchvision
torch.manual_seed(0)

# 1) an image is a uint8 array of shape (H, W, 3); models want float (B, 3, H, W)
img = torch.randint(0, 256, (480, 640, 3), dtype=torch.uint8)      # stand-in for a decoded photo
mean = torch.tensor([0.485, 0.456, 0.406]).view(3, 1, 1)            # ImageNet statistics
std  = torch.tensor([0.229, 0.224, 0.225]).view(3, 1, 1)
x = img.permute(2, 0, 1).float() / 255.0                             # HWC -> CHW, [0, 255] -> [0, 1]
x = F.interpolate(x[None], size=(224, 298), mode="bilinear", antialias=True, align_corners=False)  # shorter side -> 224
left = (298 - 224) // 2
x = x[..., :, left:left + 224]                                       # center crop -> (1, 3, 224, 224)
x = (x - mean) / std                                                 # formula (163.1)
print(x.shape, round(x.mean().item(), 3))

# 2) convolution = sliding dot product; check a hand version against F.conv2d
w = torch.randn(8, 3, 3, 3)                                          # C_out=8, C_in=3, k=3
b = torch.randn(8)
y_ref = F.conv2d(x, w, b, stride=2, padding=1)
cols = F.unfold(x, kernel_size=3, padding=1, stride=2)               # (1, 3*3*3, L): every 3x3x3 window as a column
y_hand = (w.view(8, -1) @ cols + b[:, None]).view(1, 8, 112, 112)    # formula (163.3) for every position
print(y_ref.shape, (y_ref - y_hand).abs().max().item() < 1e-4)

def out_size(n, k, s, p):                                            # formula (163.4)
    return (n + 2 * p - k) // s + 1
print(out_size(224, 7, 2, 3), out_size(112, 3, 2, 1))                # ResNet stem: 112, then 56

# 3) receptive field of a stack, formula (163.6)
def receptive_field(layers):                                         # layers: list of (kernel, stride)
    r, j = 1, 1
    for k, s in layers:
        r, j = r + (k - 1) * j, j * s
    return r, j
print(receptive_field([(3, 1)] * 3))                                 # (7, 1): three 3x3 convs see 7x7

# 4) a residual block, formula (163.9)
class BasicBlock(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, c):
        super().__init__()
        self.c1, self.b1 = nn.Conv2d(c, c, 3, padding=1, bias=False), nn.BatchNorm2d(c)
        self.c2, self.b2 = nn.Conv2d(c, c, 3, padding=1, bias=False), nn.BatchNorm2d(c)
    def forward(self, x):
        h = F.relu(self.b1(self.c1(x)))
        return F.relu(x + self.b2(self.c2(h)))                       # y = x + F(x)
blk = BasicBlock(64)
print(blk(torch.randn(2, 64, 56, 56)).shape, sum(p.numel() for p in blk.parameters()))

# 5) transfer learning: freeze the backbone, train a new head
net = torchvision.models.resnet18(weights=None)   # in practice: weights=torchvision.models.ResNet18_Weights.DEFAULT
for p in net.parameters():
    p.requires_grad = False
net.fc = nn.Linear(net.fc.in_features, 5)                            # new head for 5 classes, trainable
print(sum(p.numel() for p in net.parameters() if p.requires_grad))   # 512*5 + 5 = 2565
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(net.fc.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
net.eval()                                                           # frozen BatchNorm: use its stored statistics
xb, yb = torch.randn(16, 3, 224, 224), torch.randint(0, 5, (16,))
for step in range(3):
    loss = F.cross_entropy(net(xb), yb)
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    print(step, round(loss.item(), 4))

Exact loss values can differ slightly across PyTorch versions. [Likely]

    Common mistakes

    • Wrong normalization. Using ImageNet \(\mu, \sigma\) for a CLIP or SigLIP encoder, or forgetting the division by 255. Always load the encoder’s own preprocessor config.
    • BGR instead of RGB. cv2.imread returns BGR. Convert with cv2.cvtColor(img, cv2.COLOR_BGR2RGB).
    • Wrong layout. Passing \((H, W, C)\) where \((C, H, W)\) is expected. With a 3 × 224 × 224 conv input, a 224 × 224 × 3 tensor fails loudly, but a reshape instead of a permute fails silently and scrambles the pixels.
    • Ignoring EXIF orientation. Phone photos store a rotation flag; if you do not apply it (for example with PIL.ImageOps.exif_transpose), the model sees the image sideways.
    • Center-cropping away the answer. For documents, charts and wide video frames, the important text is often at the edges. Pad or tile instead.
    • Different resize at training and serving. Bilinear without anti-aliasing in one place and PIL bicubic in another gives different inputs.
    • Frozen backbone left in train mode. BatchNorm running statistics drift; call eval() on frozen parts.
    • Confusing receptive field with understanding. A 427-pixel theoretical receptive field does not mean ResNet-50 relates opposite corners of the image; the effective field is much smaller.

    Interview questions

    Why do we normalize images, and why must we use the encoder’s own mean and standard deviation?basic
    Centred inputs with unit spread make optimization better conditioned, like any feature scaling. But once a model is trained, its first-layer weights expect inputs with exactly the statistics used in training. Using other \(\mu, \sigma\) shifts and rescales every input: for example white is +2.25 to +2.64 with ImageNet statistics but +1 with SigLIP’s. The model still runs, so the bug is silent. Use the preprocessor config shipped with the model.
    A conv layer has k = 3, stride 2, padding 1, 64 input and 128 output channels, on a 56 × 56 input. Give the output size, parameters and multiply-adds.basic
    Output side \(\lfloor (56 + 2 - 3)/2 \rfloor + 1 = 28\). Parameters \(9 \cdot 64 \cdot 128 + 128 = 73{,}856\), independent of image size. Multiply-adds \(9 \cdot 64 \cdot 128 \cdot 28 \cdot 28 = 57{,}802{,}752\), a quarter of the stride-1 version because there are four times fewer output positions.
    Why stack 3 × 3 convolutions instead of using one 7 × 7?core
    Three 3 × 3 layers have the same 7 × 7 receptive field (formula 163.6: 3, 5, 7). With \(C\) channels they cost \(27C^2\) weights versus \(49C^2\), about 55%, and they add two extra nonlinearities, so the function is more expressive. This was the main design idea of VGG and is used in ResNet.
    What is the receptive field, and how does it differ from the effective receptive field?core
    The (theoretical) receptive field is the input region that can influence a unit; it follows \(r_\ell = r_{\ell-1} + (k_\ell - 1) j_{\ell-1}\), with the jump \(j\) the product of strides. For ResNet-50 it is 427 pixels. The effective receptive field measures how much each input pixel actually changes the output (via the gradient); Luo et al. found it is roughly Gaussian and covers only a fraction of the theoretical one, dominated by the centre. So a big theoretical field does not guarantee global reasoning.
    Why do residual connections make very deep networks trainable?core
    Plain deep networks showed higher training error as depth grew, an optimization problem. With \(y = x + \mathcal{F}(x)\), the identity is the default and each block only learns a correction; the Jacobian is \(I + \partial\mathcal{F}/\partial x\), so gradients have a direct path back to early layers and do not shrink through many weight multiplications. The same idea is the residual stream of transformers.
    Are CNNs translation invariant?advanced
    Stride-1 convolution is translation equivariant (shift in, same shift out), up to border effects. Invariance comes only approximately, from pooling and global average pooling at the end. Strided convs and pooling subsample, which breaks equivariance for shifts that are not multiples of the stride (aliasing); Zhang (2019) showed one-pixel shifts can change predictions and proposed blurring before subsampling. Padding also leaks absolute position, so CNNs can partly know where things are.
    Freeze the backbone or fine-tune it? What about BatchNorm?core
    With little data or a domain close to pretraining, freeze and train a linear head (cheap, no overfitting of the backbone). With more data or a distant domain, unfreeze the last stages or everything, with a smaller learning rate for the backbone than for the head, often after first training the head alone so random head gradients do not damage the features. For frozen parts, keep BatchNorm in eval mode so its running statistics do not drift; with small batches in fine-tuning, consider freezing BN statistics too.
    You must feed 16:9 video frames to a square 224 encoder for a VLM. Crop, pad or squash?core
    Crop keeps correct shapes and full resolution of the centre but drops 43.75% of the frame (edges, captions, scoreboards). Pad keeps everything undistorted but wastes 43.75% of the input on padding and gives content fewer pixels. Squash keeps everything at full width but stretches shapes by 1.78×, which the encoder never saw in training. Choose by task: pad (or tile) when edges matter, crop when the subject is central; avoid squash for models trained with crops. Better: use an encoder that supports native aspect ratio or tiling.
    What does a 1 × 1 convolution do?basic
    It applies the same linear map to the channel vector at every pixel, with no spatial mixing: \(C_{\text{in}} C_{\text{out}}\) weights. It changes the number of channels cheaply, as in ResNet bottlenecks (1 × 1 down, 3 × 3, 1 × 1 up) and in the shortcut when shapes change. It is the convolutional version of a token-wise linear layer in a transformer.
    Why did Vision Transformers overtake CNNs, and when would you still choose a CNN?advanced
    CNNs hard-code locality and equivariance. That helps with small data but limits long-range interaction (small effective receptive field) and adds texture bias. ViTs assume less, connect any two patches in one attention layer, scale well with huge pretraining sets, and produce tokens that plug directly into LLMs, which made them the default VLM encoder. Choose a CNN for small datasets without a good pretrained ViT, for dense high-resolution tasks where linear cost in pixels matters, or for edge devices with strong conv support; modern CNNs like ConvNeXt are competitive at similar compute.
    Global average pooling vs flattening into a fully connected layer?core
    Flattening a 7 × 7 × 2048 map into an FC layer needs \(100{,}352 \times K\) weights and fixes the input size. GAP reduces it to a 2048-vector first, so the head has \(2048K\) weights, works for any input resolution and is roughly invariant to where the object is. The cost is losing spatial layout, which is why detection and VLM connectors use the grid instead.

    Key takeaways

    • An image is a \(H \times W \times 3\) uint8 array; models want float \((B, 3, H, W)\) in RGB order, normalized with the encoder’s own \(\mu, \sigma\) (163.1). ImageNet, CLIP and SigLIP use different numbers.
    • Square encoders force a choice between cropping, padding and squashing (163.2); each loses something, and small details vanish at 224 pixels.
    • A convolution is a shared sliding dot product (163.3); its output size (163.4), parameters and compute (163.5) can be computed by hand. Halving the map and doubling channels keeps compute per layer constant.
    • Receptive fields grow linearly with depth and multiply with stride (163.6); the effective field is smaller. Pooling and GAP (163.7) summarize and give approximate invariance; stride causes aliasing (163.8).
    • Residual blocks (163.9) make deep CNNs trainable; ResNet-50 has 25.6 million parameters and turns 224 × 224 into a 7 × 7 × 2048 grid.
    • Transfer learning reuses a pretrained backbone with a linear probe (163.10) or fine-tuning. The CNN’s locality bias helps with small data but limits global reasoning, which motivates the ViT.

    \[ \tilde{x} = \frac{x/255 - \mu_c}{\sigma_c}, \quad H_{\text{out}} = \left\lfloor \frac{H_{\text{in}} + 2p - k}{s} \right\rfloor + 1, \quad \text{params} = k^2 C_{\text{in}} C_{\text{out}} + C_{\text{out}} \]
