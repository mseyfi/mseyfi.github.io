    Efficient FFN 3: MoE from dense models, and MoE inference

    Training a Mixture-of-Experts model from scratch is expensive, so this session shows two ways to build one from a dense model you already have: MoEfication cuts a dense feed-forward network into groups of neurons, and sparse upcycling copies it into many experts and keeps training. Then we serve the result: we count total and active parameters, see why memory must hold every expert, and work out what batching and expert parallelism do to the speed of Mixtral 8x7B and DeepSeek-V3.

    Time: 3 to 4 hours (reading 2 hours, exercises 1 to 2 hours)Level: Intermediate to interview levelBefore this: Sessions 21 and 29 (the FFN; Mixture of Experts), session 82 (why inference is slow, the roofline); session 95 helps (MoE routing)

    What you will learn

    • Why a ReLU feed-forward network is already a sparse mixture of experts, and how MoEfication finds the experts (balanced k-means or a co-activation graph) and builds a router
    • How sparse upcycling turns a dense checkpoint into an MoE: copied experts, a new random router, and why the first step can lose most of the FFN output
    • What upcycled models cost and gain: about 50% of the dense pre-training cost in the original paper, Qwen1.5-MoE-A2.7B from Qwen-1.8B, and Branch-Train-MiX
    • Total vs active parameters with exact counts for Mixtral 8x7B (46.7B / 12.9B), Qwen1.5-MoE-A2.7B (14.3B / 2.7B) and DeepSeek-V3 (671B / 37.6B), and why memory must hold all of them
    • How the batch changes MoE decode: the number of experts touched grows fast, so bytes per step rise toward the total, while FLOPs stay at the active count
    • Expert parallelism at serving time: two all-to-all exchanges per MoE layer, stragglers from hot experts, and DeepSeek-V3's redundant experts and EP32 / EP320 deployment

    Why this matters

    Mixtral 8x7B has 46.7 billion parameters but uses only 12.9 billion for each token. Its total size is exactly Mistral 7B plus seven more copies of every feed-forward network and a tiny router per layer. This session is about that kind of relation: how to make a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model from a dense model, and what it costs to serve one.

    In session 29 you met the MoE layer: a router sends each token to \(k\) of \(E\) expert feed-forward networks (FFNs). In session 95 you saw how routing is kept balanced. Two practical questions remain, and they are the two halves of this page.

    • Where do the experts come from? Training a large MoE from scratch is expensive, and most teams already own a good dense checkpoint. Two families of methods reuse it. MoEfication cuts each dense FFN into groups of neurons and adds a router, so the model gets cheaper with no new parameters. Sparse upcycling copies each FFN into many experts and keeps training, so the model gets bigger and better for a fraction of the cost. The efficient-inference survey of Zhou et al. (section 5.1.1) lists both under “expert construction”. [Certain]
    • What does an MoE cost at serving time? FLOPs follow the active parameters, but memory must hold all parameters, and with a batch of requests almost every expert gets used. So an MoE is cheap in one cost and not in the others. Serving systems answer with large batches and expert parallelism: experts spread over many GPUs. DeepSeek-V3 decodes on 320 GPUs with one expert per GPU. [Certain]

    We use the three costs and the roofline from session 82 throughout: a step takes at least \(\max(F/P,\ Q/B)\), where \(F\) is FLOPs, \(Q\) is bytes moved, \(P\) is peak compute and \(B\) is memory bandwidth.

    Interview tipCommon questions: “What is the difference between total and active parameters, and which one sets memory, latency and throughput?”, “How would you turn a dense checkpoint into an MoE?”, “Why is MoE decode at batch 1 fast but at batch 64 not much faster than a dense model of the same total size?”, “What is expert parallelism and what can go wrong?”. This page gives a formula for each.

    A dense FFN is already a sum of small experts

    Start with the FFN of one block, as in session 21. It has \(d_{ff}\) hidden neurons. Neuron \(j\) has an input vector (column \(j\) of \(W_1\)) and an output vector (row \(j\) of \(W_2\)). Write the FFN as a sum over neurons:

    \[ F(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{j=1}^{d_{ff}} \sigma\!\left(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{w}^{(1)}_j + b_j\right)\mathbf{w}^{(2)}_j + \mathbf{b}_2 \tag{96.1} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\mathbf{x}\)
    the input vector of one token, size \(d\)
    \(\mathbf{w}^{(1)}_j,\ b_j\)
    the input weights (column \(j\) of \(W_1\)) and the bias of neuron \(j\)
    \(\mathbf{w}^{(2)}_j\)
    the output weights of neuron \(j\) (row \(j\) of \(W_2\)), size \(d\)
    \(\sigma\)
    the activation function, ReLU in the models MoEfication studied
    \(\mathbf{b}_2\)
    the output bias

    Two facts follow. First, the order of the neurons does not matter: any permutation of the terms gives the same sum. Second, with ReLU, a neuron with a negative pre-activation adds exactly zero. Zhang et al. measured how often that happens: in a fine-tuned T5-Large (700 million parameters), 90% of inputs activate less than 5% of the FFN neurons. [Certain] So most of the work of a dense FFN is multiplying by zero.

    Now cut the \(d_{ff}\) neurons into \(K\) groups of equal size \(d_e = d_{ff}/K\). Group \(i\) is a small FFN on its own, with its own slices of \(W_1\), \(b_1\) and \(W_2\). Because the order does not matter, the dense FFN is the sum of these small FFNs:

    \[ F(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^{K} E_i(\mathbf{x}) + \mathbf{b}_2, \qquad E_i(\mathbf{x}) = \sigma\!\left(\mathbf{x}W_1^{i} + \mathbf{b}_1^{i}\right)W_2^{i} \tag{96.2} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(K\)
    number of experts we cut the FFN into
    \(W_1^{i}\ (d \times d_e),\ \mathbf{b}_1^{i},\ W_2^{i}\ (d_e \times d)\)
    the columns of \(W_1\), entries of \(\mathbf{b}_1\) and rows of \(W_2\) that belong to the neurons of group \(i\)
    \(E_i\)
    expert \(i\): a small FFN with \(d_e\) hidden neurons

    This is an MoE with every expert switched on. Nothing has changed yet: same parameters, same output, same FLOPs. The saving comes when we run only the few experts that hold the active neurons for this token.

    AnalogyA big office where 4,000 employees read every letter, but for any one letter only a few hundred have anything to say. MoEfication does not hire anyone. It seats people who usually speak up together in the same room, and puts a receptionist at the door who sends each letter to the two or three right rooms.

    MoEfication: split the neurons, then build a router

    MoEfication (Zhang et al., 2021) has two phases. [Certain]

    Phase 1, expert construction. Choose the groups so that neurons which are active together land in the same expert. Then, for a given token, a few experts cover almost all of its active neurons. The paper tries two ways and a random baseline:

    • Parameter clustering split. Treat the \(d_{ff}\) input vectors \(\mathbf{w}^{(1)}_j\) as points and run balanced k-means (k-means where every cluster must have the same size). The idea: neurons with similar input vectors fire on similar inputs.
    • Co-activation graph split. Run the model on training data and count how strongly each pair of neurons is active at the same time. Then cut this graph into \(K\) equal parts with a graph-partitioning tool, keeping strongly linked neurons together.
    • Random split, the baseline: equal groups with no structure.
    \[ \text{co-act}(n, m) = \sum_{\mathbf{x}} h_n(\mathbf{x})\, h_m(\mathbf{x})\ \mathbb{1}\!\left[h_n(\mathbf{x}) \gt 0,\ h_m(\mathbf{x}) \gt 0\right] \tag{96.3} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(h_n(\mathbf{x})\)
    pre-activation of neuron \(n\) for input \(\mathbf{x}\)
    \(\mathbb{1}[\cdot]\)
    1 if both neurons are active (positive) for this input, else 0
    \(\text{co-act}(n,m)\)
    the weight of the edge between neurons \(n\) and \(m\) in the co-activation graph

    Phase 2, expert selection. For each token, pick \(n\) of the \(K\) experts. The best possible choice (the paper calls it groundtruth selection) is the experts with the largest sum of positive activations, but computing it needs the whole FFN, which is what we want to avoid. So the paper trains a small MLP router that predicts these sums from \(\mathbf{x}\), and also tries a cheap similarity selection: the cosine between \(\mathbf{x}\) and the mean input vector of each expert. The MoEfied layer is

    \[ F_m(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i \in S(\mathbf{x})} E_i(\mathbf{x}) + \mathbf{b}_2, \qquad S(\mathbf{x}) = \text{top-}n \text{ experts by score } s_i(\mathbf{x}) \tag{96.4} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(S(\mathbf{x})\)
    the set of \(n\) experts chosen for this token
    \(s_i(\mathbf{x})\)
    the score of expert \(i\): the true sum of its positive activations (ideal), a router prediction, or a cosine similarity
    \(n/K\)
    the fraction of FFN parameters used for this token

    Note the gate: there is none. Each chosen expert enters with weight 1, because it is a piece of the original sum. If \(n = K\), \(F_m = F\) exactly.

    The results, on T5 and BERT with GLUE and question-answering tasks: MoEfication uses 10% to 30% of the FFN parameters for each input and keeps over 95% of the original performance. With 25% of the FFN parameters it reports 2× faster inference on CPU and 1.2× on GPU. Experts have \(d_e = 32\) neurons, so the T5 models get 64 to 512 experts per layer. [Certain] The GPU gain is small because many small expert matrices use the GPU less well than one big one, and the router itself costs time. [Likely]

    MoEfication for modern LLMs

    MoEfication relies on exact zeros from ReLU. Most current LLMs use SwiGLU with SiLU, which gives small but non-zero values almost everywhere, so dropping neurons always changes the output a little. [Likely] Later work therefore splits the FFN and then trains the result. LLaMA-MoE splits the FFNs of LLaMA-2 7B into experts, adds gates, and continues pre-training on 200B tokens, giving a model with about 3.5B active parameters. [Certain] At that point MoEfication and upcycling meet: both start from dense weights and both need extra training.

    Algorithm 1: MoEfication of one FFN layer
    1. input: \(W_1, \mathbf{b}_1, W_2, \mathbf{b}_2\); number of experts \(K\); experts used \(n\); a sample of training inputs
    2. \(d_e \leftarrow d_{ff} / K\)
    3. \(\text{lab} \leftarrow\) BalancedKMeans(columns of \(W_1\), \(K\), size \(d_e\)) // or: partition the co-activation graph (96.3)
    4. \(P \leftarrow\) permutation that sorts neurons by \(\text{lab}\)
    5. for \(i = 1 \dots K\): \(W_1^{i}, \mathbf{b}_1^{i}, W_2^{i} \leftarrow\) slice \(i\) of \(W_1P,\ \mathbf{b}_1P,\ P^\top W_2\) // formula (96.2), exact
    6. collect targets \(t_i(\mathbf{x}) = \sum \text{ReLU}(\mathbf{x}W_1^{i} + \mathbf{b}_1^{i})\) on the sample
    7. train a small MLP router \(r(\mathbf{x}) \approx (t_1, \dots, t_K)\)
    8. at inference: \(S \leftarrow\) top-\(n\) of \(r(\mathbf{x})\); return \(\sum_{i \in S} E_i(\mathbf{x}) + \mathbf{b}_2\) // formula (96.4)

    Sparse upcycling: copy the FFN, then train

    MoEfication keeps the parameter count and saves FLOPs. Sparse upcycling (Komatsuzaki et al., 2022) does the opposite: it keeps the FLOPs per token roughly fixed and adds parameters, to get a better model than the dense one. [Certain] The recipe:

    • Copy every weight of the dense checkpoint: embeddings, attention, norms.
    • In the chosen layers, replace the FFN by an MoE layer whose \(E\) experts are all exact copies of that FFN.
    • Add a router. The dense model has none, so it starts random: normal with standard deviation 0.02.
    • Continue training on the usual data.
    \[ E_i \leftarrow F_{\text{dense}} \ \ (i = 1, \dots, E), \qquad W_r \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\ 0.02^2) \tag{96.5} \]

    In the paper: half of the MLP layers become MoE layers (every other layer, starting with the second), each with 32 experts. The language encoder and the vision models use Expert Choice routing with capacity factor 2; the language decoder uses top-2 token-choice routing with an auxiliary loss weight of 0.01, to avoid a mismatch between full-batch training and one-token decoding. Reusing the dense optimizer state helped the vision models, not the language models. [Certain]

    AnalogyA restaurant with one excellent cook. Upcycling hires seven clones of that cook and adds a head waiter who assigns orders. On day one all eight cook the same way. Because each clone now gets different orders, each one slowly gets better at its own kind of dish.

    What happens at step 0

    Since all experts are the same function, the MoE output at the first step is just the FFN output times the total gate weight of the chosen experts:

    \[ y(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i \in \text{top-}k} g_i(\mathbf{x})\, F_{\text{dense}}(\mathbf{x}) = G(\mathbf{x})\, F_{\text{dense}}(\mathbf{x}), \qquad G(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i \in \text{top-}k} g_i(\mathbf{x}) \tag{96.6} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(g_i(\mathbf{x})\)
    gate weight of expert \(i\), from the router’s softmax
    \(G(\mathbf{x})\)
    the total gate weight of the \(k\) chosen experts: the factor by which the FFN output is scaled at step 0

    If the gates are renormalized over the top-\(k\) (top-\(k\) then softmax, as in Mixtral), \(G = 1\) and the upcycled model starts as the exact dense model. If they are not (softmax over all \(E\), then top-\(k\), as in Switch and GShard), \(G\) can be far below 1. With a near-random router the softmax is close to uniform, so \(G \approx k/E\): for top-2 of 32 that is 0.0625. Router logits are not exactly zero: if the hidden vector has RMS 1 in \(d = 768\), the logits have standard deviation \(0.02\sqrt{768} = 0.55\), and \(G\) is about 0.15 (Figure 2). [Likely, depends on the hidden scale] So at step 0 the FFN contributes only about 15% of its usual output. The attention path is unchanged, so the model does not break, but quality drops. [Likely]

    The paper reports this initial quality drop and recovery with more training: more MoE layers, more experts or more capacity make the drop larger; upcycling the bottom layers hurts most. Normalizing the combine weights helped vision but slightly hurt language (SuperGLUE 79.3 to 78.8). [Certain] So renormalization is not a free win; it changes the training dynamics too.

    Why do identical copies ever become different? Because the router is random, different experts receive different tokens, so they get different gradients and drift apart. Anything that breaks the symmetry helps: the Qwen team reports that adding randomness to the copied weights made the upcycled model converge faster. [Certain]

    What upcycling buys

    The key result: upcycled T5 Base, Large and XL and Vision Transformer Base and Large beat their dense versions on SuperGLUE and ImageNet while using only about 50% of the original dense pre-training cost as extra compute. They also beat MoE models trained from scratch with 100% of that budget. [Certain] Concretely, upcycled T5-Large and T5-Base gained 1.5 to 2 SuperGLUE points over continued dense training with 46% and 55% extra training. [Certain] The paper also notes that, with a very large compute budget, a from-scratch MoE eventually catches up, and then training from scratch may be preferable. [Certain] Upcycling wins when your extra budget is small compared with what the dense model already cost.

    Real examples:

    • Qwen1.5-MoE-A2.7B was made from Qwen-1.8B by upcycling with fine-grained experts: each FFN is cut into pieces (as in MoEfication), and the pieces become experts. It has 4 shared experts that are always on and 60 routed experts of which 4 are chosen. Its config: routed experts of width 1,408, a shared FFN of width 5,632 \(= 4 \times 1{,}408\), and 24 layers like Qwen-1.8B. Totals: 14.3B parameters, 2.7B active. The team reports 75% lower training cost than Qwen1.5-7B. [Certain]
    • Mixtral 8x7B has exactly the shape of Mistral 7B with 8 FFNs per layer (\(d = 4096\), 32 layers, FFN width 14,336 in both configs). [Certain] Whether Mistral AI initialized it from Mistral 7B is not stated in the paper. [Speculation]
    • Branch-Train-MiX (BTX) copies one seed model, trains each copy on its own domain (code, math, world knowledge), then uses their FFNs as the experts of one MoE, averages the other weights, and fine-tunes the router. Sparse upcycling is the special case with no separate domain training. [Certain]

    Compare GLaM, an MoE trained from scratch: 1.2 trillion parameters, 64 experts in every other layer, top-2, so 96.6B (8%) active per token. It used about one third of the energy of GPT-3’s training and half of its inference FLOPs. [Certain] From-scratch training gives the most freedom; upcycling gives the cheapest start.

    Algorithm 2: sparse upcycling
    1. input: dense checkpoint; experts \(E\); top-\(k\); set of layers \(\mathcal{L}\) to convert (e.g. every other layer)
    2. copy all non-FFN weights (embeddings, attention, norms) // optimizer state: optional
    3. for each layer \(\ell\): if \(\ell \in \mathcal{L}\) then
    4. for \(i = 1 \dots E\): \(E_i^{\ell} \leftarrow\) copy of \(F^{\ell}_{\text{dense}}\) (+ small noise to break symmetry) // formula (96.5)
    5. \(W_r^{\ell} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.02^2)\) // new router
    6. else keep \(F^{\ell}_{\text{dense}}\)
    7. train with the usual LM loss + load-balancing loss; the paper resumes the dense learning-rate schedule where it left off
    8. check quality early: the step-0 drop depends on \(G\) (96.6) and on \(|\mathcal{L}|\), \(E\), capacity

    Serving cost 1: total vs active parameters

    Now the MoE exists, upcycled or not, and we want to serve it. Split the parameters into those used by every token (embeddings, attention, norms, routers, shared experts, dense FFN layers) and the routed experts:

    \[ N_{\text{total}} = N_{\text{other}} + L_{\text{moe}}\, E\, P_e, \qquad N_{\text{active}} = N_{\text{other}} + L_{\text{moe}}\, k\, P_e \tag{96.7} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(N_{\text{other}}\)
    parameters every token uses (including always-on shared experts)
    \(L_{\text{moe}}\)
    number of MoE layers
    \(E,\ k\)
    routed experts per layer, and how many each token uses
    \(P_e\)
    parameters of one routed expert: \(3\,d\,d_e\) for a SwiGLU expert (gate, up and down matrices)

    The three costs from session 82 split cleanly. Memory size follows \(N_{\text{total}}\): every expert must sit in some GPU’s memory, because the next token may need it. FLOPs per token follow \(N_{\text{active}}\): about \(2N_{\text{active}}\). Bytes read per decode step lie in between and depend on the batch, which is the next section.

    All counts are computed from the public configs and match the papers: Mixtral 47B / 13B, DeepSeek-V3 671B / 37B (without its extra multi-token-prediction module), Qwen 14.3B / 2.7B. [Certain] A Mixtral expert in BF16 is 352 MB; one MoE layer’s 8 experts are 2.8 GB. Note what this means: Mixtral at batch 1 does the FLOPs of a 13B model but needs the memory of a 47B model. The Mixtral paper says so directly: memory cost follows the sparse parameter count, and MoE layers suit batched workloads. [Certain]

    Watch out“8x7B” does not mean 56B. Only the FFNs are copied; attention, embeddings and norms are shared. 7.24B (Mistral 7B) plus 7 extra FFN copies per layer (\(7 \times 32 \times 176.2\)M) plus routers gives 46.70B.

    Serving cost 2: the batch decides how many experts you read

    At batch 1, a decode step reads \(N_{\text{other}}\) plus the \(k\) chosen experts in each layer: the bytes of the active parameters. That is the MoE dream: a 47B model that decodes as fast as a 13B one. But a serving system decodes a batch of \(b\) sequences per step (see session 84), and different tokens pick different experts. If routing were uniform and independent, a given expert is missed by one token with probability \(1 - k/E\), so the expected number of distinct experts touched per layer is

    \[ \bar{E}(b) = E\left(1 - \left(1 - \frac{k}{E}\right)^{b}\right) \tag{96.8} \]

    and the weight bytes read in one decode step are

    \[ Q(b) = s\left(N_{\text{other,mm}} + L_{\text{moe}}\, \bar{E}(b)\, P_e\right) \tag{96.9} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(b\)
    batch size: sequences that each make one token in this step
    \(\bar{E}(b)\)
    expected number of different experts used by the batch, in one layer
    \(s\)
    bytes per weight: 2 for BF16, 1 for FP8
    \(N_{\text{other,mm}}\)
    always-used parameters that are multiplied (the input embedding is only a lookup, so it is left out)

    For Mixtral (\(E = 8, k = 2\)): \(\bar{E}(4) = 5.47\), \(\bar{E}(8) = 7.20\), \(\bar{E}(16) = 7.92\). By batch 16 nearly every expert is read in every layer, so the step reads almost all 93 GB, like a dense 47B model. For DeepSeek-V3 (\(E = 256, k = 8\)) it takes longer, but at batch 64 already 222 of 256 experts are touched per layer. Real routing is not uniform (some experts are hot), which lowers \(\bar{E}\) a bit but creates imbalance (later section). [Likely]

    The FLOPs, however, stay at \(2bN_{\text{active}}\). So the arithmetic intensity of the expert matrices is set by the tokens per expert, about \(bk/E\) once all experts are touched:

    \[ I_{\text{expert}} \approx \frac{2\,(b k / E)}{s}, \qquad b^{*} \approx \frac{E}{k} \cdot \frac{s\, I^{*}}{2} \tag{96.10} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(bk/E\)
    average number of tokens each expert processes in one step
    \(I^{*} = P/B\)
    the ridge point of the GPU: FLOPs per byte needed to be compute-bound
    \(b^{*}\)
    batch at which the experts become compute-bound: \(E/k\) times the dense model’s value

    A dense BF16 model on an H100 reaches the ridge (\(I^{*} = 295\) FLOP/byte) near batch 295 (from session 82). Mixtral needs \(E/k = 4\) times more, about 1,181. DeepSeek-V3 in FP8 (ridge 591 FLOP/byte for dense FP8 on an H100) needs about 9,450 sequences in one step. This is why MoE serving is all about very large batches, gathered across many GPUs. [Certain, as arithmetic]

    In Figure 3 the whole step turns compute-bound a little earlier (about 1,080 sequences for Mixtral, 1,740 for Qwen1.5-MoE and 5,400 for DeepSeek-V3), because the attention and shared weights, which every token uses, reach the ridge first and their FLOPs count too. The experts on their own still sit below the ridge until \(b^{*}\).

    Worked example: Mixtral 8x7B decode on 2 H100 SXM (BF16, weights only)

    Fit. 93.4 GB of weights on 160 GB of HBM leaves 66.6 GB for the KV cache and activations.

    Batch 1. Bytes read: \(2 \times (12.88 - 0.13)\) B \(= 25.5\) GB (the input embedding of 0.13B is a lookup). Bandwidth 6.7 TB/s, so at least 3.81 ms per token, 263 tokens/s. A dense 47B model would need 13.90 ms. MoE wins by 3.6×.

    Batch 8. \(\bar{E}(8) = 8(1 - 0.75^8) = 7.20\) experts per layer. Bytes: 84.1 GB, 12.55 ms per step, 637 tokens/s in total. The MoE advantage over a dense 47B model is almost gone in bytes.

    Batch 32. All 8 experts read: 93.1 GB, 13.90 ms, 2,302 tokens/s. Same time as a dense 47B model, but the FLOPs (0.41 ms of compute) are 3.6× smaller.

    Batch 1,024. Compute time \(2 \times 1024 \times 12.75\text{B} / 1978\) TFLOP/s \(= 13.2\) ms, close to the memory time of 13.9 ms: near the ridge, about 73,700 tokens/s. A dense 47B model would already be compute-bound here at 3.6× the FLOPs, so the MoE gives 3.6× the peak throughput.

    Lesson. MoE helps latency at batch 1 (fewer bytes) and throughput at huge batches (fewer FLOPs). In between, it is a 47B model in bytes.

    Serving cost 3: expert parallelism and its traffic

    Large MoEs do not fit one GPU, so the experts must be spread out. Two ways:

    • Tensor parallelism (session 48) cuts every expert matrix across GPUs. Every GPU does a slice of every expert. Simple, but each expert’s already small matrices get even smaller.
    • Expert parallelism (EP) puts whole experts on different GPUs: with \(G\) GPUs, each holds \(E/G\) experts. Attention usually runs data-parallel (each GPU on its own requests). In each MoE layer, tokens travel to the GPUs that hold their experts and the results travel back: two all-to-all exchanges (session 43, session 49).

    The bytes each token sends per MoE layer, if all \(k\) experts are on other GPUs:

    \[ Q_{\text{a2a}} \approx k\, d\,\left(s_{\text{dispatch}} + s_{\text{combine}}\right) \tag{96.11} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(d\)
    hidden size: each copy of a token is one \(d\)-vector
    \(s_{\text{dispatch}},\ s_{\text{combine}}\)
    bytes per number for sending tokens out and results back

    Mixtral in BF16: \(2 \times 4096 \times 4 = 32{,}768\) bytes per token per layer, 1 MiB per token over 32 layers. DeepSeek-V3 sends tokens in FP8 and results in BF16 [Certain]: \(8 \times 7168 \times 3 = 172{,}032\) bytes per token per layer, about 10 MB per token over 58 layers. That is an upper bound: DeepSeek-V3 limits each token to experts on at most 4 nodes, which cuts the slow cross-node part. [Certain about the limit; the size of the saving depends on the routing, Likely]

    The other problem is imbalance. Each MoE layer waits for the slowest GPU. If some experts are popular (“hot”), their GPU gets more tokens and everyone else waits:

    \[ t_{\text{layer}} \propto \max_{g} \ \text{load}_g, \qquad \text{imbalance} = \frac{\max_g \text{load}_g}{\tfrac{1}{G}\sum_g \text{load}_g} \tag{96.12} \]

    Load-balancing losses act during training (session 95), but live traffic differs from training data. The Mixtral paper also shows that consecutive tokens often go to the same expert in higher layers (61.6% to 67.0% for “first or second choice” at layer 15, against about 46% if random), and warns that this locality can over-subscribe some experts under EP. [Certain]

    DeepSeek-V3’s answer is redundant experts. It watches expert load online and gives the hottest experts a second copy on another GPU; tokens for that expert are split between the copies. In prefill, the unit is 4 nodes with 32 GPUs, attention TP4 with DP8, MoE EP32, and 32 redundant experts (each GPU holds its 8 experts plus 1 redundant one), re-chosen about every 10 minutes. In decode, the unit is 40 nodes with 320 GPUs, MoE EP320: each GPU holds only one expert, and 64 GPUs host the redundant and shared experts. To hide the all-to-all, it overlaps the attention of one micro-batch with the dispatch, MoE and combine of another. [Certain]

    Other tricks at serving time

    • Offloading for one user. On a single small GPU, keep most experts in CPU memory and copy the needed ones in. Because consecutive tokens often reuse experts, a cache of recently used experts on the GPU helps (Eliseev and Mazur, cited by the Mixtral paper). [Certain] See session 130.
    • Quantize the experts. Experts are 87% to 97% of the bytes (table above), so expert weights are the first place to quantize; DeepSeek-V3 already ships FP8 weights. [Certain]
    • Separate prefill and decode. Prefill has many tokens per expert (compute-bound); decode has few (memory-bound). DeepSeek-V3 uses different units for the two, which is prefill–decode disaggregation (session 137). [Certain]
    • Grouped GEMM kernels. Run all experts of a layer in one kernel call over variable-size groups of tokens, instead of a loop of small matrix multiplies (MegaBlocks style, see session 29). [Certain]
    Algorithm 3: one MoE layer in an expert-parallel decode step
    1. input: on each GPU \(g\): hidden states \(X_g\) of its own requests; expert placement \(\text{host}(e)\) (with redundant copies)
    2. \(\text{scores} \leftarrow\) router\((X_g)\); \(\text{idx}, w \leftarrow\) top-\(k\)(scores) // local, cheap
    3. for each (token, expert \(e\)): pick a copy of \(e\) (the least-loaded host) // redundant experts
    4. all-to-all dispatch: send each token copy to its host GPU // FP8 in DeepSeek-V3, formula (96.11)
    5. on each GPU: grouped GEMM over its experts with the tokens it received // \(bk/E\) tokens per expert
    6. all-to-all combine: send results back to the token’s home GPU
    7. \(y \leftarrow \sum_j w_j \cdot \text{result}_j\) (+ shared expert output) // layer time ∝ max load, (96.12)
    8. overlap: while micro-batch A is in steps 4 to 6, run attention of micro-batch B
    9. log per-expert token counts; every few minutes recompute which experts get redundant copies

    In code

    A runnable, deterministic PyTorch script with three parts: MoEfy a small ReLU FFN and measure the error for each number of experts; upcycle a SwiGLU FFN and check the step-0 output with and without renormalization; count Mixtral from its config and apply formula (96.8).

    import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F
torch.manual_seed(0)

# ---------- Part 1: MoEfication of a ReLU FFN ----------
d, dff, k_exp = 16, 64, 8            # 8 experts of 8 neurons each
de = dff // k_exp
topics = F.normalize(torch.randn(4, d), dim=1)
owner = torch.arange(dff) % 4        # each neuron "belongs" to one topic
W1 = 2.0 * topics[owner].T + 0.3 * torch.randn(d, dff)   # (d, dff)
b1 = -torch.ones(dff)
W2 = torch.randn(dff, d) / dff ** 0.5
b2 = torch.zeros(d)
def ffn(x):                           # dense FFN, eq. (96.1)
    return F.relu(x @ W1 + b1) @ W2 + b2

def balanced_kmeans(V, k, iters=20):  # V: (n, d) rows = W1 columns
    n = V.shape[0]; size = n // k
    C = V[torch.randperm(n)[:k]].clone()
    for _ in range(iters):
        dist = torch.cdist(V, C)                       # (n, k)
        order = dist.flatten().argsort()
        lab = torch.full((n,), -1); cnt = torch.zeros(k, dtype=torch.long)
        for idx in order.tolist():
            i, c = divmod(idx, k)
            if lab[i] < 0 and cnt[c] < size:
                lab[i] = c; cnt[c] += 1
        C = torch.stack([V[lab == c].mean(0) for c in range(k)])
    return lab

def split(lab):                       # eq. (96.2): permute, then cut
    perm = torch.cat([torch.nonzero(lab == c).flatten() for c in range(k_exp)])
    return (W1[:, perm].reshape(d, k_exp, de), b1[perm].reshape(k_exp, de),
            W2[perm].reshape(k_exp, de, d))

def moefied(x, n, W1e, b1e, W2e):     # eq. (96.4) with ideal selection
    a = F.relu(torch.einsum('bd,dke->bke', x, W1e) + b1e)   # (B, k, de)
    score = a.sum(-1)                                     # sum of positive values
    keep = torch.zeros_like(score).scatter(1, score.topk(n, dim=1).indices, 1.0)
    return torch.einsum('bke,ked->bd', a * keep[..., None], W2e) + b2

clustered = split(balanced_kmeans(W1.T, k_exp))
random_sp = split(torch.randperm(dff) % k_exp)
x = topics[torch.arange(256) % 4] + 0.3 * torch.randn(256, d)
y = ffn(x)
print("active neurons per input: %.1f of %d" % ((x @ W1 + b1 > 0).float().sum(1).mean(), dff))
for n in [8, 4, 2, 1]:
    e_c = ((moefied(x, n, *clustered) - y).norm(dim=1) / y.norm(dim=1)).mean()
    e_r = ((moefied(x, n, *random_sp) - y).norm(dim=1) / y.norm(dim=1)).mean()
    print(f"experts {n}/8 ({n/8:.1%} of FFN): rel. error clustered {e_c:.3f}, random {e_r:.3f}")

# ---------- Part 2: sparse upcycling of a SwiGLU FFN ----------
class SwiGLU(nn.Module):
    def __init__(s, d, f):
        super().__init__(); s.g = nn.Linear(d, f, bias=False)
        s.u = nn.Linear(d, f, bias=False); s.o = nn.Linear(f, d, bias=False)
    def forward(s, x): return s.o(F.silu(s.g(x)) * s.u(x))

class UpcycledMoE(nn.Module):
    def __init__(s, dense, E, k, renorm=True):
        super().__init__()
        d = dense.g.in_features
        s.experts = nn.ModuleList([SwiGLU(d, dense.g.out_features) for _ in range(E)])
        for e in s.experts: e.load_state_dict(dense.state_dict())   # copy, eq. (96.5)
        s.router = nn.Linear(d, E, bias=False)
        nn.init.normal_(s.router.weight, std=0.02)                   # new, random
        s.k, s.renorm = k, renorm
    def forward(s, x):
        p = s.router(x).softmax(-1)
        w, idx = p.topk(s.k, dim=-1)
        if s.renorm: w = w / w.sum(-1, keepdim=True)
        y = torch.zeros_like(x)
        for j in range(s.k):
            for e in range(len(s.experts)):
                m = idx[:, j] == e
                if m.any(): y[m] += w[m, j, None] * s.experts[e](x[m])
        return y

dense = SwiGLU(64, 256)
xs = torch.randn(512, 64)
with torch.no_grad():
    yd = dense(xs)
    for renorm in [True, False]:
        moe = UpcycledMoE(dense, E=8, k=2, renorm=renorm)
        ym = moe(xs)
        ratio = (ym.norm(dim=1) / yd.norm(dim=1)).mean()
        print(f"renorm={renorm}: max |moe-dense| = {(ym-yd).abs().max():.2e}, output/dense = {ratio:.3f}")

# ---------- Part 3: serving math for Mixtral 8x7B (config.json) ----------
d, L, V, ff, E, k = 4096, 32, 32000, 14336, 8, 2
expert = 3 * d * ff
other = 2 * V * d + d + L * (2 * d * d + 2 * d * 1024 + 2 * d + d * E)
total, active = other + L * E * expert, other + L * k * expert
print(f"total {total/1e9:.2f}B  active {active/1e9:.2f}B  BF16 weights {2*total/1e9:.1f} GB")
for b in [1, 4, 16, 64]:
    touched = E * (1 - (1 - k / E) ** b)      # eq. (96.8)
    print(f"batch {b:3d}: experts touched per layer {touched:.2f} of {E}")

    Common mistakes

    • Sizing GPUs by active parameters. Memory must hold every expert: Mixtral needs 93.4 GB in BF16, not 26 GB.
    • Expecting batch-1 speed at batch 32. By formula (96.8), a batch of 16 touches 7.9 of Mixtral’s 8 experts per layer; bytes per step approach the total.
    • Thinking upcycling is free. The step-0 output can be a small fraction of the dense one (96.6), and the model needs real extra training (about 50% of the dense budget in the paper) to pay off.
    • Copying experts without breaking symmetry. Identical experts with the same tokens stay identical. The random router breaks it; small noise on the copies helps more.
    • MoEfying a SwiGLU model and expecting ReLU results. SiLU gives few exact zeros, so dropping experts always costs accuracy unless you retrain.
    • Counting “8x7B” as 56B. Only the FFNs are copied; it is 46.7B.
    • Ignoring the slowest GPU in EP. The layer waits for the busiest GPU (96.12); average load says nothing about time.
    • Forgetting the all-to-all. Each MoE layer moves about \(k\,d\,(s_d + s_c)\) bytes per token twice across GPUs; on slow links this can exceed the expert compute.

    Interview questions

    What is the difference between total and active parameters, and what does each one determine?basic
    Total parameters are all weights, including every expert; active parameters are those one token uses (shared parts plus \(k\) experts per MoE layer), formula (96.7). Memory size follows the total, FLOPs per token follow the active count, and bytes read per decode step lie in between and grow with the batch. Mixtral 8x7B: 46.7B total, 12.9B active.
    What is MoEfication, and why does it work on T5 but less well on LLaMA-style models?core
    It rewrites a dense FFN as a sum of neuron groups (exact, by permuting neurons), groups co-active neurons with balanced k-means or a co-activation graph, and trains a router to pick the few groups that hold the active neurons. With ReLU most neurons output exactly 0 (90% of inputs activate under 5% of neurons in T5-Large), so skipping groups loses little: 10% to 30% of FFN parameters keep over 95% of performance. SwiGLU/SiLU outputs are rarely exactly zero, so skipping always loses something; methods like LLaMA-MoE split and then continue pre-training.
    Describe sparse upcycling step by step.basic
    Take a dense checkpoint, copy all weights, replace the FFN in some layers (every other one in the paper) by an MoE whose experts are copies of that FFN, add a randomly initialized router (std 0.02), and continue training with the LM loss plus a load-balancing loss. Optimizer state can be reused (it helped vision, not language). The paper used 32 experts, Expert Choice in encoders and top-2 in decoders.
    Why does an upcycled model lose quality at step 0, and how can you avoid it?core
    All experts equal the dense FFN, so the MoE output is \(G(\mathbf{x})\) times the dense FFN output (96.6), where \(G\) is the total gate weight of the chosen experts. With softmax over all experts and then top-\(k\), and a near-uniform random router, \(G \approx k/E\) (0.0625 for top-2 of 32). Renormalizing the top-\(k\) gates makes \(G = 1\) and the start exact. Other factors (more MoE layers, more experts, converting bottom layers) also enlarge the drop, and the paper found renormalization helped vision but slightly hurt language.
    How do identical expert copies become specialized?core
    The router is random, so each expert receives a different subset of tokens, gets different gradients and drifts apart; the router in turn learns to send tokens to the experts that handle them best. Adding small noise to the copies (reported by the Qwen team to speed convergence), or fine-grained splitting of the FFN into different pieces, breaks the symmetry faster.
    Estimate the decode time per token of Mixtral 8x7B at batch 1 on 2 H100s.core
    Bytes read ≈ active multiplied parameters × 2 bytes = \(2 \times 12.75\text{B} = 25.5\) GB. Combined bandwidth 6.7 TB/s gives about 3.8 ms per token, about 260 tokens/s, as a lower bound ignoring KV cache and communication. A dense 47B model would need about 13.9 ms.
    Why is MoE decode at moderate batch sizes not much faster than a dense model of the same total size?advanced
    Different tokens choose different experts. Under uniform routing, the batch touches \(E(1-(1-k/E)^b)\) experts per layer (96.8): for Mixtral, 7.2 of 8 at batch 8. Decode is memory-bound, so step time follows bytes read, which approach the total parameters. FLOPs stay at the active count, so the MoE advantage comes back only when the batch is large enough to be compute-bound: about \(E/k\) times the dense ridge batch (96.10), roughly 1,200 for Mixtral on H100.
    What is expert parallelism, and what are its two main costs?core
    Whole experts are placed on different GPUs (\(E/G\) each); tokens are sent to the GPUs holding their experts and back, with two all-to-all exchanges per MoE layer. Costs: (1) communication, about \(k\,d\,(s_d+s_c)\) bytes per token per layer (96.11), 32 KiB for Mixtral in BF16; (2) imbalance: the layer waits for the busiest GPU (96.12), and hot experts make that GPU a straggler.
    How does DeepSeek-V3 deploy its MoE for inference?advanced
    Prefill: units of 4 nodes / 32 GPUs, attention TP4 with SP and DP8, MoE EP32, 32 redundant experts (each GPU has its 8 experts plus one redundant), chosen from online load statistics about every 10 minutes. Decode: 40 nodes / 320 GPUs, EP320 with one expert per GPU, and 64 GPUs for redundant and shared experts. Dispatch is in FP8, combine in BF16; tokens go to at most 4 nodes; attention of one micro-batch overlaps the all-to-all and MoE of another.
    Upcycling or training an MoE from scratch: how do you choose?advanced
    Upcycle when you have a good dense checkpoint and an extra budget that is small compared with its pre-training cost: the paper shows upcycled models beat both dense continuation and from-scratch MoEs at about 50% extra compute. With a budget close to or above the original cost, a from-scratch MoE catches up and has more freedom (expert sizes, number of layers, router design). Upcycling also locks in the dense shape (width, depth, tokenizer); fine-grained upcycling (Qwen1.5-MoE) or BTX relax some of that.
    Your Mixtral service has p99 latency spikes only under heavy load. What MoE-specific causes would you check?advanced
    (1) Expert imbalance under EP: hot experts make one GPU the straggler; log per-expert token counts per layer and add redundant copies or rebalance placement. (2) All-to-all congestion across nodes at large batch. (3) KV memory pressure: weights take 93 GB, so the batch is capped and requests queue. (4) Prefill of long prompts mixed into decode batches; consider chunked prefill or separate prefill and decode pools.

    Key takeaways

    • A dense FFN is exactly a sum of neuron groups (96.2). MoEfication groups co-active neurons and adds a router (96.4): 10% to 30% of FFN parameters keep over 95% of quality for ReLU models.
    • Sparse upcycling copies the FFN into \(E\) experts and adds a random router (96.5). At step 0 the FFN output is scaled by \(G\) (96.6); renormalized top-\(k\) gates give an exact start. About 50% extra compute beat dense continuation in the paper.
    • Memory follows total parameters, FLOPs follow active ones (96.7): Mixtral 46.7B / 12.9B, Qwen1.5-MoE 14.3B / 2.7B, DeepSeek-V3 671B / 37.6B.
    • A decode batch touches \(E(1-(1-k/E)^b)\) experts per layer (96.8), so bytes per step rise quickly toward the total; the MoE’s FLOP advantage pays off only at very large batches, about \(E/k\) times the dense ridge batch (96.10).
    • Expert parallelism adds two all-to-all exchanges per MoE layer (96.11) and waits for the busiest GPU (96.12); redundant copies of hot experts, as in DeepSeek-V3, cut the straggler effect.
