    Distributed serving and prefill–decode disaggregation

    A large model is served by many GPUs at once. This session shows how tensor and pipeline parallelism split one model for serving, why the two phases of generation (prefill and decode) fight when they share a GPU, and how Splitwise, DistServe and TetriInfer give each phase its own GPUs and move the KV cache between them. It ends with SpotServe, which serves on cheap cloud GPUs that can be taken away at any time.

    Time: 3 to 4 hours (reading 2 hours, exercises 1 to 2 hours)Level: Intermediate to interview levelBefore this: The generation loop: prefill and decode (session 78), The KV cache (session 80), Collective communication (session 43), Batching and scheduling (sessions 135 and 136)

    What you will learn

    • Tensor-parallel and pipeline-parallel serving: what each costs per token, and why tensor parallelism inside a node is the default for latency
    • Why prefill and decode interfere on one GPU, measured as stalls in time per output token
    • Goodput, the queueing view of TTFT from DistServe, and how to choose the ratio of prefill GPUs to decode GPUs
    • Splitwise, DistServe and TetriInfer: what each one adds (cheaper hardware for decode, placement search, chunked prefill and length prediction)
    • How large the KV cache is, how long it takes to move, and why layer-wise transfer hides most of the cost
    • SpotServe: re-planning the parallel layout when a spot instance is taken away, device mapping as bipartite matching, and token-level recovery
    • When disaggregation helps and when it does not

    Why this matters

    Llama 3.1 70B in BF16 is 141 GB of weights. One H100 has 80 GB. So the smallest way to serve it at full precision is already a small distributed system: at least two GPUs that must talk to each other for every token. Big chat services use thousands of GPUs, and the way those GPUs are grouped decides the cost per token and the latency users feel.

    This session answers three questions that come up in every serving design review and in many interviews:

    • How do we split one model over several GPUs for serving? Tensor parallelism and pipeline parallelism, which you met for training in session 48 and session 49, behave differently at inference time. Tensor parallelism makes each token faster. Pipeline parallelism does not; it only adds capacity.
    • Should the two phases of generation share GPUs? Prefill (reading the prompt) is compute-bound; decode (writing one token at a time) is memory-bound (session 78). When they share a GPU, a long prefill stalls every running decode. Splitwise (Patel et al., 2023), DistServe (Zhong et al., 2024) and TetriInfer (Hu et al., 2024) put them on different GPUs. This is called prefill–decode disaggregation, or PD disaggregation. [Certain]
    • What does it cost to move the KV cache, and can we serve on cheap GPUs that may disappear? After prefill, the KV cache must travel to the decode GPU. And cloud providers sell spare GPUs (spot instances) at large discounts but can take them back with little warning. SpotServe (Miao et al., 2023) serves LLMs on such GPUs. [Certain]

    The efficient-inference survey of Zhou et al. lists these systems under serving-system optimization, as “distributed systems”. [Certain] The ideas are now in production: Moonshot AI’s Kimi service runs on Mooncake, a disaggregated design, and DeepSeek-V3 is deployed with separate prefill and decode units (32 GPUs per prefill unit, 320 GPUs per decode unit). [Certain] vLLM, SGLang, TensorRT-LLM and NVIDIA Dynamo all offer disaggregated serving. [Likely]

    Interview tipExpect: “TP or PP for serving a 70B model, and why?”, “What is PD disaggregation and when does it help?”, “How big is the KV cache you must move, and how long does it take?”, “What is goodput?”, “How would you serve on spot GPUs?”. Each one is answered below with a formula and numbers.

    Two phases that want different machines

    Recall the two phases (session 78, session 81):

    • Prefill runs the whole prompt (\(s\) tokens) through the model at once. It does about \(2N\) FLOPs per token for \(N\) weights, so it is a large matrix multiply. It is compute-bound: the GPU’s arithmetic units are the limit. It produces the first output token and the KV cache of the prompt. Its latency is the time to first token (TTFT).
    • Decode makes one token per step for every running request. Each step reads all weights and the KV cache of every request in the batch, but does little arithmetic per byte. It is memory-bound. Its speed is the time per output token (TPOT), also called inter-token latency (ITL).

    The two phases also like different settings. Prefill wants many GPUs per request to cut TTFT, and gains little from batching (one long prompt already fills the GPU). Decode wants big batches, because the weights are read once per step for the whole batch, and a lot of memory for the KV cache. Splitwise measured both on real GPUs: prompt throughput stops growing above about 2,048 batched tokens, while token throughput keeps growing with batch size up to 64, where memory runs out; and capping a DGX-H100’s power from 700 W to 350 W hurts prompt latency a lot but token latency almost not at all. [Certain]

    Interference

    Modern servers use continuous batching (session 135): new requests join the running batch at any step. With the simplest policy, when a new prompt arrives the next step runs its prefill, and all decoding requests wait for it. A 2,048-token prefill of Llama 3.1 8B takes about 69 ms on an H100 (at an assumed 50% of peak FLOPs), while a decode step takes about 5 to 6 ms. So every new prompt makes every running user see a pause of more than 10 decode steps. TetriInfer measured this kind of interference: one heavy prefill in the batch made a light decode step 5× slower, and decode slowed prefill up to about 2.5×. [Certain]

    Chunked prefill (session 135, Sarathi-Serve) softens this by cutting the prompt into chunks and mixing a chunk into each decode step. Disaggregation removes it: prefill GPUs only prefill, decode GPUs only decode. Figure 1 compares two GPUs used both ways.

    AnalogyA restaurant kitchen has one stove. Some orders are big catering trays (prefill: a lot of work at once), most are small plates that must go out every few minutes (decode). If the same cook does both, every catering tray makes all the small-plate customers wait. Disaggregation gives the trays their own cook. The new problem: you must pick how many cooks of each kind to hire, and carry the half-finished food (the KV cache) from one station to the other.

    Spreading one model over many GPUs

    Before we separate the phases, we need the tools to put one copy of the model on several GPUs. Inside a model copy there are two main ways; across copies there is the trivial third one.

    • Data parallelism (replicas). Copy the whole model to several GPU groups and send each request to one copy. No communication between copies. It adds throughput, not speed per request.
    • Tensor parallelism (TP). Split every weight matrix over \(t\) GPUs, as in Megatron-LM (session 48). Each GPU holds \(1/t\) of every layer and computes \(1/t\) of every matrix multiply. After the attention block and after the MLP block, the partial results are summed with an all-reduce (session 43): two all-reduces per layer. [Certain]
    • Pipeline parallelism (PP). Give each of \(p\) GPUs a consecutive group of layers (a stage). A token’s hidden state flows from stage to stage with point-to-point sends. Communication is small, so PP works even over slow links between machines.

    In decode, a step is memory-bound, so its time is about “bytes read divided by memory bandwidth”. TP divides the bytes per GPU by \(t\) and adds communication:

    \[ t_{\text{step}}^{\text{TP}} \approx \frac{W + B\,c\,\kappa}{t \cdot B_{\text{hbm}}} \;+\; 2L\left(\alpha + \frac{2(t-1)}{t}\cdot\frac{B\,h\,b}{B_{\text{link}}}\right) \tag{137.1} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(W\)
    bytes of all weights (141 GB for Llama 3.1 70B in BF16)
    \(B, c\)
    number of sequences in the batch, and their average context length (tokens)
    \(\kappa\)
    KV-cache bytes per token for the whole model (equation 137.6 below; 320 KiB for Llama 3.1 70B)
    \(t\)
    tensor-parallel degree (number of GPUs that share each layer)
    \(B_{\text{hbm}}\)
    memory bandwidth of one GPU (3.35 TB/s for an H100 SXM)
    \(L, h, b\)
    number of layers, hidden size, bytes per number (2 for BF16)
    \(\alpha\)
    fixed start-up time of one all-reduce (a few microseconds; we assume 10 µs inside a node)
    \(B_{\text{link}}\)
    bandwidth of the GPU-to-GPU link per direction (about 450 GB/s for H100 NVLink, about 50 GB/s for a 400 Gb/s InfiniBand port)

    The first term is the useful work. The second term is the price of TP: \(2L = 160\) all-reduces per token for a 70B model. Each one moves only \(B \cdot h \cdot 2\) bytes (16 KB per sequence), so at small batches the start-up time \(\alpha\) dominates: \(160 \times 10\) µs = 1.6 ms per token, whatever the batch. That is fine inside a node with NVLink, and bad across nodes, where \(\alpha\) is larger and the link 9× slower.

    PP has no all-reduce, but it does not make one token faster: the token must pass through all \(p\) stages one after the other. If \(B\) sequences are split into \(p\) micro-batches so that all stages stay busy, each GPU reads its weights once per micro-batch, so for the same \(B\) the weights are read \(p\) times more often than with TP. In the memory-bound regime PP therefore gives about \(p\) times lower throughput than TP for the same number of sequences; it catches up only when the micro-batches are large enough to be compute-bound. Figure 2 lets you check this.

    Parallelism and queueing: the DistServe view of TTFT

    For prefill there is one more effect: requests wait in a queue. DistServe models a prefill instance as a queue with random (Poisson) arrivals at rate \(R\) and a fixed service time \(D\) per request (an M/D/1 queue). Its average TTFT is the service time plus the average waiting time: [Certain]

    \[ \overline{\text{TTFT}}_{1} = D + \frac{R D^2}{2(1 - R D)} \tag{137.2} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(D\)
    time to prefill one request on one GPU
    \(R\)
    arrival rate of requests (per second); the queue is stable only if \(R D \lt 1\)

    Now give the instance a second GPU. With 2-stage pipeline parallelism (“inter-op” in the paper) each stage needs \(D/2\), a request still needs \(D\) in total, but two requests can be in flight. With 2-way tensor parallelism (“intra-op”) one request takes \(D/K\), where \(1 \lt K \lt 2\) is the real speed-up (communication makes it less than 2). DistServe derives: [Certain]

    \[ \overline{\text{TTFT}}_{\text{inter}} = D + \frac{R D^2}{4(2 - R D)}, \qquad \overline{\text{TTFT}}_{\text{intra}} = \frac{D}{K} + \frac{R D^2}{2K(K - R D)} \tag{137.3} \]

    At low load, waiting is small, and TP wins because it shortens \(D\). At high load, waiting dominates, and PP wins because it can accept up to \(2/D\) requests per second while TP accepts only \(K/D\). The best choice depends on the traffic, not only on the model. Figure 3 draws the three curves.

    Disaggregation: prefill GPUs and decode GPUs

    In a colocated system (vLLM’s default, for example), every GPU group runs both phases. In a disaggregated system there are two pools. A request first goes to a prefill instance, which computes the prompt’s KV cache and the first token. Then the KV cache is sent to a decode instance, which generates the rest. Each pool can use its own parallelism, batch size and even its own GPU type.

    Algorithm 1: the life of a request with PD disaggregation
    1. input: a request (prompt of \(s\) tokens, output limit); a prefill pool and a decode pool
    2. router picks a prefill instance // least loaded, or the one with the longest cached prefix
    3. router (or the prefill instance) picks a decode instance and reserves KV memory for \(s\) + expected output tokens
    4. prefill instance runs the prompt; after each layer \(\ell\), it starts sending that layer’s KV blocks to the decode instance // layer-wise transfer, overlapped with layer \(\ell+1\)
    5. prefill instance samples the first token and streams it to the user // TTFT ends here
    6. when all KV blocks have arrived, the decode instance adds the request to its running batch
    7. decode instance generates tokens until end-of-sequence; frees the KV blocks // TPOT is set here only

    Two properties make this attractive. First, no interference: a decode step never waits for a prefill. Second, independent tuning: you can give prefill high TP to cut TTFT and give decode a big batch to raise throughput. The vLLM documentation lists exactly these two benefits and adds a warning: “Disaggregated prefill DOES NOT improve throughput.” [Certain] The gain is in meeting latency targets, and that needs a better metric than raw throughput.

    Goodput

    A service has a service-level objective (SLO), for example “TTFT under 0.25 s and TPOT under 0.1 s for 90% of requests”. DistServe defines per-GPU goodput as “the maximum request rate that can be served adhering to the SLO attainment goal (say, 90%) for each GPU provisioned”. [Certain] Throughput counts every finished request; goodput counts only requests served fast enough, divided by the number of GPUs.

    With \(n_P\) prefill instances that can each serve \(r_P\) requests/s within the TTFT target, and \(n_D\) decode instances that can each serve \(r_D\) within the TPOT target, the system serves

    \[ \text{goodput per GPU} = \frac{\min(n_P\, r_P,\; n_D\, r_D)}{n_P\, g_P + n_D\, g_D}, \qquad \text{best ratio: } \frac{n_P}{n_D} \approx \frac{r_D}{r_P} \tag{137.4} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(r_P, r_D\)
    goodput of one prefill instance and one decode instance (requests/s that meet their own SLO)
    \(g_P, g_D\)
    GPUs per prefill instance and per decode instance
    \(\min\)
    the slower pool sets the rate: requests cannot leave decode faster than they enter it
    Worked example: the DistServe motivating case

    DistServe’s first experiment: a 13B model on one A100 80 GB, prompts of 512 tokens, 64 output tokens. A colocated GPU meets the SLOs up to about 1.6 requests/s. A GPU that only prefills reaches 5.6 requests/s; a GPU that only decodes reaches 10. [Certain]

    With 2 prefill GPUs and 1 decode GPU: \(\min(2 \times 5.6,\ 1 \times 10) = \min(11.2,\ 10) = 10\) requests/s on 3 GPUs, which is 3.33 requests/s per GPU, or \(3.33 / 1.6 = 2.1\times\) the colocated system. The ratio rule gives \(n_P / n_D \approx 10 / 5.6 = 1.8\), and 2:1 is the closest small integer ratio.

    Splitwise: cheaper GPUs for decode

    Splitwise (Patel et al., 2023) starts from a hardware observation: decode does not need the newest GPU. Its Table I compares one DGX-A100 and one DGX-H100 machine (8 GPUs each): the H100 machine has 3.43× the compute but only 1.64× the memory bandwidth, the same 80 GB per GPU, 1.75× the power and 2.16× the cost per hour ($38 versus $17.6). [Certain] Decode is memory-bound, so it gains only the 1.64×, at 2.16× the price.

    Splitwise keeps three pools: a prompt pool, a token pool, and a mixed pool that grows and shrinks with load and runs normal mixed batching when one of the queues gets long. [Certain] It studies four cluster designs: Splitwise-AA (A100 for both phases), Splitwise-HH (H100 for both), Splitwise-HA (H100 for prompts, A100 for tokens), and Splitwise-HHcap (H100 for both, with the token machines power-capped). [Certain] The KV cache moves over InfiniBand with MSCCL++ one-sided writes; small prompts are sent after prefill, large ones layer by layer during prefill. [Certain] Its headline result: 1.4× higher throughput at 20% lower cost, or 2.35× more throughput at the same cost and power budget, compared with current designs. [Certain]

    DistServe: search for the best placement

    DistServe (Zhong et al., OSDI 2024) treats disaggregation as an optimization problem: for a given model, workload and SLO, choose the parallelism of each phase (TP and PP degrees), the number of instances of each, and where to put them in the cluster, to maximize per-GPU goodput. [Certain]

    Algorithm 2: DistServe placement for clusters with fast cross-node links (after Zhong et al., Algorithm 1)
    1. input: model, GPUs per node, SLOs, a sample of the workload, the target request rate \(\lambda\)
    2. for each prefill config (intra-op degree, inter-op degree) that fits in memory do
    3.   simulate the workload and binary-search the highest rate that meets the TTFT SLO; divide by its GPUs // per-GPU goodput of this config
    4. for each decode config do the same with the TPOT SLO
    5. keep the best prefill config and the best decode config // they are chosen independently
    6. replicate each until its pool can carry \(\lambda\): \(n_P = \lceil \lambda / r_P \rceil\), \(n_D = \lceil \lambda / r_D \rceil\)

    The search is cheap: under 1.3 minutes in the largest setting of the paper. [Certain] A second algorithm handles clusters with slow links between nodes (25 Gbps in their testbed): it forces the prefill and decode parts of the same pipeline stage onto the same node, so that the KV cache moves over NVLink instead of the network. [Certain] We will see why in the KV-transfer section. DistServe reports that it can serve 7.4× more requests, or meet 12.6× tighter SLOs, than vLLM and DeepSpeed-MII while keeping more than 90% of requests within the latency limits, on OPT-13B to OPT-175B with chatbot, code-completion and summarization workloads. [Certain]

    TetriInfer: chunks, predicted lengths and flipping roles

    TetriInfer (Hu et al., 2024) adds three ideas to disaggregation. [Certain]

    • Fixed-size chunked prefill. Prompts are cut and merged into chunks of exactly ChunkSize tokens (512 for OPT-13B on their V100 testbed), padding the last chunk, so each prefill step keeps the accelerator just at its saturation point.
    • A length predictor and two-level scheduling. A small model (OPT-125M, fine-tuned as a classifier) predicts the output length of each request in buckets of 200 tokens, with 74.9% accuracy. A global scheduler sends prompts to the least-loaded prefill instance; the prefill side then picks a decode instance with power-of-two random choice among those with enough predicted free memory, so heavy decodes do not pile up on one instance.
    • Instance flip. Prefill and decode are only roles. An idle instance can switch roles in about 5 to 7 ms (plus draining time), with no model reload.

    The abstract reports up to 97% lower average TTFT, 47% lower average job completion time and 38% fewer resources than vLLM. These are the best case, a workload with light prefills and heavy decodes; for a mixed workload the paper reports 85%, 50% and 21%, and for heavy prefills with heavy decodes vLLM was 14% better in performance per dollar. [Certain] The prototype emulates the network transfer time instead of moving data over a real link. [Certain] So read TetriInfer as a study of scheduling, not of KV transport.

    In production: Mooncake and DeepSeek-V3

    Mooncake (Moonshot AI) is the serving platform of the Kimi chatbot. It separates prefill and decode clusters and uses the spare CPU memory and SSDs of the GPU servers as a large distributed KV-cache store, with a scheduler built around where KV caches already are, and early rejection of requests when overloaded. It reports that real Kimi traffic got 75% more requests served. [Certain] DeepSeek-V3 runs prefill on units of 4 nodes (32 GPUs: attention with TP4 and sequence parallelism plus 8-way data parallelism, experts with 32-way expert parallelism) and decode on units of 40 nodes (320 GPUs, 320-way expert parallelism). [Certain] For a mixture-of-experts model the two phases want very different expert layouts, which is another reason to separate them (session 96).

    Moving the KV cache

    Disaggregation has a price that colocation does not have: the KV cache of every prompt must cross from the prefill GPUs to the decode GPUs. How large is it? Per token, every layer stores one key and one value vector per KV head (session 80):

    \[ \kappa = 2 \cdot L \cdot n_{\text{kv}} \cdot d_h \cdot b \ \ \text{bytes per token}, \qquad M_{\text{KV}} = s \cdot \kappa \tag{137.5} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(2\)
    one key and one value
    \(L, n_{\text{kv}}, d_h\)
    layers, KV heads, head dimension
    \(b\)
    bytes per number: 2 for BF16, 1 for FP8
    \(s\)
    prompt length in tokens; \(M_{\text{KV}}\) is the bytes to move for one request
    Model (attention type)\(L\), KV heads × dim\(\kappa\) in BF164,096-token prompt
    Llama 3.1 8B (GQA)32, 8 × 128128 KiB0.54 GB
    Llama 3.1 70B (GQA)80, 8 × 128320 KiB1.34 GB
    OPT-66B (MHA)64, 72 × 1282.25 MiB9.66 GB
    DeepSeek-V3 (MLA, 512 + 64 per layer)61, one 576-wide latent68.6 KiB0.29 GB

    The configurations are from the models’ config files. DistServe gives the same kind of number for OPT-66B: a 512-token request has about 1.13 GB of KV cache (we get \(512 \times 2.25\) MiB = 1.125 GiB), so 10 requests/s need about 11.3 GB/s, roughly 90 Gbps, of bandwidth between the pools. [Certain] Grouped-query attention, MLA (session 26) and KV quantization (session 110) shrink this a lot.

    The time to move it is \(t_x = M_{\text{KV}} / B_{\text{net}}\). The useful comparison is with the prefill time \(t_p\). Ignoring the attention part of prefill FLOPs, both grow linearly with \(s\), so their ratio does not depend on the prompt length:

    \[ \frac{t_x}{t_p} \approx \frac{s\,\kappa / B_{\text{net}}}{2 N s / F_{\text{eff}}} = \frac{\kappa\, F_{\text{eff}}}{2 N\, B_{\text{net}}} \tag{137.6} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(B_{\text{net}}\)
    total bandwidth between the prefill instance and the decode instance
    \(N\)
    number of weights; prefill does about \(2N\) FLOPs per prompt token
    \(F_{\text{eff}}\)
    FLOP/s that the prefill instance really achieves (all its GPUs together)

    For Llama 3.1 70B on 8 H100s at 50% of peak (\(F_{\text{eff}} = 3.96 \times 10^{15}\)) and 8 × 400 Gb/s InfiniBand (\(B_{\text{net}} = 400\) GB/s), one prompt token takes 35.7 µs of prefill and 0.82 µs of transfer: the transfer is about 2.3% of the prefill. A faster GPU makes the ratio worse (larger \(F_{\text{eff}}\)); a bigger model with GQA makes it better (larger \(N\), same \(\kappa\)).

    Hide the transfer behind the prefill

    The KV cache of layer \(\ell\) is final as soon as layer \(\ell\) finishes. So the prefill instance can send layer \(\ell\) while it computes layer \(\ell + 1\). Splitwise calls this layer-wise transfer, versus serialized transfer after the whole prefill. [Certain] If each layer takes \(t_p / L\) to compute and \(t_x / L\) to send, and the link sends one layer at a time, the delay added after the prefill ends is

    \[ \Delta_{\text{serial}} = t_x, \qquad \Delta_{\text{layer}} = \max\!\left( \frac{t_x}{L},\ \ t_x - \frac{L-1}{L}\, t_p \right) \tag{137.7} \]

    When \(t_x \le t_p\), only the last layer’s transfer remains: the delay shrinks by a factor \(L\). When the link is too slow (\(t_x \gt t_p\)), the transfer cannot keep up and most of it shows. Splitwise measured an added end-to-end latency of 0.8% with its layer-wise scheme (up to 3% with serialized transfer for large prompts), and DistServe found the transfer below 0.1% of total latency for OPT-175B, with 95% of transfers under 30 ms. [Certain]

    Worked example: Llama 3.1 70B, a 4,096-token prompt

    KV cache: \(4096 \times 327{,}680 = 1.34\) GB. Prefill on 8 H100s at 50% of peak: \((2 \times 70.55 \times 10^9 \times 4096 + 2 \times 80 \times 4096^2 \times 8192) / 3.96 \times 10^{15} = 0.152\) s.

    Over 400 GB/s (8 InfiniBand ports): \(t_x = 3.36\) ms, 2.2% of the prefill. Layer-wise, the added delay is \(3.36 / 80 = 0.042\) ms. Over one 400 Gb/s port (50 GB/s): \(t_x = 26.8\) ms serialized, 0.34 ms layer-wise. Over a 25 Gb/s network (3.125 GB/s, the cross-node speed of the DistServe testbed): \(t_x = 429\) ms, more than the whole prefill; even layer-wise, \(429 - 0.9875 \times 152 = 280\) ms is added. That is why DistServe’s second algorithm keeps the KV transfer inside a node on such clusters.

    Practical details of KV transport

    • Paged layout. Serving engines store the KV cache in fixed-size blocks scattered in memory (session 134). The transfer must gather many small blocks, so engines use RDMA (direct GPU-to-GPU writes over InfiniBand or RoCE, without the CPU) and batch the blocks. vLLM moves KV with pluggable “connectors”, for example NixlConnector and MooncakeConnector. [Certain]
    • Different parallelism on each side. If prefill uses TP8 and decode TP4, each decode GPU must receive the KV heads of two prefill GPUs. The mapping of heads to GPUs must be planned. [Likely]
    • Admission control. The decode instance must have free memory for the incoming KV cache and the future tokens before the transfer starts (step 3 of Algorithm 1); otherwise the request waits on the prefill side, holding memory there. [Likely]
    • Prefix caching across the cluster. If the KV cache of a common prefix (a system prompt, a long document) already sits on some machine, routing the request there skips part of the prefill. Mooncake and Dynamo route by KV-cache overlap for this reason. [Certain]
    Watch outThe ratio in equation 137.6 is per token, so “KV transfer is cheap” is true only with a fast network and a GQA or MLA model. An old MHA model on 25 Gbps Ethernet, or very fast prefill hardware with a slow network, can make the transfer the bottleneck.

    Serving on cheap spot instances: SpotServe

    Clouds rent out spare GPUs as spot (or preemptible) instances at up to 90% below the on-demand price. The catch: the provider can take an instance back at any time, after a short grace period (SpotServe uses 30 seconds; AWS documents a two-minute notice). [Certain for the paper; Likely for the AWS detail] For training, a preemption costs a restart from the last checkpoint (session 51). For serving, it is harder: requests are running right now, and the model may be split over the instance that disappears.

    SpotServe (Miao et al., 2023) handles this with three parts. [Certain]

    • Parallelization controller. When instances are lost or gained, or the request rate changes, it picks a new configuration \(C = (D, P, M, B)\): data-parallel degree, pipeline degree, tensor degree and maximum batch size. It minimizes the request latency subject to throughput at least the expected arrival rate; if no configuration can keep up, it maximizes throughput; ties go to fewer instances.
    • Device mapper. A new configuration has “positions” (which pipeline stage, which tensor slice). Which surviving instance should take which position? The one that already holds most of the needed weights and KV cache. This is a bipartite matching problem, solved with the Kuhn–Munkres (Hungarian) algorithm.
    • Migration planner and stateful recovery. It moves the missing weights and KV cache, front layers first, so the pipeline can restart early. Progress is committed per token, not per request, so an interrupted request continues from its last generated token instead of starting over. During the grace period, a just-in-time rule decides how many more decode steps to run before migrating.

    Formally, with surviving instances \(i\) and new positions \(j\), let \(u_{ij}\) be the bytes of weights and KV cache that instance \(i\) already has for position \(j\). The device mapper solves

    \[ \pi^\star = \argmax_{\pi \text{ one-to-one}} \sum_{i} u_{i,\pi(i)}, \qquad \text{bytes to move} = \sum_j \text{need}_j - \sum_i u_{i,\pi^\star(i)} \tag{137.8} \]

    and the just-in-time rule for a preemption runs the largest number of decode steps \(S\) that still leaves time to migrate:

    \[ S = \left\lfloor \frac{T_{\text{grace}} - T_{\text{mig}}}{t_{\text{step}}} \right\rfloor \tag{137.9} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\pi\)
    an assignment of instances to positions (each position gets at most one instance)
    \(u_{ij}\)
    reusable bytes if instance \(i\) takes position \(j\) (layers it already holds that the position needs)
    \(T_{\text{grace}}, T_{\text{mig}}, t_{\text{step}}\)
    grace period, estimated migration time, time of one decode step
    Algorithm 3: what SpotServe does on a preemption notice
    1. input: current config, the instance that will be preempted, \(T_{\text{grace}}\)
    2. controller: choose the new config \((D, P, M, B)\) for the surviving instances // latency model + arrival-rate estimate
    3. device mapper: build the reuse matrix \(u_{ij}\) and solve the matching (137.8) with Kuhn–Munkres // \(O(n^3)\)
    4. estimate \(T_{\text{mig}}\) from the bytes to move; keep decoding for \(S\) steps (137.9) // tokens are committed one by one
    5. migrate weights and KV cache, front layers first; switch to the new config
    6. resume every interrupted request from its last committed token

    The paper evaluates OPT-6.7B, GPT-20B and LLaMA-30B on AWS g4dn.12xlarge instances (4 T4 GPUs each) with a real 12-hour spot availability trace. It reports 2.4× to 9.1× lower P99 latency than the best existing baselines, and 54% lower cost than using only on-demand instances. [Certain] Figure 6 shows the device-mapping step on a small example.

    PyTorch: hand the KV cache from a prefill worker to a decode worker

    The code below builds a small GQA transformer and runs generation twice. First colocated: one model does prefill and decode. Then disaggregated: a “prefill worker” runs the prompt and pushes each layer’s KV cache through a hook as soon as that layer finishes (layer-wise transfer, as in Algorithm 1); a separate “decode worker” with its own copy of the weights receives the KV cache and continues. With the same sampling seed, both give exactly the same tokens, and the bytes sent match equation 137.5. Everything runs on the CPU and is deterministic.

    import torch, torch.nn.functional as F
torch.manual_seed(0)
V, d, L, nh, nkv = 1000, 256, 4, 8, 2                 # vocab, width, layers, query heads, KV heads (GQA)
hd = d // nh

class Layer(torch.nn.Module):
    def __init__(s):
        super().__init__()
        s.q = torch.nn.Linear(d, nh * hd, bias=False); s.kv = torch.nn.Linear(d, 2 * nkv * hd, bias=False)
        s.o = torch.nn.Linear(d, d, bias=False); s.ff = torch.nn.Sequential(torch.nn.Linear(d, 4 * d), torch.nn.GELU(), torch.nn.Linear(4 * d, d))
        s.n1, s.n2 = torch.nn.LayerNorm(d), torch.nn.LayerNorm(d)
    def forward(s, x, cache):                         # x: (T, d); cache: dict with "k", "v" of shape (nkv, t, hd)
        T, h = x.shape[0], s.n1(x)
        q = s.q(h).view(T, nh, hd).transpose(0, 1)
        k, v = s.kv(h).view(T, 2, nkv, hd).permute(1, 2, 0, 3)
        if "k" in cache: k, v = torch.cat([cache["k"], k], 1), torch.cat([cache["v"], v], 1)
        cache["k"], cache["v"] = k, v
        k, v = k.repeat_interleave(nh // nkv, 0), v.repeat_interleave(nh // nkv, 0)
        a = F.scaled_dot_product_attention(q, k, v, is_causal=(T > 1))   # causal only in prefill
        x = x + s.o(a.transpose(0, 1).reshape(T, d))
        return x + s.ff(s.n2(x))

class Model(torch.nn.Module):
    def __init__(s):
        super().__init__(); s.emb = torch.nn.Embedding(V, d); s.layers = torch.nn.ModuleList(Layer() for _ in range(L))
        s.nf, s.head = torch.nn.LayerNorm(d), torch.nn.Linear(d, V, bias=False)
    def forward(s, ids, caches, on_layer_done=None):
        x = s.emb(ids)
        for i, layer in enumerate(s.layers):
            x = layer(x, caches[i])
            if on_layer_done: on_layer_done(i, caches[i])  # hook: push this layer's KV as soon as it exists
        return s.head(s.nf(x[-1]))                         # logits of the last position

model = Model().eval()
prompt = torch.randint(0, V, (300,))

def sample(logits, g):                                  # same seed on both paths, so same draws
    return torch.multinomial(F.softmax(logits, -1), 1, generator=g)[0]

@torch.no_grad()
def colocated(n_new):                                   # one GPU does prefill and decode
    g = torch.Generator().manual_seed(1)
    caches = [dict() for _ in range(L)]; out = []; tok = sample(model(prompt, caches), g)
    for _ in range(n_new):
        out.append(tok.item()); tok = sample(model(tok.view(1), caches), g)
    return out

@torch.no_grad()
def disaggregated(n_new):
    prefill_worker = model                              # in real life: another GPU with its own weight copy
    decode_worker = Model().eval(); decode_worker.load_state_dict(model.state_dict())
    link = []                                           # stands in for NVLink / RDMA; we count the bytes
    def send(i, c):                                     # layer-wise transfer (Splitwise, DistServe)
        link.append((i, c["k"].clone(), c["v"].clone()))
    g = torch.Generator().manual_seed(1)
    first = sample(prefill_worker(prompt, [dict() for _ in range(L)], on_layer_done=send), g)
    sent = sum(k.numel() * k.element_size() + v.numel() * v.element_size() for _, k, v in link)
    caches = [dict(k=k, v=v) for _, k, v in sorted(link, key=lambda t: t[0])]   # decode side receives
    out, tok = [], first
    for _ in range(n_new):
        out.append(tok.item()); tok = sample(decode_worker(tok.view(1), caches), g)
    return out, sent

a = colocated(20)
b, sent = disaggregated(20)
print("same tokens:", a == b)
print("first tokens:", a[:8])
formula = 2 * L * nkv * hd * 4 * len(prompt)            # 2 (K and V) x layers x KV heads x head dim x 4 bytes (FP32) x tokens
print(f"KV bytes sent: {sent:,}  formula: {formula:,}  per token: {sent // len(prompt):,}")

    Read it like this. The KV cache is the complete state of a request after prefill: with it and the weights, any GPU can continue the generation and get the same result. That is why disaggregation and migration (SpotServe) work at all. The per-token size, 2,048 bytes here, is \(2 \times 4 \times 2 \times 32 \times 4\) (FP32), equation 137.5. In a real system the send hook becomes an RDMA write of paged KV blocks, and the decode side must not start until the last layer has arrived.

    A planner in the style of DistServe’s Algorithm 2, in plain Python. It uses our cost model (not the paper’s simulator) for Llama 3.1 70B on H100s, the M/D/1 formula 137.2 for TTFT, and a memory-bound decode step for TPOT. It finds the goodput of each TP degree for each phase, then the best pair and the ratio of instances (equation 137.4):

    import math
P, L, d, KV_TOK = 70.55e9, 80, 8192, 327_680        # Llama 3.1 70B, BF16 KV bytes per token
FLOPS, HBM, MEM, MFU = 989e12, 3.35e12, 80e9, 0.5   # one H100 SXM; MFU is our assumption
S_IN, S_OUT = 2048, 512                              # prompt and output length of the workload
SLO_TTFT, SLO_TPOT = 0.5, 0.040                      # seconds (average TTFT, per-token time)
TP_EFF = {1: 1.0, 2: 0.95, 4: 0.90, 8: 0.85}         # assumed scaling efficiency of tensor parallelism

def prefill_time(tp):                                # D in equation 137.2
    flops = 2 * P * S_IN + 2 * L * S_IN ** 2 * d
    return flops / (tp * FLOPS * MFU * TP_EFF[tp])

def prefill_goodput(tp):                             # max rate R with D + R D^2 / (2(1 - R D)) <= SLO
    D = prefill_time(tp)
    if D > SLO_TTFT: return 0.0
    lo, hi = 0.0, 1 / D
    for _ in range(60):
        R = (lo + hi) / 2
        lo, hi = (R, hi) if D + R * D * D / (2 * (1 - R * D)) <= SLO_TTFT else (lo, R)
    return lo

def decode_goodput(tp):                              # largest batch that meets TPOT and fits in memory
    best = 0.0
    for b in range(1, 1025):
        kv = b * (S_IN + S_OUT / 2) * KV_TOK         # average context during decoding
        if (2 * P + kv) / tp > 0.9 * MEM: break
        t = max((2 * P + kv) / (tp * HBM), 2 * P * b / (tp * FLOPS * MFU)) / TP_EFF[tp]
        if t <= SLO_TPOT: best = b / (S_OUT * t)    # finished requests per second
    return best

print(" TP | prefill D(s)  req/s | decode req/s")
for tp in (2, 4, 8):
    print(f" {tp:2d} | {prefill_time(tp):7.3f} {prefill_goodput(tp):7.2f} | {decode_goodput(tp):8.2f}")

best = None
for tp_p in (2, 4, 8):
    for tp_d in (2, 4, 8):
        rp, rd = prefill_goodput(tp_p), decode_goodput(tp_d)
        if rp == 0 or rd == 0: continue
        per_gpu = 1 / (tp_p / rp + tp_d / rd)        # fluid model: GPUs needed per req/s, inverted
        if best is None or per_gpu > best[0]: best = (per_gpu, tp_p, tp_d, rd / rp)
g, tp_p, tp_d, ratio = best
print(f"best: prefill TP{tp_p}, decode TP{tp_d}, {ratio:.2f} prefill instances per decode instance, {g:.3f} req/s per GPU")

best: prefill TP8, decode TP8, 4.31 prefill instances per decode instance, 1.047 req/s per GPU

    Three lessons. Decode on TP2 is useless here: the 141 GB of weights leave almost no room for KV cache in 2 × 72 GB, so the batch stays tiny. Prefill goodput grows faster than linearly with TP (1.73 → 4.85 → 10.32 req/s), because a shorter \(D\) also shortens the queue (equation 137.2). And with 2,048-token prompts and 512 output tokens, we need about 4 prefill instances per decode instance: the ratio is a property of the workload, so it must be re-planned when the traffic changes.

    Common mistakes

    • Expecting disaggregation to raise throughput. It improves goodput under latency targets. If you only care about raw tokens per second, a colocated system with large batches is often as good or better; vLLM’s documentation says so directly.
    • Using pipeline parallelism to cut latency. A token still passes through every stage. PP adds capacity and works over slow links; for decode latency, use TP inside a node (Figure 2).
    • Running TP across nodes. Two all-reduces per layer over InfiniBand cost more than the useful work at large batches. Keep TP groups inside the NVLink domain.
    • Fixing the prefill-to-decode ratio once. The best ratio is \(r_D / r_P\), and both depend on prompt and output lengths, which change with the time of day and the product. Re-plan, flip roles, or keep a mixed pool.
    • Ignoring the KV transfer on slow networks. Check \(t_x / t_p\) with equation 137.6 before you design. For MHA models or 25 Gbps links, place prefill and decode in the same node.
    • Sending the KV cache only after the prefill. Serialized transfer adds \(t_x\) to the second token. Layer-wise transfer hides all but \(t_x / L\) when the link is fast enough.
    • Reporting average TPOT only. Interference shows in the tail: the worst gap between tokens, or P99 TPOT. Averages can look equal while users see pauses (Figure 1).
    • Treating a spot preemption like a crash. There is a grace period, and the KV cache is the state of the request. Commit tokens, migrate state, and remap devices to reuse what survivors already hold.

    Interview questions

    What is prefill–decode disaggregation, and what problem does it solve?basic
    It runs the two phases of generation on different GPU instances: prefill instances process prompts and produce the KV cache and the first token; decode instances receive the KV cache and generate the rest. It solves interference (a long prefill in the same batch stalls all decodes, hurting TPOT, and decodes slow prefills, hurting TTFT) and coupling (one parallel config and batch size must serve both phases). After the split, each pool can use its own TP degree, batch size and even GPU type. The costs are the KV transfer and the need to balance the two pools.
    Why are prefill and decode so different on a GPU?basic
    Prefill processes \(s\) tokens per weight read, so its arithmetic intensity is high and it is compute-bound; a single long prompt fills the GPU, and batching more prompts helps little (Splitwise saw no gain past about 2,048 batched tokens). Decode processes one token per sequence per weight read, so it is memory-bound; it gets better with larger batches until memory for the KV cache runs out. Prefill is sensitive to compute and power; decode to memory bandwidth and capacity.
    You serve Llama 3.1 70B in BF16 on H100s. TP or PP? How many GPUs?core
    Weights are 141 GB, so at least 2 GPUs, but TP2 leaves almost no room for KV cache. TP4 or TP8 inside one NVLink node is the usual choice: equation 137.1 shows the step time falls almost as \(1/t\), with about \(2L\alpha = 1.6\) ms of all-reduce overhead per token. PP8 on the same 8 GPUs gives the same memory per GPU but about 5× the time per token at 32 sequences, because each token crosses 8 stages and each GPU rereads its weights per micro-batch. Use replicas (data parallelism) or PP across nodes, TP within.
    Define goodput. Why is it better than throughput for serving?core
    DistServe’s per-GPU goodput is the highest request rate at which a target share of requests (say 90%) meets both the TTFT and TPOT SLOs, divided by the number of GPUs. Throughput counts requests that finish, even if they were too slow to be useful. Goodput penalizes interference and queueing, and dividing by GPUs makes designs with different sizes comparable; it is the quantity a cost-per-useful-request business cares about.
    How do you choose the number of prefill and decode instances?core
    Measure or simulate the goodput of one prefill instance \(r_P\) (rate meeting TTFT) and one decode instance \(r_D\) (rate meeting TPOT) for the workload. The system rate is \(\min(n_P r_P, n_D r_D)\), so balance them: \(n_P / n_D \approx r_D / r_P\). Example from DistServe: \(r_P = 5.6\), \(r_D = 10\) req/s per GPU gives 2:1 and 3.33 req/s per GPU versus 1.6 colocated. Long prompts lower \(r_P\) and raise the needed ratio; long outputs lower \(r_D\). Re-plan when the mix changes, or use role flipping (TetriInfer) or a mixed pool (Splitwise).
    Estimate the KV-cache transfer for a 4,096-token prompt on Llama 3.1 70B. Is it a problem?core
    \(\kappa = 2 \times 80 \times 8 \times 128 \times 2 = 327{,}680\) bytes per token, so 1.34 GB. Over 8 × 400 Gb/s InfiniBand (400 GB/s) that is 3.4 ms, against a prefill of about 0.15 s on 8 H100s at 50% of peak: about 2%. Sent layer by layer during the prefill, only about \(3.4/80 = 0.04\) ms remains. Over 25 Gb/s it would be 0.43 s, more than the prefill, so on slow networks keep each prefill–decode pair inside one node.
    Why does the ratio of transfer time to prefill time not depend on prompt length?advanced
    Both are linear in \(s\) (ignoring attention): KV bytes are \(s \kappa\) and prefill FLOPs are about \(2Ns\). So \(t_x/t_p = \kappa F_{\text{eff}} / (2 N B_{\text{net}})\), equation 137.6. It falls slowly for very long prompts because attention FLOPs grow like \(s^2\). It grows with faster GPUs (\(F_{\text{eff}}\)) and with larger KV per weight (MHA instead of GQA or MLA), and falls with faster networks.
    For a prefill instance, when is 2-way tensor parallelism better than 2-way pipeline parallelism?advanced
    DistServe’s M/D/1 analysis: TP gives \(D/K + RD^2/(2K(K-RD))\), PP gives \(D + RD^2/(4(2-RD))\), where \(K \lt 2\) is the TP speed-up. At low arrival rates, the execution time dominates, and TP wins because it shortens \(D\). At high rates, queueing dominates, and PP wins because its capacity is \(2/D\) instead of \(K/D\). So the best prefill parallelism depends on load and on how well TP scales.
    What does Splitwise add beyond splitting the phases?core
    Hardware heterogeneity and power: since decode is memory-bound, it can run on cheaper or power-capped machines. A DGX-H100 has 3.43× the compute of a DGX-A100 but 1.64× the memory bandwidth and 2.16× the cost; token-phase latency barely changes when power is capped by half. Splitwise-HA uses H100 for prompts and A100 for tokens. It also keeps a mixed pool for load spikes and uses layer-wise KV transfer over InfiniBand. Result: 1.4× throughput at 20% lower cost, or 2.35× throughput at equal cost and power.
    What is TetriInfer’s approach, and what would you check before trusting its numbers?advanced
    Fixed-size chunked prefill (512 tokens) to keep prefill at saturation; disaggregated prefill and decode instances; a small OPT-125M classifier that predicts output length in 200-token buckets (74.9% accuracy) so that decode placement avoids hotspots (power-of-two choice); and instances that flip roles in milliseconds. Check: the headline 97%/47%/38% is the best workload (light prefill, heavy decode), and vLLM wins on heavy-prefill-heavy-decode; the testbed is 4 V100s with an emulated network (no real KV transfer); and length predictors trained on one dataset may fail on another.
    How would you serve an LLM on spot GPUs? What does SpotServe do?advanced
    Use the grace period and treat the KV cache as the request state. SpotServe re-plans the parallel configuration \((D, P, M, B)\) when instances come and go, choosing the lowest latency that keeps up with the arrival rate. A device mapper solves a bipartite matching (Kuhn–Munkres) between surviving instances and new positions to maximize reused weights and KV cache, so less data moves. It commits progress per token, decides with a just-in-time rule how many decode steps fit before migrating, and migrates front layers first. It reports 2.4–9.1× lower P99 latency than baselines and 54% lower cost than on-demand only.
    Design question: a chat API with 4,000-token prompts, 300-token answers, TTFT under 1 s and TPOT under 50 ms. Sketch the serving layout.advanced
    Pick the model and its memory (weights + KV per token from 137.5). Inside each node, use TP4 or TP8. Long prompts and strict TPOT point to disaggregation: prefill instances with high TP for low TTFT, decode instances with big batches. Compute \(r_P\) from the prefill time and the queueing formula (137.2) for the 1 s TTFT, \(r_D\) from the largest batch whose step time stays under 50 ms and fits in memory, then set \(n_P / n_D \approx r_D / r_P\) and size for peak traffic with headroom. Check the KV transfer with 137.6 for your network and send it layer by layer. Add prefix-aware routing for shared system prompts, autoscaling or role flipping, and monitor P99 TTFT and TPOT, not averages.

    Key takeaways

    • For serving, tensor parallelism makes each token faster at the cost of \(2L\) all-reduces per token (137.1); keep it inside the NVLink domain. Pipeline parallelism adds capacity, not speed; use it, or replicas, across nodes.
    • Prefill is compute-bound and decode is memory-bound. On a shared GPU, prefills stall decodes; disaggregation removes this interference and lets each phase pick its own parallelism, batch and hardware.
    • Measure designs by per-GPU goodput under SLOs, not raw throughput. Balance the pools: \(n_P / n_D \approx r_D / r_P\) (137.4); the ratio depends on the workload. Queueing (137.2, 137.3) decides whether TP or PP is better for prefill.
    • Splitwise adds cheaper or power-capped GPUs for decode; DistServe searches placements for goodput (7.4× more requests or 12.6× tighter SLOs); TetriInfer adds fixed-size chunks, length prediction and role flipping.
    • The KV cache is \(2 L n_{\text{kv}} d_h b\) bytes per token (137.5); its transfer time relative to prefill is \(\kappa F_{\text{eff}} / (2 N B_{\text{net}})\) (137.6), about 2% for Llama 3.1 70B on 400 GB/s, and layer-wise sending hides most of it (137.7).
    • Spot GPUs are cheap but can vanish. SpotServe re-plans the config, maps devices with a bipartite matching to reuse state (137.8), commits tokens one by one and uses the grace period (137.9).

    Key formulas

    \[ \overline{\text{TTFT}} = D + \frac{RD^2}{2(1-RD)}, \quad \frac{n_P}{n_D} \approx \frac{r_D}{r_P}, \quad \kappa = 2 L n_{\text{kv}} d_h b, \quad \frac{t_x}{t_p} \approx \frac{\kappa F_{\text{eff}}}{2 N B_{\text{net}}} \]
