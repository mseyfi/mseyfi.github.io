    Evaluation of LLM systems

    A model is only one part of an LLM product, and public scores do not tell you if your product works. This session compares static benchmarks, arenas and your own test set, shows how LLM judges are biased and how to correct them, and gives the statistics (intervals, paired tests, sample size) you need to trust a difference. It ends with a regression suite and a release gate you can build.

    Time: 3 hours (reading 2 hours, exercises 1 hour)Level: Beginner to interview levelBefore this: Sessions 40 and 149; Sessions 152 to 154 help

    What you will learn

    • What static benchmarks, arenas and your own test set each measure, and when to use which
    • How Chatbot Arena turns pairwise votes into ratings with the Bradley–Terry model
    • LLM-as-judge setups, and the position, length and self-preference biases with real numbers from MT-Bench and AlpacaEval
    • How to check a judge against humans with Cohen’s kappa
    • Wilson intervals, paired comparisons, McNemar’s test and the paired bootstrap
    • How many test items you need: the sample-size formula and why pairing helps
    • How to build a versioned, sliced regression suite and a per-slice release gate

    Why this matters

    You change one prompt, swap the model for a cheaper one, or add a retrieval step. Is the product better or worse now? Without a good evaluation, nobody can answer this question. The team ships changes on feeling, and users find the regressions.

    In session 40 we scored a base model on public benchmarks. In session 149 we scored single outputs with BLEU, ROUGE and BERTScore. A real LLM system is more than a model: it has a prompt, maybe a retriever (session 152), tools and an agent loop (session 154), and output rules. Its quality depends on all of these parts together, and on the questions your users ask. This session is about measuring that system, and about making the numbers trustworthy.

    Four ideas carry the session:

    1. Three sources of evidence: static benchmarks (fixed public test sets such as MMLU and GSM8K), arenas (people vote between two anonymous answers), and your own test set. Each one answers a different question.
    2. LLM-as-judge: using a strong model to grade answers. It is cheap and fast, but it has known biases: it prefers the first answer, longer answers, and sometimes its own answers.
    3. Statistics: every score is an estimate from a sample. You need confidence intervals, paired comparisons, and enough items to see the difference you care about.
    4. A regression suite: a versioned test set with graders that runs on every change and blocks a release when some part of the product gets worse.
    Interview tip“How would you evaluate an LLM feature before launch?” is one of the most common applied-ML interview questions. A strong answer names the test set (where it comes from), the graders (code checks, LLM judge, humans), how the judge is checked against humans, the statistics (paired test, error bars, sample size), and the release gate. This session gives you each piece with numbers.

    Three sources of evidence

    Before any statistics, decide what question you want to answer. The three common sources of evidence answer different questions:

    Static benchmarks

    A static benchmark is a fixed set of questions with known answers. MMLU has multiple-choice questions on 57 subjects, with 14,042 test questions. GSM8K has grade-school math word problems; its test split has 1,319 problems. [Certain] They are cheap to run and everybody uses the same items, so you can compare with published numbers. How to run them correctly (log-likelihood scoring, answer extraction, harness settings, for example with the LM Evaluation Harness) was the topic of session 40. Suites such as HELM go wider: it evaluated 30 models on 42 scenarios and, for its 16 core scenarios, measured 7 metrics (accuracy, calibration, robustness, fairness, bias, toxicity and efficiency), not only accuracy. [Certain]

    They have three problems for product work. First, contamination: test items are on the web, so they can end up in training data, and the score then measures memory. Second, saturation: when the best models score near the top, the benchmark can no longer separate them. Top models now score close to 90% on MMLU, and part of the remaining errors are wrong labels in the dataset itself. [Likely] Third, a benchmark question is not your user’s question. A model that is 3 points better on MMLU can be worse at following your output format.

    Arenas

    In an arena, a user types any prompt, sees the answers of two anonymous models side by side, and votes for the better one (or a tie). Chatbot Arena collected more than 240,000 such votes in its first months. [Certain] Because the prompts are new and come from real people, an arena is hard to game by leaking test data. But voters reward style: long, well-formatted, confident answers win more votes, even when they are not more correct. [Likely]

    How do you turn many pairwise votes into one score per model? With the Bradley–Terry model. Each model \(i\) gets one number, its rating \(R_i\). The probability that \(i\) beats \(j\) depends only on the difference of ratings:

    \[ P(i \succ j) = \frac{1}{1 + 10^{(R_j - R_i)/400}} \tag{155.1} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(i \succ j\)
    “the answer of model \(i\) is preferred to the answer of model \(j\)”
    \(R_i, R_j\)
    ratings on the chess-style Elo scale; only differences matter
    400
    a scale constant from chess: a gap of 400 points means 10 to 1 odds

    A gap of 0 gives 50%. A gap of 100 points gives 64.0%, a gap of 200 gives 76.0%. The ratings are fitted to all votes at once by maximum likelihood (choose the ratings that make the observed votes most probable):

    \[ \hat{R} = \argmax_{R} \sum_{i \ne j} W_{ij} \, \log P(i \succ j) \tag{155.2} \]

    Here \(W_{ij}\) is the number of votes where \(i\) beat \(j\). Classic Elo instead updates the two ratings after each single game, so the result depends on the order of the games. Chatbot Arena moved to the Bradley–Terry fit for this reason, and it reports bootstrap confidence intervals for each rating. [Likely] Figure 2 fits (155.2) to a vote table you can change.

    AnalogyA chess federation does not need every player to play every other player. Each game says a little about two players, and the rating system joins all games into one ladder. An arena does the same for models: each vote is one game.

    Your own test set

    The third source is a set of inputs that look like your real traffic, with a way to grade each output. It is the only one that measures your system. Good places to collect items: real (anonymized) user requests sampled from logs, every bug report and failure you have seen, hard cases written by domain experts, and edge cases (empty input, very long input, other languages, attempts to break the rules). Label each item with a slice (a subgroup such as “billing questions” or “long documents”) so you can see where the system fails, not only how often.

    Graders: who decides if an answer is good?

    A test item needs a grader: a rule that turns an output into a score. Use the cheapest grader that is correct for the item:

    LLM-as-judge and its biases

    An LLM judge is a strong model that reads a question and one or two answers and outputs a grade. There are three common setups: single-answer grading (give a score from 1 to 10, ideally with a rubric), pairwise comparison (which of A and B is better?), and reference-guided grading (compare the answer with a correct reference, useful for math and facts). MT-Bench, a set of 80 multi-turn questions in 8 categories, was built to study these judges. With ties removed, GPT-4 as a pairwise judge agreed with human experts 85% of the time, while two humans agreed with each other 81% of the time. [Certain] So a good judge can be as consistent as a human rater. But it has biases that a human rater may not have.

    Position bias

    The judge prefers an answer because of where it is shown, usually first. In the MT-Bench study, each pair was judged twice with the order swapped. GPT-4 gave the same verdict in both orders only 65.0% of the time; in 30.0% of cases it preferred whichever answer came first. Claude-v1 was consistent in only 23.8% of cases and preferred the first answer in 75.0%. [Certain] The standard fix is to judge both orders: count a win only if the same answer wins in both orders, and call it a tie otherwise.

    Length (verbosity) bias

    The judge prefers longer answers, even when the extra text adds nothing. In the MT-Bench study, a “repetitive list” attack (the same answer with its items repeated in new words) fooled Claude-v1 and GPT-3.5 in 91.3% of cases and GPT-4 in 8.7%. [Certain] AlpacaEval, an automatic benchmark that uses an LLM judge, fixed this with a length-controlled win rate: a regression model predicts what the judge would say if both answers had the same length. This raised its Spearman correlation with Chatbot Arena rankings from 0.94 to 0.98. [Certain]

    Self-preference

    A judge may prefer answers written by itself or by its own model family, perhaps because their style looks “right” to it. In the MT-Bench study, GPT-4 gave its own answers a 10% higher win rate and Claude-v1 25% higher, but the authors said the data were too small to be sure. [Certain] Practical rule: do not let a model judge a contest it takes part in, or use judges from several families.

    AnalogyA teacher grading essays late at night. She is a little kinder to the first essay on the pile, she gives more points to long essays, and she likes essays that sound like her own writing. A careful school shuffles the pile, sets a word limit, and has a second teacher check a sample.

    We can write a simple model of a biased pairwise judge. Let \(q_X\) be the true quality of answer \(X\). The judge sees answer \(F\) first and answer \(S\) second:

    \[ P(\text{judge picks } F) = \sigma\!\big( k\,(q_F - q_S) \;+\; \beta \;+\; \gamma \,\tfrac{\ell_F - \ell_S}{100} \big) \tag{155.3} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\sigma\)
    the sigmoid function, \(\sigma(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-x})\), which turns any number into a probability
    \(k\)
    how sharply the judge sees quality differences (in Figure 3, \(k = 3\))
    \(\beta\)
    position bias: extra push toward the first answer; \(\beta = 0\) means no bias
    \(\gamma\)
    length bias: extra push per 100 tokens of extra length
    \(\ell_F, \ell_S\)
    lengths of the two answers in tokens

    Swapping the order turns \(+\beta\) into \(-\beta\) for the same pair, so averaging the two orders cancels the position bias. It does not cancel the length bias: the longer answer is still longer in both orders. Figure 3 shows this on 300 simulated pairs.

    Algorithm 1: a pairwise LLM judge with order swapping
    1. input: question \(x\), answers \(a\) and \(b\), judge prompt with a rubric, judge model \(J\)
    2. \(v_1 \leftarrow J(x, \text{first} = a, \text{second} = b)\) // returns "first", "second" or "tie"
    3. \(v_2 \leftarrow J(x, \text{first} = b, \text{second} = a)\) // same pair, order swapped
    4. map both verdicts back to names: \(w_1, w_2 \in \{a, b, \text{tie}\}\)
    5. if \(w_1 = w_2\): return \(w_1\)
    6. else: log the item as inconsistent; return tie // conservative rule from the MT-Bench study
    7. use temperature 0, a fixed judge version, and ask for a short reason before the verdict

    Other practical rules for judges: give a rubric (what earns each score), ask the judge to explain before it scores, prefer pairwise or pass/fail over 1–10 scales (scales drift and cluster), pin the judge model version, and treat the judge prompt as code that is versioned and reviewed. For RAG systems, ask the judge narrow questions such as “is every claim in the answer supported by the given passages?” (session 153).

    Is the judge good enough? Check it against humans

    Before you trust a judge, have humans label a sample (100–300 items is common [Likely]) and measure agreement. Raw agreement is misleading when most items pass: a judge that always says “pass” agrees 90% of the time if 90% of items pass. Cohen’s kappa corrects for the agreement you would get by chance:

    \[ \kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e}, \qquad p_e = p_J\, p_H + (1 - p_J)(1 - p_H) \tag{155.4} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(p_o\)
    observed agreement: the share of items where judge and human give the same label
    \(p_e\)
    agreement expected by chance if the two labeled independently
    \(p_J, p_H\)
    the share of items the judge (or the human) labels “pass”

    \(\kappa = 1\) is perfect agreement, \(\kappa = 0\) is no better than chance. Values above about 0.6 are often called “substantial”. [Likely] Also look at the two kinds of disagreement separately: a judge that passes bad answers is more dangerous than one that fails good ones.

    Worked example: a judge checked on 200 items

    A judge and a human label 200 answers as pass or fail. Both say pass on 120, both say fail on 40. The judge says pass but the human says fail on 25; the judge says fail but the human says pass on 15.

    Observed agreement \(p_o = (120 + 40)/200 = 0.800\). The judge passes \(p_J = 145/200 = 0.725\); the human passes \(p_H = 135/200 = 0.675\). Chance agreement \(p_e = 0.725 \times 0.675 + 0.275 \times 0.325 = 0.4894 + 0.0894 = 0.5788\).

    \(\kappa = (0.800 - 0.5788)/(1 - 0.5788) = 0.525\). The 80% agreement sounds good, but kappa is only moderate. And the judge is too kind: 25 of the 65 answers the human failed were passed by the judge. Fix the rubric for those cases before you use this judge as a gate.

    Statistics: is the difference real?

    Your test set is a sample. Run the same system on a different sample of 400 questions and you get a different score. So every score needs an error bar. Session 40 gave the basic formula, \(\mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\), and the 95% interval \(p \pm 1.96\,\mathrm{SE}\). Product test sets add three problems: they are small, the two systems answer the same items, and you need to plan how many items to label.

    Small test sets: the Wilson interval

    The simple interval \(p \pm 1.96\,\mathrm{SE}\) (the Wald interval) breaks for small \(n\) or for \(p\) near 0 or 1. With 18 of 20 correct, it gives \([0.769, 1.031]\), which goes above 100%. With 20 of 20 correct, it gives \([1, 1]\): zero uncertainty, which is clearly wrong. The Wilson interval stays inside \([0, 1]\) and is accurate for small samples:

    \[ \frac{\hat p + \frac{z^2}{2n}}{1 + \frac{z^2}{n}} \;\pm\; \frac{z}{1 + \frac{z^2}{n}} \sqrt{\frac{\hat p (1 - \hat p)}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}} \tag{155.5} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\hat p\)
    the measured pass rate (\(k/n\))
    \(n\)
    number of items
    \(z\)
    1.96 for a 95% interval

    For 18 of 20, Wilson gives \([0.699, 0.972]\). For 20 of 20, it gives \([0.839, 1]\): even a perfect score on 20 items only tells you the true rate is probably above 84%.

    Comparing two systems: use the pairs

    When the old and new system answer the same items, most items are “easy for both” or “hard for both”. Those items tell you nothing about the difference. Only the discordant items (where exactly one system is right) carry information. A paired analysis uses this. For each item \(i\), let \(d_i = y_i^{\text{new}} - y_i^{\text{old}}\), which is \(+1\), \(0\) or \(-1\):

    \[ \bar d = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} d_i = \frac{b - c}{n}, \qquad \mathrm{SE}(\bar d) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\Big(\frac{b + c}{n} - \bar d^{\,2}\Big)} \tag{155.6} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(y_i\)
    1 if the system passes item \(i\), 0 if not
    \(b\)
    items the new system passes and the old one fails
    \(c\)
    items the old system passes and the new one fails
    \((b + c)/n\)
    the discordance rate: the share of items where the two systems disagree

    A classic test that uses only the discordant items is McNemar’s test. If the two systems are equally good, \(b\) and \(c\) should be about equal, and

    \[ \chi^2 = \frac{(b - c)^2}{b + c} \tag{155.7} \]

    follows a chi-square distribution with 1 degree of freedom; values above 3.84 are significant at the 5% level. For small \(b + c\) (say under 25), use the exact binomial version instead. [Certain]

    Worked example: the same data, unpaired vs paired

    400 items. The old system passes 300 (75.0%), the new one 316 (79.0%). The new one wins \(b = 36\) items the old one failed, and loses \(c = 20\) items the old one passed.

    Unpaired (wrong here): \(\mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{0.75 \cdot 0.25/400 + 0.79 \cdot 0.21/400} = 0.0297\). The gain is \(0.04\), so \(z = 1.35\) and the 95% interval is \([-1.8, +9.8]\) points. Not significant.

    Paired: \(\bar d = 16/400 = 0.04\), and \((b + c)/n = 0.14\), so \(\mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{(0.14 - 0.0016)/400} = 0.0186\). Now \(z = 2.15\) and the interval is \([+0.4, +7.6]\) points. McNemar: \(\chi^2 = 16^2/56 = 4.57\), p-value 0.033 (exact binomial version: 0.044). The gain is real.

    Same data, different answer. The paired SE is smaller because the 344 items where both systems agree add no noise to the difference.

    If you cannot assume a formula, use the paired bootstrap: resample the \(n\) items with replacement many times, keeping each item’s old and new scores together, and compute the difference each time. The middle 95% of those differences is the interval. It works for any metric, including judge scores and averages over several samples per item.

    Watch outItems are often not independent. Ten questions about the same document, or five turns of the same conversation, are a cluster: they tend to be right or wrong together. Treating them as independent makes error bars too small. Bootstrap whole clusters (documents, conversations), not single items, or use clustered standard errors (Miller, 2024, “Adding Error Bars to Evals”, gives the formulas). [Certain] Also, if the system is sampled with temperature above 0, run each item several times and average; the sampling noise is a second source of variance.

    How many items do you need?

    Turn the question around: what is the smallest difference \(\delta\) you care about, and how many items \(n\) do you need to see it? With significance level 5% (\(z_{\alpha/2} = 1.96\)) and power 80% (\(z_\beta = 0.84\); power is the chance of detecting a real difference of size \(\delta\)):

    \[ n_{\text{unpaired}} \approx \frac{(z_{\alpha/2} + z_\beta)^2 \,\big(p_1(1-p_1) + p_2(1-p_2)\big)}{\delta^2}, \qquad n_{\text{paired}} \approx \frac{(z_{\alpha/2} + z_\beta)^2 \,(r - \delta^2)}{\delta^2} \tag{155.8} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(p_1, p_2\)
    pass rates of the old and new system (\(p_2 = p_1 + \delta\))
    \(\delta\)
    the smallest difference you want to detect
    \(r\)
    the discordance rate \((b + c)/n\); it must be at least \(\delta\)
    \((z_{\alpha/2} + z_\beta)^2\)
    \((1.96 + 0.8416)^2 = 7.85\) for 5% significance and 80% power

    The \(1/\delta^2\) is the painful part: to see half the difference you need four times the items. Example: base rate 75%, \(\delta = 2\) points. Unpaired: \(n \approx 7{,}155\) items. Paired, if the two systems disagree on 10% of items: \(n \approx 1{,}955\). If they disagree on only 5%: \(n \approx 974\). Small prompt changes usually have low discordance, so pairing helps the most exactly where you need it.

    A regression suite for your product

    A regression suite is your test set, its graders and a pass/fail rule, run automatically on every change: a new prompt, a new model version, a new retriever, a new tool. Like unit tests in software, its job is to catch things that used to work and now break.

    How to build it

    • Start small and real. 50–200 items from real traffic and known failures are more useful than 10,000 synthetic ones. Grow it every week.
    • Every bug becomes a test. When a user reports a bad answer, add it (and a few variants) with the correct grading rule.
    • Slices. Tag each item: task type, language, input length, customer tier, risk level (safety, money, legal). Report every slice, not only the average.
    • Freeze and version. The suite, the graders and the judge prompt have version numbers. A score is only comparable with a score from the same suite version.
    • Keep a hidden part. If engineers tune prompts on the whole suite, the suite becomes a training set and stops predicting real quality. Keep a held-out part that is only run at release time.
    • Measure cost and latency too. A change that gains 1 point but doubles latency or token cost may be a regression for the product (session 81).

    The release gate

    The average can hide a disaster. A new model can gain 3 points on common questions and lose 8 points on refunds, and the average barely moves. So the gate looks at every slice. For a slice with paired difference \(\bar d\), standard error \(\mathrm{SE}\), and a tolerance \(\tau\) (the largest drop you accept):

    \[ \text{verdict} = \begin{cases} \text{regression (block)} & \text{if } \bar d + z\,\mathrm{SE} \lt 0 \\ \text{pass} & \text{if } \bar d - z\,\mathrm{SE} \gt -\tau \\ \text{not enough evidence} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \tag{155.9} \]

    The first line says “we are confident the slice got worse”. The second says “we are confident it did not get worse by more than \(\tau\)” (a non-inferiority test). The third is honest: the slice is too small to know, so add items or have a person look. Safety slices often use \(\tau = 0\) and a hard rule (any new failure blocks). [Likely]

    Algorithm 2: a regression gate for an LLM system
    1. input: suite \(D\) (items with slices), graders \(g\), current system \(S_0\), candidate \(S_1\), tolerances \(\tau_s\), confidence \(z\)
    2. for each item \(x_i \in D\): \(y_i^0 \leftarrow g(x_i, S_0(x_i))\), \(y_i^1 \leftarrow g(x_i, S_1(x_i))\) // same items, same graders
    3. \(d_i \leftarrow y_i^1 - y_i^0\)
    4. for each slice \(s\): compute \(\bar d_s\) and \(\mathrm{SE}_s\) with (155.6), or a cluster bootstrap
    5. \(\quad\) verdict\(_s \leftarrow\) rule (155.9) with \(\tau_s\) // safety slices: \(\tau_s = 0\)
    6. if any verdict is “regression”: block and show the failing items
    7. else if any verdict is “not enough evidence”: send to human review or add items
    8. else: pass; also check cost and latency budgets
    9. store all outputs, grades and judge reasons with the suite version

    After launch

    Offline tests cannot see everything. After launch, add online signals: thumbs up and down, how often users retry or edit the answer, task completion, and A/B tests on real traffic (the same paired-or-not statistics apply). Sample live traffic every week, grade it, and move the surprising cases into the suite. The suite and the product then improve together.

    All of it in code

    The PyTorch code below fits Bradley–Terry ratings, computes a Wilson interval, runs the paired comparison and McNemar’s test, a paired bootstrap, and Cohen’s kappa for a judge. It is deterministic and runs on a CPU in about a second.

    import math, torch
torch.manual_seed(0)

# 1) Bradley-Terry ratings from pairwise votes, formula (155.2)
names = ["A", "B", "C", "D"]
W = torch.tensor([[0, 60, 70, 80],      # W[i, j] = votes where i beat j
                  [40, 0, 55, 70],
                  [30, 45, 0, 65],
                  [20, 30, 35, 0]], dtype=torch.float64)
s = torch.zeros(4, dtype=torch.float64, requires_grad=True)   # one strength per model
opt = torch.optim.LBFGS([s], max_iter=200, tolerance_grad=1e-12, line_search_fn="strong_wolfe")
def nll():
    opt.zero_grad()
    logp = torch.nn.functional.logsigmoid(s[:, None] - s[None, :])  # log P(i beats j)
    loss = -(W * logp).sum()
    loss.backward()
    return loss
opt.step(nll)
elo = 400 / math.log(10) * (s - s.mean()).detach() + 1000         # Elo scale, formula (155.1)
print([f"{n}:{r:.1f}" for n, r in zip(names, elo.tolist())])      # A:1114.0 B:1027.9 C:981.8 D:876.2

# 2) Wilson interval, formula (155.5)
def wilson(k, n, z=1.96):
    p = k / n; den = 1 + z * z / n
    c = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / den
    h = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / den
    return c - h, c + h
print("wilson 18/20:", [round(v, 3) for v in wilson(18, 20)])     # [0.699, 0.972]

# 3) paired comparison on the same 400 items, formulas (155.6) and (155.7)
n = 400
old = torch.zeros(n); new = torch.zeros(n)
old[:300] = 1                       # old system passes 300 items
new[:280] = 1; new[300:336] = 1     # new loses 20 of them, wins 36 others: 316 passes
perm = torch.randperm(n); old, new = old[perm], new[perm]
d = new - old
se = d.std(unbiased=False) / math.sqrt(n)
print(f"diff {d.mean():.3f}  paired SE {se:.4f}  z {d.mean() / se:.2f}")  # 0.040 0.0186 2.15
b = int(((new == 1) & (old == 0)).sum()); c = int(((new == 0) & (old == 1)).sum())
print("McNemar chi2:", round((b - c) ** 2 / (b + c), 2))                 # 4.57

# 4) paired bootstrap: resample items, keep the pairs together
g = torch.Generator().manual_seed(0)
idx = torch.randint(0, n, (10000, n), generator=g)
boot = d[idx].mean(dim=1)
lo, hi = torch.quantile(boot, torch.tensor([0.025, 0.975], dtype=boot.dtype))
print(f"bootstrap 95% CI [{lo:.3f}, {hi:.3f}]")                           # [0.002, 0.078]

# 5) judge vs human agreement: Cohen's kappa, formula (155.4)
judge = torch.tensor([1] * 145 + [0] * 55)
human = torch.tensor([1] * 120 + [0] * 25 + [1] * 15 + [0] * 40)
po = (judge == human).float().mean()
pj, ph = judge.float().mean(), human.float().mean()
pe = pj * ph + (1 - pj) * (1 - ph)
print(f"agreement {po:.3f}  chance {pe:.4f}  kappa {(po - pe) / (1 - pe):.3f}")   # 0.800 0.5788 0.525

    The bootstrap interval \([0.002, 0.078]\) is close to the formula interval \([0.004, 0.076]\) from the worked example, as it should be for 400 items.

    Common mistakes

    • Reporting one number without an error bar. “81% vs 79%” on 300 items is noise. Always report the interval and \(n\).
    • Unpaired tests on paired data. Both systems answered the same items; use (155.6), McNemar or a paired bootstrap. The unpaired test throws away most of your power.
    • Judging in one order only. Position bias then moves the win rate by many points. Judge both orders, or at least randomize the order per item.
    • Trusting the judge without checking it. Measure agreement with humans (kappa, and both error types) on your data before using a judge as a gate. Re-check when you change the judge model or prompt.
    • Letting length win. If the new system writes 40% longer answers and wins the judge, check the length-controlled result before you celebrate.
    • Only watching the average. Slices catch the regressions that averages hide (Figure 5).
    • Tuning on the test set. After many prompt edits against the same suite, its score is optimistic. Keep a held-out part and refresh it.
    • Treating clustered items as independent. Many questions about one document or video give error bars that are too small. Bootstrap by cluster.

    Interview questions

    What are the pros and cons of static benchmarks, arenas, and your own test set?basic
    Static benchmarks (MMLU, GSM8K) are cheap, repeatable and comparable with papers, but they can leak into training data, they saturate, and they do not look like your users’ requests. Arenas use fresh, real prompts and pairwise human votes, so they are hard to contaminate, but voters reward style and length and the domain is general. Your own test set is the only one that measures your system and lets you find failing slices, but it costs effort, must be maintained, and is often small. Use benchmarks and arenas to shortlist models, and your own set to decide.
    How does Chatbot Arena turn pairwise votes into a leaderboard?core
    It fits a Bradley–Terry model: each model has a rating, and \(P(i \succ j) = 1/(1 + 10^{(R_j - R_i)/400})\). The ratings are the maximum-likelihood fit to all votes at once (a logistic regression on rating differences). Unlike online Elo, the result does not depend on the order of the votes. Confidence intervals come from bootstrapping the votes. A 100-point gap means about a 64% win rate.
    Name the main biases of an LLM judge and how to reduce each one.core
    Position bias: prefers the answer shown first (GPT-4 was consistent across orders only 65% of the time in the MT-Bench study); fix by judging both orders and calling inconsistent verdicts a tie. Length bias: prefers longer answers; fix with length-controlled win rates (a regression that removes the length effect), length limits, or rubrics that penalize padding. Self-preference: favors its own model family; use a judge from another family or several judges. Also: give a rubric, ask for a reason before the verdict, use temperature 0, and pin the judge version.
    Why does swapping the order fix position bias but not length bias?advanced
    Position bias depends on the slot, not the answer: in one order it pushes answer A up, in the other order it pushes answer B up by the same amount, so averaging the two orders cancels it. Length bias depends on the answer itself: the longer answer is longer in both orders, so it gets the same push both times and the average keeps it. You need a different tool for length, such as a regression that estimates the verdict at equal length.
    How do you know if your LLM judge is good enough to use?core
    Label a sample (a few hundred items) with humans, ideally two humans to know the human-human agreement. Compare the judge with them: raw agreement, Cohen’s kappa (which removes chance agreement), and the confusion matrix, because passing bad answers and failing good answers have different costs. Check per slice. If the judge agrees with humans about as well as humans agree with each other, it is usable. Re-check after any change to the judge model or prompt.
    Old system 75%, new system 79% on the same 400 items. Is the gain real?core
    It depends on the pairing. An unpaired test gives SE ≈ 0.030 and z ≈ 1.35, not significant. But the items are shared, so use a paired test. If the new system wins 36 items and loses 20, the paired SE is \(\sqrt{(0.14 - 0.04^2)/400} = 0.0186\), z ≈ 2.15, and McNemar’s \(\chi^2 = 16^2/56 = 4.57\) (p ≈ 0.03): significant. The answer comes from the discordant items only.
    How many test items do you need to detect a 2-point improvement?core
    Use \(n \approx (z_{\alpha/2} + z_\beta)^2 \sigma^2 / \delta^2\). For 5% significance and 80% power, \((1.96 + 0.84)^2 \approx 7.85\). Unpaired at a 75% base rate: \(7.85 \times (0.1875 + 0.1771)/0.0004 \approx 7{,}155\). Paired with 10% discordance: \(7.85 \times (0.10 - 0.0004)/0.0004 \approx 1{,}955\). The key point is the \(1/\delta^2\) scaling: half the effect needs four times the data.
    Why is the Wald interval bad for small test sets, and what do you use instead?basic
    \(p \pm 1.96\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\) assumes a normal shape, which fails for small \(n\) or \(p\) near 0 or 1. It can go above 1 (18/20 gives [0.769, 1.031]) and gives zero width for 20/20. The Wilson interval stays inside [0, 1] and has good coverage for small samples: 18/20 gives [0.699, 0.972], 20/20 gives [0.839, 1]. A bootstrap is another option.
    Your test questions come in groups of ten per document. What changes in the statistics?advanced
    The items are clustered: questions about the same document tend to be right or wrong together, so the effective sample size is smaller than the number of questions. Treating them as independent gives error bars that are too small and too many “significant” results. Use clustered standard errors or bootstrap whole documents. The same holds for turns of one conversation, or many questions about one video.
    Design a regression suite and release gate for an LLM product.advanced
    Collect items from real traffic, past failures and expert-written hard cases; tag slices (task, language, length, risk). For each item pick the cheapest correct grader: exact or rule checks, execution, or a rubric-based LLM judge validated against humans. Version the suite, graders and judge prompt; keep a held-out part. On each change, run old and new on the same items, compute paired differences per slice, and apply a rule: block if a slice is confidently worse, pass if it is confidently not worse than a tolerance, and send to review if the evidence is too thin. Safety slices use zero tolerance. Also check cost and latency. After launch, add online signals and feed new failures back into the suite.
    The new model wins 70% of pairwise judge comparisons, but users do not notice. What could be going on?advanced
    Likely causes: length or style bias (the new model writes longer, better-formatted answers; check length-controlled win rate and answer lengths); position bias if only one order was judged; self-preference if the judge is from the same family; a judge prompt that measures something users do not care about; a test set that does not look like real traffic; or a win rate on items where both answers are fine (the gain is in cases that rarely matter). Check judge agreement with humans on these pairs and look at online metrics such as retries and edits.

    Key takeaways

    • Benchmarks and arenas help you choose a model; only your own test set, with slices, tells you whether your system works.
    • Arenas turn pairwise votes into ratings with Bradley–Terry (155.1, 155.2); a 100-point gap is a 64% win rate.
    • LLM judges can match human agreement, but they prefer the first answer, longer answers and sometimes their own. Judge both orders, control for length, and check the judge against humans with kappa (155.4).
    • Use Wilson intervals (155.5) for small sets and paired analysis (155.6, 155.7, bootstrap) when two systems answer the same items; it can turn noise into a clear result.
    • Sample size grows as \(1/\delta^2\) (155.8): 300 paired items can see a 5-point change, not a 1- or 2-point change.
    • A regression suite is versioned, sliced, and gated per slice (155.9): block confident regressions, pass confident non-inferiority, and send thin evidence to review.

    Key formulas

    \[ P(i \succ j) = \frac{1}{1 + 10^{(R_j - R_i)/400}}, \qquad \kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e} \]
