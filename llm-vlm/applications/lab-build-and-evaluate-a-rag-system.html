    Why this matters

    A RAG system (retrieval-augmented generation) answers questions from your own documents: it searches for a few relevant pieces of text and gives them to an LLM together with the question. It is easy to build a demo that works on five questions. It is much harder to know whether it works on the next five hundred. In this lab you build a small but complete RAG system and then measure it, find out what breaks, fix the biggest problem and measure again.

    Sessions 152 and 153 (session 152 and session 153) explained the parts: chunking, BM25, dense embeddings, hybrid search, reranking, and the metrics recall@k, MRR and faithfulness. Session 155 explained graders and statistics. This lab puts them together on one document set. You will:

    1. index 32 internal documents of a made-up company with BM25, a dense embedder in a FAISS index, reciprocal rank fusion and a cross-encoder reranker;
    2. write a test set of 60 questions with known answers and known source passages, including 6 questions that have no answer in the documents;
    3. measure retrieval (recall@k, MRR) and answers (accuracy, abstention, faithfulness), with three different faithfulness graders;
    4. sort every failure into a category, fix the biggest category, and check with a paired test whether the improvement is real.

    The result is a lesson that you will see again in real projects. Our first system answered only 28 of 60 questions correctly. The cause was not the LLM and not the embedder. It was the chunker: most chunks of a product manual did not say which product they were about. One small change, a header line on every chunk, raised retrieval recall@5 from 0.556 to 1.000 and accuracy from 28 to 50 of 60. A second, smaller change looked like another gain (52 of 60), but the statistics say we cannot tell it apart from noise.

    Interview tip“How would you evaluate a RAG system?” is one of the most common LLM system-design questions. A strong answer separates retrieval from generation, names the metrics, describes the test set (with sources and unanswerable questions), and says how you would find and fix the biggest failure mode. This lab gives you real numbers for each step.

    Setup

    You need Python 3.10 or newer, PyTorch, Hugging Face transformers, NumPy and faiss-cpu. Four models play four roles. All of them are small enough for a laptop CPU.

    • Sparse retriever: BM25, written in 15 lines of Python. No model.
    • Dense retriever: sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2, a 6-layer BERT-style encoder that maps text to a 384-dimensional vector by mean pooling (averaging the token vectors); it truncates input at 256 tokens [Certain]. The vectors go into a FAISS IndexFlatIP, an exact inner-product index.
    • Reranker: cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L6-v2, a 6-layer cross-encoder trained on the MS MARCO passage-ranking data. It reads the question and one passage together and outputs one relevance score [Certain].
    • Reader (generator): Qwen/Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, a chat model with 1.54B parameters (28 layers, hidden size 1,536, 12 query heads and 2 key-value heads) [Certain]. It decodes greedily, so the answers are deterministic.

    Step 1: the document set and the index

    A good RAG test needs documents whose facts the model cannot know from pretraining. Otherwise a correct answer tells you nothing about retrieval. So we generate the internal knowledge base of a made-up instrument company, with a seeded random generator:

    • 20 product manuals, each with seven sections: overview, installation, specifications, error codes, maintenance, warranty and safety. The products come in families with similar names (Vela-100, Vela-200, Vela-300, Orin-3, Orin-5, Orin-7, …) and the same sentence patterns, so many chunks look alike. 12 of the manuals have test questions; the other 8 are realistic distractors.
    • 6 policies (travel, expenses, IT security, on-call, leave, lab safety), with numbers such as hotel limits and deadlines.
    • 6 incident reports, each with a date, a duration, a root cause and an owner team.

    In total: 32 documents and 6,816 words. Like real manuals, only the overview names the product. Later sentences say “The device weighs 5.1 kg”. Keep that in mind; it decides the whole lab.

    Chunking

    The baseline chunker does what many tutorials do: join the sections of a document and cut the text into windows of 50 words, with no overlap. This gives 159 chunks of 42.9 words on average (the last chunk of each document is shorter), or 56.6 WordPiece tokens on average and 82 at most, far below the embedder’s limit of 256.

    Sparse retrieval: BM25

    BM25 scores a chunk \(d\) for a question \(q\) by the question words it contains. Rare words count more, repeated words count less and less, and long chunks are penalized a little:

    \[ s_{\text{BM25}}(q, d) = \sum_{t \in q} \ln\!\left(1 + \frac{N - n_t + 0.5}{n_t + 0.5}\right) \cdot \frac{f(t, d)\,(k_1 + 1)}{f(t, d) + k_1\left(1 - b + b\,\dfrac{|d|}{\text{avgdl}}\right)} \tag{160.1} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(t \in q\)
    each distinct word of the question (we lower-case and split on anything that is not a letter, digit or decimal point)
    \(N,\ n_t\)
    number of chunks (159), and number of chunks that contain word \(t\)
    \(f(t, d)\)
    how often word \(t\) occurs in chunk \(d\)
    \(|d|,\ \text{avgdl}\)
    length of chunk \(d\) in words, and the average length over all chunks
    \(k_1 = 1.2,\ b = 0.75\)
    the usual settings: \(k_1\) controls how fast repeated words stop adding score, \(b\) how strongly long chunks are penalized

    The first factor is the IDF (inverse document frequency) of the word. A code like “E-9251” is split into “e” and “9251”; “9251” occurs in one chunk only, so its IDF is high, and BM25 finds the chunk at once. That is exactly what dense embedders are bad at.

    AnalogyBM25 is a librarian who searches the index cards for your exact words; she never misses a part number, but she does not know that “heavy” and “weighs” mean the same. The dense retriever is a librarian who understands what you mean, but who reads “E-9251” and “E-9215” as almost the same thing. Hybrid search asks both and combines their lists; the reranker is a third, slower expert who reads each candidate page carefully.

    Dense retrieval: embeddings and FAISS

    The embedder \(f\) maps the question and every chunk to unit vectors. The score is their dot product, which for unit vectors equals the cosine similarity:

    \[ s_{\text{dense}}(q, d) = f(q)^\top f(d), \qquad \lVert f(\cdot) \rVert = 1 \tag{160.2} \]

    Dense retrieval matches meaning: “How heavy is the Vela-200?” matches “The device weighs 5.1 kg” although they share no word except “the”. FAISS stores the 159 vectors of size 384 and returns the top 50 by inner product. With 159 chunks an exact search is instant; for millions of chunks you would use an approximate index (session 151).

    Step 2: write the test set

    The test set is the most important part of the lab. If it is wrong, every number after it is wrong. Each of our 60 questions has four fields: the question, its type, the gold document and the gold fact, an exact string from the document such as “weighs 5.1 kg”, plus a short key (“5.1”) that a correct answer must contain.

    TypeCountExampleWhat it tests
    spec24How heavy is the Orin-5?Paraphrase (“heavy” vs “weighs”), and telling similar products apart
    code12My Lumen-5 shows E-5500. How do I fix it?Exact identifiers, where BM25 should win
    policy12When does a purchase need sign-off from a director?Paraphrase (“sign-off” vs “written approval”)

    Three rules make a test set useful. Write questions the way users ask, not by copying sentences from the documents; otherwise BM25 looks perfect. Record the source, not only the answer, so you can score retrieval separately from generation. Add unanswerable questions, especially near misses like “Vela-500” (the product does not exist, but Vela-100, -200 and -300 do). Systems that always answer look good on a test without such questions.

    Which chunk is “relevant”? The chunks change when you change the chunker, so we do not store chunk ids in the test set. We store the character position of the key inside the gold document and call a chunk relevant if it covers that position:

    \[ \mathrm{Rel}(q) = \{\, c \;:\; \operatorname{doc}(c) = d_q \ \text{ and } \ [s_c, e_c) \cap [s_q, e_q) \neq \emptyset \,\}, \qquad \mathrm{Recall@}k(q) = \mathbb{1}\big[\mathrm{Rel}(q) \cap \mathrm{Top}_k(q) \neq \emptyset\big] \tag{160.3} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(d_q\)
    the gold document of question \(q\)
    \([s_c, e_c)\)
    the character span of chunk \(c\) inside its document
    \([s_q, e_q)\)
    the character span of the answer key (for example “5.1”) inside the gold fact
    \(\mathrm{Top}_k(q)\)
    the first \(k\) chunks returned for \(q\)
    \(\mathbb{1}[\cdot]\)
    1 if the condition is true, else 0; we average over the 54 answerable questions

    With one relevant chunk per question, recall@k is the same as the hit rate: “is the answer chunk in the top \(k\)?”. The second metric rewards putting it high:

    \[ \mathrm{MRR@}20 = \frac{1}{|Q|} \sum_{q \in Q} \frac{1}{\mathrm{rank}_q}, \qquad \frac{1}{\mathrm{rank}_q} = 0 \ \text{ if } \ \mathrm{rank}_q \gt 20 \tag{160.4} \]

    Here \(\mathrm{rank}_q\) is the position of the first relevant chunk. We cut at 20 because the reranker only sees the first 20 fused candidates.

    Algorithm 1: build a RAG test set
    1. Choose documents whose facts the reader cannot know (private, recent or synthetic).
    2. For each question type you care about, write questions in the users’ words; for each one store the gold document, the exact gold fact and a short answer key.
    3. Add 10% or more questions with no answer, including near misses (wrong product, wrong city, unknown code).
    4. Locate each key by character position in its document; define relevant chunks by span overlap (160.3), so the same test set works for every chunker.
    5. Read 10 questions and their gold facts by hand to check that the answer is really there and unique.

    Step 3: hybrid search and a reranker

    BM25 and dense retrieval fail on different questions, so we combine their top-50 lists with reciprocal rank fusion (RRF). RRF ignores the raw scores, which live on different scales, and uses only the ranks:

    \[ s_{\text{RRF}}(d) = \frac{w}{k + \mathrm{rank}_{\text{BM25}}(d)} + \frac{1 - w}{k + \mathrm{rank}_{\text{dense}}(d)} \tag{160.5} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\mathrm{rank}_{\text{BM25}}(d)\)
    position of chunk \(d\) in the BM25 list (1 = best); if \(d\) is not in the top 50, that term is 0
    \(k\)
    a smoothing constant; \(k = 60\) is the value proposed by Cormack et al. (2009) and the common default [Certain]
    \(w\)
    weight of the BM25 list; plain RRF is \(w = 0.5\) (both lists equal), which gives the same order as the unweighted sum

    Then the cross-encoder rescores the first 20 fused chunks. A cross-encoder reads question and chunk together through all its layers, so it can check that the chunk is about the right product and the right code. That is too slow for 159 chunks per question in a large system, but cheap for 20. The reader gets the top 5 after reranking.

    Worked example: why fusion alone is not enough (question “What does error E-9251 mean?”, with headers)

    The gold chunk is rank 1 in BM25 (the token “9251” is unique) but rank 18 in the dense list. Its RRF score with \(k = 60\) (the sum form, both weights 1) is \(1/61 + 1/78 = 0.01639 + 0.01282 = 0.02921\).

    Another error-code chunk of the Vela-200 is rank 2 in BM25 and rank 6 in the dense list: \(1/62 + 1/66 = 0.03128\). It wins, although it does not contain the code. In our run, fusion puts the gold chunk at rank 8, behind seven chunks that are “quite good” in both lists. RRF rewards agreement, and one list that is very sure is outvoted.

    The reranker gives the gold chunk a score of 4.80 and the next chunk 2.24. Final rank: 1. Fusion is a good way to build the candidate list; the reranker decides the order.

    Step 5: measure the answers

    Retrieval metrics say whether the right text reached the reader. Answer metrics say whether the user got the right answer. We use three, each with a precise definition.

    Accuracy. For an answerable question, the answer is correct if it contains the key (numbers must match as whole numbers, so “12” does not match “120”) and is not a refusal. For an unanswerable question, the answer is correct if it is the refusal “Not in the documents.” (an abstention):

    \[ \mathrm{Acc} = \frac{1}{|Q|}\left[\ \sum_{q \in Q_{\text{ans}}} \mathbb{1}\big[\text{key}_q \in a_q \ \wedge\ a_q \neq \varnothing\big] \;+\; \sum_{q \in Q_{\text{none}}} \mathbb{1}\big[a_q = \varnothing\big] \right] \tag{160.6} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(Q_{\text{ans}},\ Q_{\text{none}}\)
    the 54 answerable and the 6 unanswerable questions
    \(a_q\)
    the reader’s answer; \(a_q = \varnothing\) means it abstained
    \(\text{key}_q \in a_q\)
    the answer key occurs in the answer text

    Faithfulness. An answer is faithful if every fact in it is stated in the five passages the reader saw. This is the RAGAS definition (Es et al., 2023) with one claim per answer, since our answers are one sentence:

    \[ F = \frac{|\{\text{claims of the answer supported by the context}\}|}{|\{\text{claims of the answer}\}|}, \qquad \text{an abstention counts as faithful} \tag{160.7} \]

    Faithfulness is not correctness. A faithful answer can be wrong (the context itself was wrong or about another product), and an unfaithful answer can be right by luck. The 2×2 table of Figure 4 makes this concrete.

    “Supported” needs a grader. We compare three:

    1. Reference labels. A strict rule: the passage that contains the answer’s value must also name the entity of the question (product, error code, incident id or date). For an error code, the value must be in the text that belongs to that code. We then read all 180 answers and corrected the rule in 5 cases (for example “45 euros” for Berlin, where the sentence gives 45 for Lisbon and 70 for Berlin). These are our ground truth.
    2. A number rule. Faithful if every number in the answer appears somewhere in the context. This is cheap and popular.
    3. An LLM judge. The same Qwen2.5-1.5B model reads context, question and answer and answers “yes” or “no” to “is every fact in the answer stated in the context?”; we take \(p(\text{yes}) \gt 0.5\).
    NumbersThe two cheap graders missed every unfaithful answer in our baseline: the number rule flagged 1 of 32 and the judge 0 of 32. A grader that cannot say “no” gives you a high faithfulness score and no information. Always measure your grader against 50 to 100 hand labels before you use it (session 155).

    For the baseline, the answer metrics are: accuracy 28 of 60 (0.467); 24 of 54 answerable questions correct; 4 of 6 unanswerable questions correctly refused; 28 of 60 answers faithful by the reference labels (21 of the 53 that were not refusals).

    How sure are we about 0.467 with only 60 questions? The Wilson interval gives a 95% range for a proportion; it behaves better than \(\hat{p} \pm 1.96\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}\) for small \(n\):

    \[ \frac{\hat{p} + \dfrac{z^2}{2n}}{1 + \dfrac{z^2}{n}} \;\pm\; \frac{z}{1 + \dfrac{z^2}{n}} \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}} \tag{160.8} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\hat{p}\)
    the measured accuracy (28/60 = 0.467)
    \(n\)
    the number of questions (60)
    \(z = 1.96\)
    the normal quantile for a 95% interval
    Worked example: the interval for 28 of 60

    \(\hat{p} = 0.4667\), \(z^2/n = 3.8416/60 = 0.0640\). Center: \((0.4667 + 0.0320)/1.0640 = 0.4687\). Half-width: \((1.96/1.0640)\sqrt{0.4667 \cdot 0.5333/60 + 3.8416/14400} = 1.842 \times 0.0664 = 0.1224\). Interval: [0.346, 0.591].

    The interval is 24 points wide. With 60 questions you can see a big change, like the one in Step 7, but not a change of 3 points. For that you need several hundred questions, or a paired test on the same questions.

    Step 6: find the biggest failure mode

    Averages do not tell you what to fix. Categories do. We give every question of the baseline exactly one label, in this order:

    Algorithm 2: sort every answer into one failure category
    1. If the answer is correct (160.6): ok.
    2. Else, if the question has no answer in the documents: answered anyway (Barnett et al. call this “missing content”).
    3. Else, if no relevant chunk (160.3) is in the 5 passages the reader saw: missed by retrieval (“missed the top ranked documents”).
    4. Else, if the reader said “Not in the documents.”: refused.
    5. Else: misread (the answer was in the context, but the reader used the wrong part; “not extracted”).
    6. Count the categories; read 3 to 5 examples of the biggest one and write down the cause in one sentence.

    Barnett et al. (2024) list seven failure points from three real RAG projects: missing content, missed the top ranked documents, not in context, not extracted, wrong format, incorrect specificity and incomplete [Certain]. Our five categories are the first four plus “ok”, because our answers are short facts.

    The diagnosis in one sentence: a chunk from the middle of a manual does not say which product it describes, so neither retriever can match “Vela-200” to it, and when such a chunk reaches the reader, the reader cannot tell the Vela-200 from the Vela-300. It also explains the 28 wrong-and-unfaithful answers of Figure 4.

    Step 7: fix it and measure again

    Fix 1: a header on every chunk

    We keep the same 159 chunks and the same boundaries, and add one line in front of each: the document title and the section where the chunk starts. The new chunk text is used for BM25, for the embedder, for the reranker and for the reader:

    \[ \tilde{c} \;=\; \text{title}(d) \;\oplus\; \text{“ > ”} \;\oplus\; \text{section}(c) \;\oplus\; \text{“: ”} \;\oplus\; c \tag{160.9} \]

    Here \(\oplus\) means string concatenation. For example: “Vela-200 flow meter user manual > Installation: the device on a DIN rail clip … The device weighs 5.1 kg …”. The header costs 12 WordPiece tokens per chunk on average (56.6 to 68.7) and 61 more prompt tokens per question for the reader (430.6 to 491.3). This idea is used widely; Anthropic’s “contextual retrieval” goes further and lets an LLM write 50 to 100 tokens of context for each chunk, and reports that it cut the top-20 retrieval failure rate from 5.7% to 3.7% with contextual embeddings, to 2.9% with contextual BM25 added, and to 1.9% with a reranker on top [Certain].

    With headers, BM25 recall@5 rises from 0.444 to 0.741 and dense from 0.481 to 0.833, and after reranking every answer chunk is in the top 3. Accuracy goes from 28 to 50 of 60 (Wilson interval [0.720, 0.907]), and spec questions from 2 of 24 to 24 of 24.

    Is it real? A paired test

    Both systems answered the same 60 questions, so we compare them question by question. Only the questions where they disagree carry information. Let \(b\) be the number of questions the new system fixed and \(c\) the number it broke. If the two systems were equally good, each disagreement would go either way with probability 1/2. The exact two-sided sign test (McNemar’s test for small counts) gives

    \[ p = \min\!\left(1,\ 2 \sum_{j=0}^{\min(b,c)} \binom{b+c}{j} \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{b+c}\right) \tag{160.10} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(b\)
    questions wrong before and right after
    \(c\)
    questions right before and wrong after
    \(p\)
    the probability of a split at least this uneven if the two systems were equally good
    Worked example: fix 1 and fix 2

    Fix 1 (baseline to headers): \(b = 28\), \(c = 6\). Then \(p = 2 \sum_{j=0}^{6} \binom{34}{j} / 2^{34} = 0.000195\). The improvement is real.

    Look at the 6 broken questions too. With headers the reader refused 4 of them (“Not in the documents.”, with the right chunk at rank 1) and misread 2. Retrieval got better or stayed the same for all 6, and 2 of them had been right only by luck in the baseline (correct but not faithful). A small reader is sensitive to small changes of its prompt.

    Fix 2 (headers to whole sentences inside sections, 194 chunks of 35.1 words on average): \(b = 6\), \(c = 4\), so \(p = 2 \sum_{j=0}^{4} \binom{10}{j} / 2^{10} = 0.754\). The accuracy went from 50 to 52, but a split of 6 to 4 happens easily by chance. We cannot claim that fix 2 helps answers, although it clearly helps retrieval at rank 1 (0.907 to 0.981).

    Watch outDo not tune the system on the same 60 questions and then report the result on them. We designed both fixes after reading failures of this test set, so our “after” numbers are optimistic. In a real project, keep a second, held-out set of questions that you never read while debugging, and report on it.

    What to fix next

    After fix 1, retrieval is no longer the problem: recall@5 is 1.000. The remaining 10 errors are reader errors: 5 refusals with the right chunk at rank 1, 4 misreads and 1 answer to the unanswerable Vela-500 question (“3.0 kg”, copied from the Vela-300). The next fixes are on the generation side: a larger reader, an instruction that lists the products in the passages, or putting only the top 3 passages in the prompt. Each one must be measured the same way.

def chunk(docs, mode):
    out = []
    def mk(d, s, e, sec):
        body = d["text"][s:e]

        for name, t in d["sections"]:

            starts.append((len(text), name)); text += t
        d["text"] = text
        if mode == "section":   # whole sentences, never across a section, at most W words
            ends = [p for p, _ in starts[1:]] + [len(text) + 1]
            for (p0, sec), p1 in zip(starts, ends):
                sents = [(m.start() + p0, m.end() + p0) for m in re.finditer(r"\S.*?[.!?](?=\s|$)", text[p0:p1 - 1])]
                cur = []
                for a, b in sents:

                        out.append(mk(d, cur[0][0], cur[-1][1], sec)); cur = []
                    cur.append((a, b))
                if cur: out.append(mk(d, cur[0][0], cur[-1][1], sec))
            continue

        for i in range(0, len(words), W):
            s, e = words[i][0], words[min(i + W, len(words)) - 1][1]
            sec = [n for p, n in starts if p <= s][-1]
            out.append(mk(d, s, e, sec))
    return out

D = [tok(c["text"]) for c in chunks]; N = len(D); avg = sum(map(len, D)) / N
df = Counter(w for d in D for w in set(d)); TF = [Counter(d) for d in D]
def bm25(q, k1=1.2, b=0.75):
    sc = np.zeros(N)
    for w in dict.fromkeys(tok(q)):   # fixed order: sums are reproducible across runs
        if w not in df: continue
        idf = math.log(1 + (N - df[w] + 0.5) / (df[w] + 0.5))
        for i in range(N):
            f = TF[i][w]
            if f: sc[i] += idf * f * (k1 + 1) / (f + k1 * (1 - b + b * len(D[i]) / avg))
    return sc

        v = (h * m).sum(1) / m.sum(1); out.append(torch.nn.functional.normalize(v, dim=-1))
    return torch.cat(out).numpy().astype("float32")

    o = np.argsort(-s, kind="stable"); return [ids[j] for j in o], [float(s[j]) for j in o]

    return [i for i, _ in sorted(sc.items(), key=lambda x: (-x[1], x[0]))]

    """Grade the saved answers: accuracy, three faithfulness graders, failure categories, statistics."""
import json, math, re, sys
from collections import Counter
from data import ERRORS
CAUSES = ["expired TLS certificate", "database migration", "firmware update that reset the clock", "full disk", "wrong DNS record", "memory leak"]
PHR = [p for e in ERRORS for p in e[:2]] + CAUSES + ["nitrile", "neoprene", "butyl"]
NUM = r"(?<![\w.])\d+(?:\.\d+)?(?![\w])"
def nums(s): return set(re.findall(NUM, s.replace("±", " ")))
def abst(a): return "not in the documents" in a.lower()
def correct(q, a):
    if q["type"] == "none": return abst(a)
    if abst(a): return False
    k = q["keys"][0]
    if re.fullmatch(r"\d+(\.\d+)?", k): return k in nums(a)
    return k.lower() in a.lower()
def strip_cite(a): return re.sub(r"\[\d+\]", " ", a)
NAMES = r"(?:Vela|Orin|Tamsin|Brio|Kestrel|Lumen|Corvo)-\w+|E-\d+|INC-\d+|\d+ \w+ 2025"
def clean(a): return re.sub(NAMES, " ", strip_cite(a))   # names and ids are not claims
def values(q, a):
    v = nums(clean(a))
    a2 = clean(a)
    v |= {p for p in PHR if p.lower() in a2.lower()}
    return v
def grounded(q, a, ctx):  # cheap rule: every number of the answer occurs somewhere in the context
    if abst(a): return True
    c = nums(strip_cite(ctx.replace("\n", " ")))
    return all(n in c for n in nums(clean(a)))
def entity(q):
    for pat in [r"E-\d+", r"INC-\d+", r"\d+ \w+ 2025", r"(?:Vela|Orin|Tamsin|Brio|Kestrel|Lumen|Corvo)-\w+"]:
        m = re.search(pat, q["q"])
        if m: return m.group(0)
    return None
def strict(q, a, passages):  # reference rule: value and entity in the same passage
    if abst(a): return True
    v = values(q, a); e = entity(q)
    if not v: return None
    for p in passages:
        if e and e.startswith("E-") and e in p:   # an error code: look only at the text that belongs to this code
            p = p[p.index(e):]; nxt = p.find("Error E-", 1); p = p if nxt < 0 else p[:nxt]
        pl = p.lower(); pn = nums(clean(p).replace("±", " "))
        if all((x in pn) if re.fullmatch(r"\d+(\.\d+)?", x) else (x.lower() in pl) for x in v) and (e is None or e.lower() in pl):
            return True
    return False

def wilson(k, n, z=1.96):
    p = k / n; d = 1 + z * z / n; c = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / d; h = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / d
    return c - h, c + h
def rank(L, rel):
    for r, i in enumerate(L):
        if i in rel: return r + 1
    return None

    Common mistakes

    • Measuring only the final answer. If you do not score retrieval separately, you cannot tell a chunking problem from a reader problem. In our baseline, 22 of 32 failures were retrieval misses.
    • Chunks without context. A fixed window cut out of the middle of a document loses its title and section. With many similar documents this is often the biggest failure; add a header (160.9) or chunk by structure.
    • Copying questions from the documents. Questions with the exact words of the source make BM25 look perfect. Write them as users would (“heavy”, not “weighs”).
    • No unanswerable questions. Then a system that always answers looks best. Our baseline answered the non-existent Vela-500 and Kestrel-20 with numbers from their siblings.
    • Trusting a faithfulness grader you did not check. Our number rule and our LLM judge called almost every wrong answer faithful. Check any grader against hand labels first.
    • Confusing faithfulness with correctness. An answer can follow the context exactly and still be wrong, if retrieval gave the wrong context. Report both.
    • Reporting small differences on small test sets. With 60 questions the 95% interval is about ±12 points. Use a paired test (160.10) on the same questions, and do not claim a gain like our fix 2 without one.
    • Tuning on the test set. Every fix you design after reading failures makes that test set optimistic. Keep a held-out set.

    Interview questions

    How do you evaluate a RAG system end to end?basic
    Build a test set of real-looking questions, each with a known answer and a known source passage, plus some questions with no answer in the corpus. Score retrieval and generation separately. Retrieval: recall@k (is a relevant chunk in the top \(k\)?) and MRR, per stage (sparse, dense, fused, reranked) and per question type. Generation: answer accuracy against the known answers, abstention on unanswerable questions, and faithfulness (is every claim supported by the passages?). Then sort failures into categories (missed by retrieval, misread, refused, answered anyway), fix the biggest one, and compare old and new system on the same questions with a paired test.
    Why combine BM25 and dense retrieval? What does each miss?basic
    BM25 matches exact words with IDF weights, so rare identifiers (error codes, part numbers, names) are found reliably, but synonyms and paraphrases are missed (“heavy” vs “weighs”). Dense retrieval matches meaning, so paraphrases work, but small embedders blur identifiers: “E-9251” and “E-9215” look alike. In our lab with headers, BM25 had recall@5 of 1.00 on error codes and 0.46 on spec questions; dense had 0.42 and 0.96. RRF fusion reached 0.92 and 1.00.
    Explain reciprocal rank fusion. Why ranks and not scores? What is k for?core
    RRF scores each document by \(\sum_r 1/(k + \mathrm{rank}_r(d))\) over the result lists (160.5). Raw scores of BM25 and cosine similarity have different scales and distributions, so adding them needs normalization that is fragile; ranks are comparable. \(k\) (60 by default) flattens the curve: with small \(k\), the first places of each list dominate; with large \(k\), documents that appear in both lists win. A weakness: RRF rewards agreement, so a document that is first in one list and 18th in the other can lose to documents that are mid-ranked in both; in our run that pushed a gold chunk from rank 1 (BM25) to rank 8, and only the reranker fixed it.
    What does a cross-encoder reranker do better than a bi-encoder, and what does it cost?core
    A bi-encoder embeds question and chunk separately, so their interaction is a single dot product; chunks can be embedded once, offline. A cross-encoder reads question and chunk together through all layers, so attention can compare every question token with every chunk token: it can check that the chunk is about the right product or code. The cost is one forward pass per (question, chunk) pair at query time, so it is used only on the top 20 to 100 candidates. In our baseline it raised recall@1 from 0.296 to 0.463; it cannot recover chunks that are not in its candidate list.
    Faithfulness vs correctness: give an example of each without the other.core
    Faithful but wrong: retrieval returns the Vela-300 warranty chunk without a product name, and the reader answers “60 months” for the Vela-200; if the chunk had said “Vela-300”, the reader could have noticed. More generally, a faithful answer repeats a wrong or outdated source. Correct but unfaithful: our baseline answered 40 °C for the Tamsin-X2, which is right, but it took the number from a chunk of another product, so it was right by luck. Faithfulness measures the reader given its context; correctness measures the whole system. You need both.
    How would you build a faithfulness grader you can trust?advanced
    Label 50 to 100 answers by hand first, including clearly unfaithful ones. Then measure each candidate grader against those labels: agreement, and especially recall on the unfaithful class. In our lab the LLM judge (a 1.5B model) said “yes” to all 37 unfaithful answers over two configurations and “no” to all 17 refusals, so its agreement was 20/60 on the baseline. Improvements: a larger judge from a different model family, claim-by-claim checking (split the answer into claims, verify each against specific passages), an NLI model, asking the judge to quote the supporting sentence and checking that the quote exists, and domain rules such as “the entity must appear in the supporting passage”. Re-check the grader when the system changes.
    Your RAG system has recall@5 of 1.0 but 17% wrong answers. Where do you look?core
    At the reader and the prompt. Sort the errors: refusals with the right chunk present (the reader is too cautious or confused by distractor passages), misreads (it used the wrong number from the right passage, or a sibling’s passage), and answers to unanswerable questions. In our lab with headers, all 5 refusals had the gold chunk at rank 1. Try fewer passages (top 3), a stronger reader, a prompt that tells the reader to match the entity, and check that facts are not cut across chunk boundaries (3 of our 4 misreads).
    How big must a RAG test set be?advanced
    It depends on the effect you want to see. For one system, the 95% interval of an accuracy near 0.5 is about \(\pm 1.96\sqrt{0.25/n}\): ±0.127 for \(n = 60\), ±0.044 for \(n = 500\). For comparing two systems, a paired test on the same questions is much more sensitive, because only disagreements count. Our fix 1 (28 fixed, 6 broken) was clear with 60 questions (\(p = 0.0002\)); fix 2 (6 fixed, 4 broken, \(p = 0.75\)) was not. Start with 50 to 100 carefully built questions per important question type, and grow the set from production logs.
    What is contextual retrieval, and when does it help?advanced
    Prepend context to each chunk before indexing it: at the simplest, the document title and section path (our 160.9); in Anthropic’s version, 50 to 100 tokens written by an LLM that reads the whole document and the chunk. It helps most when chunks are not self-contained: pronouns, “the device”, tables without titles, many similar documents. Anthropic reports a drop of the top-20 failure rate from 5.7% to 3.7% with contextual embeddings and to 1.9% with contextual BM25 and reranking. In our lab a plain header raised reranked recall@5 from 0.556 to 1.000. The costs: more tokens per chunk (index size, embedding time, prompt length) and, for the LLM version, one LLM call per chunk at indexing time.
    How should a RAG system handle questions it cannot answer, and how do you measure it?core
    Instruct the reader to abstain with a fixed phrase when the passages do not contain the answer, optionally add a retrieval-score threshold, and include unanswerable questions in the test set, especially near misses (a product that does not exist, a city that is not in the policy). Measure two rates: abstention on unanswerable questions (our runs: 4/6, 5/6, 5/6) and false refusals on answerable ones (3, 5 and 2 of 54). Both must be reported; tuning only one makes the other worse.
    You change the chunker and recall@1 improves from 0.907 to 0.981, but answer accuracy changes only from 50 to 52 of 60. Ship it?advanced
    The answer change is not significant (6 fixed, 4 broken, sign test \(p = 0.75\)), so do not claim it improves answers. But the retrieval gain is consistent, and it removed a known failure (facts cut across chunk boundaries). Read the 4 broken questions: if they are reader errors not caused by the new chunks (in our case the reader took a number from a sibling product’s chunk at rank 3), the chunker is not to blame. Decide with a larger or held-out test set, and look at cost (194 vs 159 chunks, shorter prompts: 394 vs 491 tokens).

    Key takeaways

    • A RAG test set needs questions in users’ words, a known answer and a known source for each, and unanswerable near-miss questions. Define relevance by position in the document (160.3) so it survives changes of the chunker.
    • Score retrieval per stage and per question type (recall@k, MRR), and answers separately (accuracy, abstention, faithfulness). Faithfulness is not correctness.
    • BM25 finds identifiers, dense retrieval finds paraphrases, RRF (160.5) combines them, and a cross-encoder fixes the order; in our lab fusion moved a gold chunk from rank 1 to 8 and the reranker moved it back.
    • Sort failures into categories. Our biggest one was chunks without context: 22 of 60 questions missed by retrieval. A header line (160.9) raised reranked recall@5 from 0.556 to 1.000 and accuracy from 28 to 50 of 60 (\(p = 0.0002\)).
    • Check graders against hand labels: our number rule and 1.5B LLM judge called almost every unfaithful answer faithful. Use paired tests (160.10) on small sets: our second fix (50 to 52 of 60) was not significant.
