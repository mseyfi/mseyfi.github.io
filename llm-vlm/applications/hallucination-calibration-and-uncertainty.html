    Hallucination, calibration and uncertainty

    A language model writes a wrong answer in the same calm, fluent voice as a right one. This session explains why that happens (the training objective, missing knowledge, grading that rewards guessing, and decoding), how to measure whether a model’s confidence can be trusted (calibration, ECE, temperature scaling), how to detect a likely hallucination (self-consistency, semantic entropy, retrieval checks), and how to design products that say “I don’t know” at the right moments.

    Time: 3 to 4 hours (reading 2 hours, exercises 1 to 2 hours)Level: IntermediateBefore this: Sessions 3, 79, 152 and 155

    What you will learn

    • What a hallucination is: factuality errors and faithfulness errors, with examples
    • Four causes: the next-token objective, facts seen only once, grading that rewards guessing, and decoding that compounds errors
    • Calibration: reliability diagrams, expected calibration error (ECE), Brier score, and temperature scaling fitted by NLL
    • Why the expected-score rule gives a clean abstention threshold t = k/(1+k) for a wrong-answer penalty k
    • Detection without labels: self-consistency, SelfCheckGPT, and semantic entropy (and why plain token entropy fails on paraphrases)
    • Detection with sources: retrieval checks, claim-level verification, Chain-of-Verification and FActScore
    • Product designs that reduce harm: grounding with citations, abstention, tools for exact facts, verification passes and honest UI

    Why this matters

    Ask a model for the birthday of a little-known scientist, and it may answer “14 March 1962” with full confidence. The date can be invented. The sentence looks exactly like a true one: same grammar, same calm tone. This is a hallucination: fluent, plausible text that is not supported by the facts or by the sources the model was given.

    Hallucinations are the main reason LLM products fail in law, medicine, finance and customer support. A lawyer who files a brief with invented case citations, a support bot that promises a refund policy that does not exist, a coding assistant that calls an API function that was never written: in each case the model did not “lie” on purpose. It did what it was trained to do, which is to produce likely text. Likely text and true text are not the same thing.

    The useful engineering question is not “how do we make hallucination zero?” (we cannot, see below) but three smaller questions. Why does the model produce a wrong answer instead of saying “I don’t know”? How confident is it, and can we trust that confidence? And how do we detect a likely error and design the product so that the error does less harm? This session answers all three, with numbers.

    Interview tipCommon questions: “Why do LLMs hallucinate?”, “What is calibration and how do you measure it?”, “How would you detect hallucinations without labels?”, “Design a QA system for a bank that must not invent facts.” Strong answers separate causes, measurement and product design, and give the expected-score argument for abstaining.

    In session 155 we learned how to measure a system. Here we measure one special property: whether the system knows when it is wrong. In session 157 we will look inside the model for the signals that carry this knowledge.

    What counts as a hallucination

    The survey of Huang et al. splits hallucinations into two families. [Certain]

    • Factuality hallucination: the output disagrees with facts about the world. Example: “The Eiffel Tower was finished in 1925.” (It was finished in 1889.) Sub-types: wrong facts, and invented facts (a paper, a person, a court case that does not exist).
    • Faithfulness hallucination: the output disagrees with the input: the user’s instruction, the document to summarize, or the retrieved passages. Example: a summary says “the company’s profit rose” when the article says it fell. The output may even be true in the world and still be unfaithful, because it was not in the source.

    The difference matters for detection. Factuality needs world knowledge or a search engine to check. Faithfulness can be checked against the given text alone, which is much easier to automate. In RAG systems (session 152) we mostly care about faithfulness: every claim must be supported by a retrieved passage.

    Vision-language models have their own version: object hallucination, describing objects that are not in the image (the POPE benchmark asks yes/no questions like “Is there a dog in the image?” to measure it). Video models add event hallucination: describing an action that never happens in the clip, or giving the wrong time for it.

    Why models hallucinate

    There is not one cause. There are four, and they stack.

    Cause 1: the training objective never says “unknown”

    Pretraining minimizes the negative log-likelihood of the next token (session 3):

    \[ \mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{\lt t}) \tag{156.1} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(x_t\)
    the token at position \(t\) in a training document
    \(x_{\lt t}\)
    all tokens before it (the context)
    \(p_\theta\)
    the model’s probability, with weights \(\theta\)

    This objective rewards putting probability on what the text says next, not on what is true. After “Marie Curie was born in”, the training text says “1867”, so the model learns to put mass on years. For a person the model saw only once, it still must put its probability somewhere, and it puts it on plausible years. Sampling from that distribution produces a plausible year. The model has no special token for “I have no memory of this”. The format of a biography (born in, a year, a city) is easy to learn; the specific fact is hard.

    AnalogyA student who memorized the shape of every history answer (“In the year ____, King ____ defeated ____ at ____”) can fill in any blank with names that sound right. The handwriting is perfect. Only a teacher with the real facts can see which blanks are wrong.

    Cause 2: some facts are seen only once

    Kalai and Vempala proved a striking result: a model that is calibrated (its probabilities match reality, defined below) must hallucinate on “arbitrary” facts, such as birthdays, that appear only once in training, even if the training data has no errors. [Certain] Kalai, Nachum, Vempala and Zhang (2025) state the practical version: the hallucination rate of a base model on such facts should be at least about the singleton rate, the fraction of those facts that appear exactly once in the training data. [Certain]

    \[ \text{hallucination rate on arbitrary facts} \ \gtrsim\ \text{sr} = \frac{\#\{\text{facts seen exactly once}\}}{\#\{\text{facts}\}} \tag{156.2} \]

    Their example: if 20% of birthday facts appear exactly once in the pretraining data, expect the base model to hallucinate on at least about 20% of birthday questions. [Certain] The intuition comes from an old statistics idea (Good–Turing): the fraction of things you saw once is a good estimate of how much you have not seen at all. A fact seen once gives the model no way to tell it apart from the many plausible facts it never saw.

    Fine-tuning can make this worse. Gekhman et al. found that when supervised fine-tuning teaches facts the model did not know from pretraining, the model learns them slowly, and once it does learn them, its tendency to hallucinate grows. [Certain] Training the model to answer questions it cannot answer teaches it to answer confidently anyway.

    Cause 3: grading rewards guessing

    Most benchmarks give 1 point for a correct answer and 0 for both a wrong answer and “I don’t know”. Under this rule, guessing is always at least as good as abstaining. Kalai et al. (2025) argue that this is the main reason post-trained models keep hallucinating: training and evaluation reward good test-takers, and good test-takers guess. [Certain]

    Let us write it down. Suppose the model believes its answer is correct with probability \(p\). A correct answer earns 1 point, a wrong answer costs \(k\) points, and “I don’t know” earns 0:

    \[ \E[\text{score} \mid \text{answer}] = p \cdot 1 - (1 - p)\, k \ \gt\ 0 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad p \gt t = \frac{k}{1 + k} \tag{156.3} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(p\)
    the probability that the answer is correct (the model’s confidence, if it is calibrated)
    \(k\)
    the penalty for a wrong answer; \(k = 0\) is the usual benchmark rule
    \(t\)
    the confidence threshold above which answering beats abstaining

    With \(k = 0\), the threshold is \(t = 0\): always answer. With \(k = 1\), answer only above 50%. With \(k = 2\), above 2/3. With \(k = 9\), above 90%. Kalai et al. propose putting this rule in the instructions (“answer only if you are more than \(t\) confident; mistakes cost \(t/(1-t)\) points”), so that the best behavior is clear and can be graded. [Certain] Note that \(t/(1-t) = k\): the two forms say the same thing.

    Worked example: answer or abstain?

    The model is 60% sure. Under the usual 0/1 rule, answering earns \(0.6\) on average and abstaining earns 0, so it answers. Now let a wrong answer cost \(k = 2\) (a wrong medical fact is worse than no fact). Answering earns \(0.6 - 0.4 \times 2 = -0.2\). Abstaining earns 0. So it should abstain. The threshold is \(t = 2/3 \approx 0.667\), and 0.6 is below it. Try it in Figure 1.

    Cause 4: decoding compounds small errors

    Generation picks one token at a time (session 79). Sampling with a high temperature or a large top-\(p\) sometimes picks a low-probability token, and once a wrong name or number is written, the model continues as if it were true: the next tokens are conditioned on it. Nothing in the loop goes back to fix it. Long answers multiply the risk. If each independent factual claim is wrong with probability \(e\), an answer with \(n\) claims contains at least one error with probability

    \[ P(\text{at least one error}) = 1 - (1 - e)^n \tag{156.4} \]

    With \(e = 2\%\) and \(n = 50\) claims (a long biography), that is \(1 - 0.98^{50} = 63.6\%\). Even with \(e = 1\%\) it is \(39.5\%\). This is why long-form factuality is measured per claim (FActScore splits text into atomic facts and checks each one) rather than per answer. [Certain]

    A fifth, softer cause: models copy human mistakes. TruthfulQA collected questions where many people hold a false belief; its authors reported that in their tests the largest models were often the least truthful, because they imitated the popular wrong answer better. [Likely] Figure 2 puts the causes and their main fixes side by side.

    Calibration: can we trust the confidence?

    A model is calibrated if, among all the answers it gives with confidence 0.8, about 80% are correct. Formally, for the predicted answer \(\hat{Y}\) and its confidence \(\hat{P}\):

    \[ P\big(\hat{Y} = Y \ \big|\ \hat{P} = p\big) = p \quad \text{for all } p \in [0, 1] \tag{156.5} \]

    Calibration is different from accuracy. A weather forecaster who says “30% rain” on days when it rains 30% of the time is perfectly calibrated, even though they never say “it will rain” with certainty. A model that is 55% accurate can be perfectly calibrated if its confidences average 55% in the right places. And a model can be very accurate and badly calibrated, if it says 99% when it is right 90% of the time.

    AnalogyA friend who says “I’m sure” about everything is useless as a guide, even if they are right often. A friend who says “I’m sure” only when they are, and “I think so” otherwise, is worth much more. Calibration is that honesty, measured.

    Measuring it: the reliability diagram and ECE

    Sort the predictions into \(M\) bins by confidence (for example 10 bins of width 0.1). For each bin \(B_m\), compare the average confidence \(\text{conf}(B_m)\) with the fraction correct \(\text{acc}(B_m)\). A plot of accuracy against confidence is a reliability diagram; a calibrated model lies on the diagonal. The expected calibration error summarizes the gaps, weighted by how many predictions fall in each bin:

    \[ \text{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{|B_m|}{N} \, \big| \text{acc}(B_m) - \text{conf}(B_m) \big| \tag{156.6} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(N\)
    the number of predictions
    \(B_m\)
    the predictions whose confidence falls in bin \(m\); \(|B_m|\) is how many
    \(\text{acc}(B_m)\)
    the fraction of those predictions that are correct
    \(\text{conf}(B_m)\)
    their average confidence
    Worked example: ECE with three bins

    100 answers. 40 have confidence about 0.95 and 70% of them are right. 30 have confidence 0.75 and 60% are right. 30 have confidence 0.55 and 50% are right. Then \(\text{ECE} = 0.4 \times |0.70 - 0.95| + 0.3 \times |0.60 - 0.75| + 0.3 \times |0.50 - 0.55| = 0.100 + 0.045 + 0.015 = 0.16\). Every bin is overconfident: the model says more than it knows.

    Figure 3 runs formula (156.6) in the browser on 20 real-looking predictions. First every prediction drops into the bin of its confidence. Then each bin computes its accuracy, its mean confidence and its share of the ECE, and the sum grows bin by bin. Change the number of bins to see that ECE is not one fixed property of a model: it also depends on how you bin.

    ECE has known weaknesses: it depends on the number of bins, and it only looks at the top answer. A proper scoring rule rewards honest probabilities without bins. The two common ones are the negative log-likelihood (NLL, the same cross-entropy as training) and the Brier score, the squared error between the probability vector and the one-hot truth:

    \[ \text{Brier} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{c=1}^{K} \big( p_{ic} - \mathbb{1}[y_i = c] \big)^2 \tag{156.7} \]

    Here \(p_{ic}\) is the probability the model gives to class \(c\) on example \(i\), \(K\) is the number of classes, and \(\mathbb{1}[y_i = c]\) is 1 for the true class and 0 otherwise. Report ECE because people understand it; tune and compare with NLL or Brier because they cannot be gamed by binning.

    Why modern networks are overconfident, and the one-number fix

    Guo et al. (2017) found that modern deep networks are much less calibrated than older, smaller ones: they are overconfident. [Certain] Training keeps pushing the logits of the correct class higher, long after the accuracy has stopped improving, so the softmax becomes too sharp. Their fix is surprisingly simple: temperature scaling. Divide all logits by one number \(T \gt 0\), learned on a held-out set by minimizing NLL:

    \[ \hat{p}_i = \softmax\!\left( \frac{\mathbf{z}_i}{T} \right), \qquad T^\ast = \argmin_{T \gt 0} \; -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log \hat{p}_{i, y_i} \tag{156.8} \]

    \(T \gt 1\) makes the distribution flatter (less confident); \(T \lt 1\) makes it sharper. Because dividing by a positive number does not change which logit is largest, accuracy does not change. Only the confidence does. Example: logits \((3, 1, 0, 0)\) give a top probability of 0.81 at \(T = 1\) and 0.55 at \(T = 2\). [Certain]

    How do we find \(T^\ast\)? It is a one-dimensional problem, so a grid search works. Gradient descent shows better what happens. To keep \(T\) positive, optimize \(u = \ln T\) instead. The derivative of the NLL in (156.8) with respect to \(u\) is short:

    \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial u} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{z_{i, y_i} - \sum_{c} \hat{p}_{ic}\, z_{ic}}{T}, \qquad u \leftarrow u - \eta\, \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial u}, \qquad T = e^{u} \tag{156.9} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(z_{i, y_i}\)
    the logit of the true answer of question \(i\)
    \(\sum_c \hat{p}_{ic} z_{ic}\)
    the average logit under the current scaled probabilities
    \(\eta\)
    the learning rate

    Read the sign. If the true answers have higher logits than the model’s average, the gradient is positive, so \(u\) goes down and \(T\) shrinks: the model may be sharper. If the true answers have lower logits than average (the model is often confidently wrong), the gradient is negative and \(T\) grows. At \(T^\ast\) the two balance. Figure 4 runs this loop on 1,000 questions and redraws the reliability diagram after every step.

    What about LLMs? Kadavath et al. found that large pretrained models are fairly well calibrated on multiple-choice and true/false questions when the question has the right format, and that a model can be asked to judge its own answer: P(True), the probability it gives to “this answer is true”. [Certain] The GPT-4 report showed that the pretrained model was well calibrated on MMLU and that post-training (RLHF) made calibration noticeably worse. [Likely] This matches cause 3: preference training rewards answers that sound confident. So a chat model’s token probabilities are often overconfident, and you must recalibrate them before you use them as thresholds.

    Uncertainty for free-form answers

    Temperature scaling works when the answer is one of a few classes. A chat answer is a sentence, and the same meaning can be written in many ways. How do we get one confidence number for it?

    Token probabilities and sequence entropy

    The simplest score is the probability of the generated answer, \(\log p(\mathbf{y} \mid x) = \sum_j \log p(y_j \mid y_{\lt j}, x)\), usually divided by the length so long answers are not punished. A related score is the entropy of the answer distribution, estimated from \(S\) samples \(\mathbf{y}^{(1)}, \dots, \mathbf{y}^{(S)}\):

    \[ H(Y \mid x) \approx -\frac{1}{S} \sum_{s=1}^{S} \frac{1}{|\mathbf{y}^{(s)}|} \log p\big(\mathbf{y}^{(s)} \mid x\big) \tag{156.10} \]

    The problem: these scores measure uncertainty about the words, not about the meaning. “Jane Austen.”, “It was written by Jane Austen.” and “The author is Jane Austen.” are three different strings. A model that is completely sure of the fact can still spread its probability over many phrasings, so the entropy looks high. This is what Kuhn, Gal and Farquhar call the problem of semantic equivalence. [Certain]

    Self-consistency and SelfCheckGPT

    A first fix: sample several answers and check whether they agree. If the model knows a fact, it tends to produce it again and again; if it is guessing, the samples disagree. This is the idea of self-consistency (used for reasoning by Wang et al., session 72) and of SelfCheckGPT, which samples extra responses and checks each sentence of the main answer against them, with no access to the model’s internals and no external database. [Certain] The simplest agreement score is the fraction of samples that share the most common meaning.

    Semantic entropy

    Kuhn et al. make this precise. Sample \(S\) answers. Group them into meaning clusters: two answers go in the same cluster if each one entails the other (an NLI model, session 145, or an LLM judge decides this; it is called bidirectional entailment). Then compute the entropy over clusters instead of over strings:

    \[ \text{SE}(x) = -\sum_{c} p(c \mid x) \log p(c \mid x), \qquad p(c \mid x) \approx \frac{n_c}{S} \tag{156.11} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(c\)
    one meaning cluster (for example “Canberra”)
    \(n_c\)
    how many of the \(S\) samples fell into cluster \(c\)
    \(p(c \mid x)\)
    the probability of meaning \(c\); the count version shown here is the “discrete” semantic entropy; one can also sum the sequence probabilities of the members

    Low semantic entropy: the model keeps saying the same thing, in any words. High semantic entropy: it keeps saying different things, which is a strong sign of a confabulation (an arbitrary, made-up answer). Farquhar et al. showed in Nature (2024) that semantic entropy detects such errors across several datasets and models better than token-level baselines. [Certain] It cannot catch an error the model makes consistently: if training taught a wrong fact, all samples agree on it, and semantic entropy is low.

    Worked example: string entropy versus semantic entropy

    Ten samples, all meaning “Jane Austen” but with 9 different wordings. Entropy over normalized strings: \(-\big(0.2 \ln 0.2 + 8 \times 0.1 \ln 0.1\big) = 2.164\) nats. Semantic entropy: one cluster, \(\text{SE} = 0\). Now ten samples for a question the model cannot know, giving 8 different years: \(\text{SE} = -(2 \times 0.2 \ln 0.2 + 6 \times 0.1 \ln 0.1) = 2.025\) nats. String entropy cannot tell the two cases apart (2.164 in both!); semantic entropy can. A split of 8 versus 2 gives \(-(0.8 \ln 0.8 + 0.2 \ln 0.2) = 0.500\) nats. For comparison, 10 equally likely meanings would give \(\ln 10 = 2.303\).

    Figure 5 runs this procedure step by step. Samples arrive one at a time. Each new sample is compared with the representative (the first member) of every existing cluster; if both directions of entailment hold, it joins that cluster, otherwise it starts a new one. The entropies are recomputed after every sample, so you can watch the estimate settle.

    Algorithm 1: flag a likely confabulation with semantic entropy
    1. input: question \(x\), model, number of samples \(S\), entailment checker, threshold \(\tau\)
    2. \(\mathbf{y}^{(0)} \leftarrow\) the main answer (greedy or low temperature)
    3. for \(s = 1 \dots S\): \(\mathbf{y}^{(s)} \leftarrow\) sample at temperature about 1 // diversity is needed
    4. clusters \(\leftarrow\) [ ]
    5. for each sample \(\mathbf{y}\): put it in the first cluster whose representative \(\mathbf{r}\) satisfies entails(\(\mathbf{y}, \mathbf{r}\)) and entails(\(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{y}\)); else start a new cluster
    6. \(p_c \leftarrow n_c / S\) for each cluster; \(\text{SE} \leftarrow -\sum_c p_c \log p_c\) // formula (156.11)
    7. if \(\text{SE} \gt \tau\): return “not sure” (abstain, retrieve, or ask a human)
    8. else: return \(\mathbf{y}^{(0)}\) with agreement \(\max_c p_c\)

    Other signals: verbal confidence, P(True) and probes

    • Verbal confidence: ask the model “how confident are you, 0 to 100%?”. Cheap and black-box, but the numbers tend to cluster on round values and are often overconfident after RLHF. [Likely] Calibrate them like any score.
    • P(True) and P(IK): Kadavath et al. ask the model whether a proposed answer is true and read the probability of “True”; it works better when the model first sees several of its own samples. They also train a head that predicts “I know the answer” (P(IK)) from the question alone. [Certain]
    • Probes on hidden states: a small linear classifier trained on the model’s internal activations can often predict whether an answer will be correct, which suggests the model “knows more than it says”. This is a topic for session 157. [Likely]

    Checking against sources

    Consistency checks catch answers the model is unsure about. They cannot catch confident errors. For those you need an outside reference: retrieved documents, a database, a tool. This is the strongest detection, and it is how production systems do it.

    • Retrieval check (faithfulness): split the answer into claims; for each claim, ask an NLI model or an LLM judge “is this claim supported by these passages?”. Unsupported claims are removed or marked. This is the groundedness metric of RAG evaluation (session 153).
    • Claim-level fact checking: FActScore splits a long answer into atomic facts and checks each against a knowledge source (Wikipedia in the paper); the score is the fraction supported. [Certain]
    • Chain-of-Verification (CoVe): the model (1) drafts an answer, (2) writes verification questions about its own claims, (3) answers them independently, without seeing the draft, so it does not copy its own error, and (4) writes a corrected final answer. [Certain]
    • Citations: make the model cite a passage id for each sentence, then check automatically that the cited passage exists and supports the sentence. A citation that points nowhere is itself a hallucination.
    Algorithm 2: a grounded answer with abstention
    1. input: question \(x\), retriever, generator, support checker, confidence threshold \(t\)
    2. passages \(\leftarrow\) retrieve\((x)\); if the best retrieval score is low: return “I could not find this in the documents”
    3. draft \(\leftarrow\) generate\((x\), passages, “answer only from the passages, cite [id] after each sentence, say not found if they do not contain the answer”)
    4. for each sentence \(s\) in draft: supported\((s) \leftarrow\) check\((s\), passage[cited id]\()\) // NLI or LLM judge
    5. drop sentences that are not supported; if nothing is left: return “not found”
    6. \(p \leftarrow\) calibrated confidence (for example agreement over a few samples, recalibrated on labeled data)
    7. if \(p \le t\): return “not sure” + the passages for the user to read // formula (156.3) with t = k/(1+k)
    8. return the supported sentences with their citations

    Abstaining: selective prediction

    Once we have a calibrated confidence, we can let the model answer only when the confidence is above a threshold \(t\). This is selective prediction. Two numbers describe it:

    \[ \text{coverage}(t) = \frac{\#\{i : \hat{p}_i \gt t\}}{N}, \qquad \text{selective risk}(t) = \frac{\#\{i : \hat{p}_i \gt t,\ \hat{y}_i \ne y_i\}}{\#\{i : \hat{p}_i \gt t\}} \tag{156.12} \]

    Coverage is the fraction of questions the model answers. Selective risk is the error rate among the answered ones. Raising \(t\) lowers both: fewer answers, but fewer of them wrong. The best \(t\) depends on the cost of a wrong answer, through formula (156.3). If the confidences are calibrated, the theory says \(t = k/(1 + k)\) maximizes the score; Figure 6 lets you check this on the recalibrated data from Figure 4.

    Product designs that reduce hallucination

    Detection is half the job. The other half is designing the product so that hallucinations are rare and cheap when they happen.

    DesignWhat it doesCost or limit

    Allow and reward “I don’t know”Instruction plus examples of abstention in SFT data; grade with a penalty \(k\) so guessing does not winToo many abstentions annoy users; tune \(t\) per use case
    Use tools for exact factsDates, prices, math, account data come from a calculator, database or API, not from the weights (session 154)Tool errors and wrong tool arguments are new failure modes
    Constrain the outputPick from a fixed list (product ids, ICD codes) with structured output; the model cannot invent an idOnly works when the answer space is known

    Putting it together in code

    The PyTorch code below builds an overconfident four-choice “model”, measures ECE, fits a temperature by NLL with LBFGS, computes semantic entropy from cluster ids, and applies the abstention rule. It is deterministic; the printed values are shown in the comments.

    import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F
torch.manual_seed(0)

# 1) A toy 4-choice quiz. The "true" logits give the real chance of each answer.
#    The model reports logits 2.5 times too large: it is overconfident.
N, K = 5000, 4
z_true = 1.2 * torch.randn(N, K)
labels = torch.multinomial(F.softmax(z_true, -1), 1).squeeze(1)
logits = 2.5 * z_true

def ece(probs, labels, bins=10):                   # formula (156.6)
    conf, pred = probs.max(-1)
    correct = (pred == labels).float()
    idx = torch.clamp((conf * bins).long(), max=bins - 1)
    total = 0.0
    for b in range(bins):
        m = idx == b
        if m.any():
            total += m.float().mean() * (conf[m].mean() - correct[m].mean()).abs()
    return float(total)

# 2) Temperature scaling, formula (156.8): one number T, fitted on held-out data.
def fit_temperature(logits, labels):
    log_t = torch.zeros(1, requires_grad=True)       # T = exp(log_t) stays positive
    opt = torch.optim.LBFGS([log_t], lr=0.1, max_iter=200)
    def closure():
        opt.zero_grad()
        loss = F.cross_entropy(logits / log_t.exp(), labels)
        loss.backward()
        return loss
    opt.step(closure)
    return float(log_t.detach().exp())

val, test = slice(0, 2500), slice(2500, N)
T = fit_temperature(logits[val], labels[val])
p1 = F.softmax(logits[test], -1)
pT = F.softmax(logits[test] / T, -1)
print(f"T = {T:.2f}")                                                  # T = 2.62
print(f"accuracy {(p1.argmax(-1) == labels[test]).float().mean():.3f}") # 0.551, same for any T
print(f"ECE before {ece(p1, labels[test]):.3f} after {ece(pT, labels[test]):.3f}")  # 0.228 -> 0.023

# 3) Semantic entropy, formula (156.11), from cluster ids of S sampled answers.
def semantic_entropy(cluster_ids):
    counts = torch.bincount(torch.tensor(cluster_ids)).float()
    p = counts[counts > 0] / counts.sum()
    return float(-(p * p.log()).sum()) + 0.0
print(f"{semantic_entropy([0]*10):.3f}")                    # 0.000  paraphrases
print(f"{semantic_entropy([0]*8 + [1]*2):.3f}")             # 0.500  8 vs 2
print(f"{semantic_entropy([0,1,0,2,3,4,1,5,6,7]):.3f}")     # 2.025  scattered

# 4) Abstain unless confident: t = k / (1 + k), formula (156.3).
k = 2.0
t = k / (1 + k)
conf, pred = pT.max(-1)
ok = (pred == labels[test]).float()
answer = conf > t
score = (ok * answer).sum() - k * ((1 - ok) * answer).sum()
print(f"coverage {answer.float().mean():.3f}  accuracy when answering {ok[answer].mean():.3f}")  # 0.233, 0.794
print(f"score {score / len(ok):.3f}  answer-all {(ok.sum() - k * (1 - ok).sum()) / len(ok):.3f}")  # 0.089 vs -0.346

    Note that the fitted \(T\) here is 2.62, not exactly 2.5: it is estimated from 2,500 noisy labels. In a real LLM system, the “logits” would be the model’s scores for the answer options (or a confidence feature such as agreement), and the labels would come from a small human-checked set.

    Common mistakes

    • Treating raw token probabilities of a chat model as calibrated. Post-training tends to make them overconfident. Measure a reliability diagram on labeled data and recalibrate first.
    • Fitting the temperature on the test set. \(T\) must be fitted on a held-out validation set, then evaluated on a separate test set, or the ECE you report is too optimistic.
    • Using token entropy for free-form answers. Paraphrases make it high even when the model is sure. Cluster by meaning (semantic entropy) or check agreement.
    • Believing consistency means truth. A model can repeat the same wrong fact in every sample. Consistency checks catch guessing, not confident errors; use sources for those.
    • Grading with 0/1 accuracy only. This rewards guessing and hides the abstention behavior you want. Report accuracy, abstention rate and a penalized score.
    • Fine-tuning new facts into the model to stop hallucination. It can increase hallucination; put facts in retrieval.
    • Using one global threshold for every use case. The right \(t\) comes from the cost of a wrong answer, which differs between a chit-chat bot and a dosage calculator.
    • Reporting ECE with very few examples per bin. With 10 bins and 200 examples, bin accuracies are noisy. Report the number of examples and a proper score (NLL or Brier) too.

    Interview questions

    Why do LLMs hallucinate? Give at least three causes.basic
    (1) The pretraining objective rewards likely text, not true text, and has no way to express “I don’t know”; the model fills the shape of an answer with plausible content. (2) Facts seen only once in training cannot be told apart from plausible facts never seen; for a calibrated model, the hallucination rate on such facts is at least about the singleton rate. (3) Post-training and benchmarks grade with 0/1, which rewards guessing over abstaining. (4) Decoding samples token by token and never goes back, so one wrong token is continued, and long answers compound per-claim errors: \(1 - (1-e)^n\). Faithfulness errors add another cause: the model prefers its own prior knowledge over the given context.
    What is the difference between a factuality and a faithfulness hallucination?basic
    Factuality: the output contradicts facts about the world (wrong or invented facts). Faithfulness: the output contradicts or goes beyond the input, such as the instructions, the document to summarize or the retrieved passages. A faithfulness error can be checked using only the given text (NLI or an LLM judge), which is why RAG systems measure groundedness. Factuality needs an external source.
    Define calibration and ECE. Can a model be accurate but badly calibrated?core
    Calibrated means \(P(\text{correct} \mid \text{confidence} = p) = p\). ECE bins predictions by confidence and averages \(|\text{acc} - \text{conf}|\) over bins, weighted by bin size. Yes: a model with 90% accuracy that always reports 99.9% confidence is accurate but overconfident, and its ECE is about 0.1. Conversely, a 55%-accurate model can be well calibrated. Accuracy is about the argmax; calibration is about the probability values.
    How does temperature scaling work, and why does it not change accuracy?core
    Divide all logits by a single \(T \gt 0\) fitted on a held-out set by minimizing NLL. \(T \gt 1\) flattens the softmax and lowers overconfidence. Dividing by a positive constant preserves the order of the logits, so the argmax, and therefore accuracy, is unchanged. It is a one-parameter version of Platt scaling, and Guo et al. found it surprisingly effective for modern overconfident networks.
    Derive the confidence threshold for abstaining when a wrong answer costs k points.core
    Answering gives expected score \(p - (1-p)k\); abstaining gives 0. Answer iff \(p - k + pk \gt 0\), that is \(p(1 + k) \gt k\), so \(p \gt k/(1+k)\). With \(k = 0\) (standard benchmarks) the threshold is 0, so guessing is always optimal. With \(k = 1\), 0.5; \(k = 2\), 2/3; \(k = 9\), 0.9. This requires the confidence to be calibrated; otherwise the threshold is applied to the wrong numbers.
    Why does plain sequence entropy fail as an uncertainty measure for free-form answers, and what does semantic entropy do instead?core
    Different wordings of the same meaning are different sequences, so a model that is certain of the fact but varies its phrasing has high sequence entropy. Semantic entropy samples several answers, clusters them by bidirectional entailment (A entails B and B entails A), and computes entropy over clusters: \(-\sum_c p(c)\log p(c)\). Ten paraphrases of “Jane Austen” give SE = 0; eight different years give SE about 2.0 nats. It costs \(S\) extra generations plus the entailment checks.
    Which hallucinations can sampling-based detection (self-consistency, SelfCheckGPT, semantic entropy) not catch?advanced
    Consistent errors: if the model has learned a wrong fact, or a popular misconception (the TruthfulQA kind), all samples agree, agreement is high and entropy is low. These methods detect arbitrary guessing (confabulation), not systematic error. They also miss errors that sampling at the chosen temperature never varies. To catch consistent errors you need an external reference: retrieval, a database, a tool, or human review.
    Design a question-answering assistant for a bank that must not invent facts.advanced
    Retrieval over approved documents only, with a minimum retrieval score; prompt to answer only from passages with a citation per sentence, and to say “not found”. Account data and numbers come from tools (APIs), never from the weights. Post-check each sentence for support against its cited passage (NLI or a judge model) and drop unsupported ones. Compute a calibrated confidence (agreement over a few samples, recalibrated on a labeled set) and abstain or hand off to a human below \(t = k/(1+k)\) with a high \(k\). Low temperature. UI shows sources. Evaluate offline on a labeled set with groundedness, abstention rate and penalized score; monitor the same metrics in production.
    What did Kadavath et al. find about whether LLMs know what they know?advanced
    Large pretrained models are well calibrated on multiple-choice and true/false questions in a suitable format. They can evaluate their own answers: P(True), the probability that a proposed answer is true, works reasonably and improves when the model sees several of its own samples first. A model can also be trained to predict P(IK), “I know the answer”, from the question alone; it partly transfers to new tasks and rises when relevant source material is in the context.
    Why can RLHF make calibration worse, and what can you do about it?advanced
    Preference data and reward models tend to favor answers that sound confident and complete; the policy learns to put more mass on one answer, so its token probabilities drift away from frequencies of being correct. The GPT-4 report shows a well-calibrated pretrained model and a worse-calibrated post-trained one on MMLU. Fixes: recalibrate on labeled data (temperature or isotonic regression), use sampling-based confidence instead of raw probabilities, include abstention examples in SFT, and use reward or grading schemes that penalize confident errors more than “I don’t know”.
    Why is ECE alone not enough to compare two calibration methods?core
    ECE depends on the number and placement of bins, is noisy with few examples per bin, only looks at the top-class confidence, and can be small for a useless model (always predict the base rate with the base-rate confidence). Proper scoring rules such as NLL and the Brier score reward honest full probability vectors without binning. Report ECE with the bin count and sample size, plus NLL or Brier, and look at the reliability diagram.

    Key takeaways

    • Hallucinations are fluent text not supported by facts (factuality) or by the given input (faithfulness). They come from the next-token objective (156.1), facts seen once (156.2), grading that rewards guessing (156.3), and decoding that compounds errors (156.4).
    • With a wrong-answer penalty \(k\), answer only when the calibrated confidence is above \(t = k/(1+k)\); standard 0/1 grading (\(k = 0\)) always rewards guessing.
    • Calibration (156.5) is measured with reliability diagrams, ECE (156.6) and proper scores such as Brier (156.7). Temperature scaling (156.8) fixes overconfidence with one number and does not change accuracy.
    • For free-form answers, token entropy (156.10) is fooled by paraphrases; semantic entropy (156.11) clusters samples by meaning and detects confabulations, but not consistent errors.
    • Confident errors need outside checks: retrieval support, claim-level fact checks, CoVe, tools. Products combine grounding, abstention (156.12), tools, verification and honest UI.

    Key formulas

    \[ p \gt \frac{k}{1+k}, \qquad \text{ECE} = \sum_m \frac{|B_m|}{N}\big|\text{acc}(B_m) - \text{conf}(B_m)\big|, \qquad \hat{p} = \softmax\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{z}}{T}\right), \qquad \text{SE} = -\sum_c p(c \mid x)\log p(c \mid x) \]
