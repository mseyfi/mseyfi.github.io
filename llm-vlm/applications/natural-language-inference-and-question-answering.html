    Natural language inference and question answering

    Two classic language-understanding tasks that are still used every day. Natural language inference (NLI) asks whether one sentence follows from another, and an NLI model can label text with classes it never saw in training. Question answering (QA) finds the answer to a question in a passage, or first searches a large collection for the right passage. This session builds both from the model head to the metrics, with the exact rules of the SQuAD scoring script.

    Time: 2.5 to 3.5 hours (reading 2 hours, exercises 1 to 1.5 hours)Level: Beginner to interview levelBefore this: Sessions 33 (BERT and the CLS token), 55 (fine-tuning for classic NLP tasks) and 144 (text classification)

    What you will learn

    • The three NLI labels (entailment, neutral, contradiction), the SNLI and MultiNLI datasets, and why a model that reads only the hypothesis already scores far above chance

    • How extractive QA predicts a start and an end position, how it is trained, and how the best legal span is decoded

    • Exact match and token F1: the normalization rules, the max over gold answers, and why both are unfair to chatty LLM answers

    Why this matters

    Two questions sit under a large part of language understanding. Does this sentence follow from that one? And where, in this text, is the answer to my question? The first is natural language inference (NLI). The second is question answering (QA).

    Both tasks are older than chat LLMs, but they did not go away. They became parts of bigger systems:

    • A small NLI model is a cheap zero-shot classifier: it can tag support tickets or news with labels it never saw in training, with no labeled data and no LLM call.
    • NLI is a common way to check whether a summary or a RAG answer is supported by its sources: “does the source entail this sentence?” (session 156).
    • Extractive QA (point to the answer inside a passage) gives answers that are exact quotes, with their location. That matters in legal, medical and enterprise search, where a made-up answer is not acceptable.
    • Open-domain QA, “retrieve a few passages, then read them”, is the direct ancestor of RAG (session 152).
    • Exact match and token F1 are still the default metrics for short-answer QA, including for LLMs. You must know what they reward and what they punish.
    Interview tipCommon questions: “How would you classify text into new labels without training data?”, “How does BERT do SQuAD?”, “Why not take the argmax of start and end separately?”, “What do EM and F1 measure?”, “How do you handle a passage longer than 512 tokens?”. Each one is answered below with numbers.

    Natural language inference: the task

    NLI gets two sentences. The premise \(P\) is taken as true. The hypothesis \(H\) is a new claim. The model must say which of three relations holds:

    • Entailment: if \(P\) is true, \(H\) must also be true. “A man is playing a guitar on stage.” entails “A man is playing an instrument.”
    • Contradiction: if \(P\) is true, \(H\) cannot be true. Same premise, “The man is asleep in bed.”
    • Neutral: \(P\) does not decide \(H\). Same premise, “The man is a famous musician.” Maybe; the premise does not say.

    “Must be true” here means what a normal reader would conclude, not strict logic. A careful reader would accept “instrument” from “guitar” even though no formal rule is written down. This is why NLI is also called recognizing textual entailment (RTE), and why it tests real language understanding: word meaning, negation, numbers, world knowledge.

    AnalogyA judge reads a witness statement (the premise) and then a claim (the hypothesis). The statement can prove the claim, disprove it, or say nothing about it. The judge must not use what he guesses happened; only what the statement supports.

    The datasets: SNLI and MultiNLI

    DatasetSizeWhere the premises come fromNote
    SNLI (Bowman et al., 2015)570K pairs [Certain]Image captions (Flickr30k)Hypotheses written by crowd workers, who saw only the caption, not the image [Likely]
    MultiNLI (Williams et al., 2017)433K pairs [Certain]Ten genres of written and spoken English (fiction, government reports, telephone speech, ...) [Certain]Two test conditions: matched (same genres as training) and mismatched (unseen genres)

    For each premise, a crowd worker wrote three hypotheses: one true, one maybe true, one false. This made data collection fast and cheap. It also left traces, which we look at below.

    MultiNLI is part of the GLUE benchmark. BERT-large reached 86.7% accuracy on it [Certain]. Today MultiNLI-trained models, such as facebook/bart-large-mnli and many DeBERTa models, are the standard off-the-shelf NLI models.

    The model: a cross-encoder with a 3-way head

    The standard NLI model reads both sentences together as one input: [CLS] premise [SEP] hypothesis [SEP]. Every premise token can attend to every hypothesis token in every layer. This is called a cross-encoder. The final vector at the [CLS] position, \(\mathbf{h}_{\text{CLS}}\), goes to a linear layer with 3 outputs (session 33):

    \[ p(y \mid P, H) = \softmax\big(W\,\mathbf{h}_{\text{CLS}} + \mathbf{b}\big)_y, \qquad y \in \{\text{C}, \text{N}, \text{E}\} \tag{145.1} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\mathbf{h}_{\text{CLS}} \in \R^{d}\)
    the final hidden state at the first position; it has seen both sentences
    \(W \in \R^{3 \times d}, \mathbf{b} \in \R^{3}\)
    the new classification head, trained with cross-entropy on the gold label
    C, N, E
    contradiction, neutral, entailment. The order of these three outputs differs between models: always read it from the model’s config.json
    Worked example: from logits to a label

    The head outputs logits \((z_C, z_N, z_E) = (-1.2,\ 0.4,\ 2.6)\). The softmax gives \(e^{-1.2} : e^{0.4} : e^{2.6}\), normalized: \(p = (0.020,\ 0.098,\ 0.882)\). The prediction is entailment with probability 0.882.

    A decoder LLM can do NLI with a prompt (“Premise: ... Hypothesis: ... Answer with entailment, neutral or contradiction.”), and you can score it by comparing the probabilities of the three label words, as in session 144. A fine-tuned cross-encoder of a few hundred million parameters is still much cheaper per pair, which matters when you must check thousands of pairs.

    Annotation artifacts: the hypothesis gives the answer away

    Because workers wrote hypotheses quickly, each label got its own style. Contradictions often contain negation words (“nobody”, “never”, “sleeping”). Neutral hypotheses often add purpose or detail (“for a competition”). Entailments often use general words (“a person”, “outdoors”) [Likely]. Gururangan et al. trained a classifier that sees only the hypothesis, never the premise. It scored about 67% on SNLI and 53% on MultiNLI, where chance is 33% [Certain]. A model can therefore look strong without doing inference at all.

    Watch outAlways run a hypothesis-only baseline on a new NLI-style dataset. If it is far above chance, your benchmark rewards shortcuts. The same lesson holds for VQA, where “answer-only” or “question-only” baselines often score well.

    NLI as a zero-shot classifier

    Here is the useful trick (Yin, Hay and Roth, 2019). To decide whether a text belongs to a class, turn the class name into a hypothesis and ask the NLI model whether the text entails it:

    • premise: “The camera is great, but at $1,200 the phone costs too much.”
    • hypothesis for label “price”: “This example is about price.”

    The NLI model has never seen your labels, but it understands sentences, so it can judge them. You run one premise-hypothesis pair per candidate label. The Hugging Face zero-shot-classification pipeline uses the template "This example is {}." by default [Certain] and offers two scoring rules. Let \(z_E^{(k)}\) and \(z_C^{(k)}\) be the entailment and contradiction logits for label \(k\) of \(K\).

    Single-label (exactly one label is right): softmax of the entailment logits across labels.

    \[ p(k \mid x) = \frac{\exp z_E^{(k)}}{\sum_{k'=1}^{K} \exp z_E^{(k')}} \tag{145.2} \]

    Multi-label (each label is a separate yes/no question): for each label alone, a softmax over its contradiction and entailment logits; neutral is dropped.

    \[ p_k = \frac{\exp z_E^{(k)}}{\exp z_E^{(k)} + \exp z_C^{(k)}} = \sigma\big(z_E^{(k)} - z_C^{(k)}\big) \tag{145.3} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(z_E^{(k)}, z_C^{(k)}\)
    the entailment and contradiction logits of the pair (text, hypothesis built from label \(k\))
    \(\sigma\)
    the sigmoid \(1/(1+e^{-t})\); a 2-way softmax is a sigmoid of the difference
    \(K\)
    the number of candidate labels; cost grows linearly with \(K\), one forward pass per label

    The two rules answer different questions. Single-label scores always sum to 1, so the model is forced to pick something, even for a text that fits no label. Multi-label scores are independent, so a text can fit two labels, or none. Try it in Figure 1. The logits there are illustrative numbers that we chose, not outputs of a real model.

    Worked example: Text B with both rules

    Entailment logits for (battery, price, camera, screen) are \((-1.8,\ 2.6,\ 2.4,\ -0.6)\). Single-label (145.2): softmax gives \((0.007,\ 0.534,\ 0.437,\ 0.022)\). Multi-label (145.3) for price: \(z_E - z_C = 2.6 - (-1.5) = 4.1\), and \(\sigma(4.1) = 0.984\). For camera: \(\sigma(2.4 + 1.3) = \sigma(3.7) = 0.976\). For battery: \(\sigma(-1.8 - 2.1) = 0.020\).

    Algorithm 1: zero-shot classification with an NLI model
    1. input: text \(x\), candidate labels \(\ell_1, \dots, \ell_K\), template “This example is about {}.”, mode, threshold \(t\)
    2. for \(k = 1\) to \(K\): \(H_k \leftarrow\) template filled with \(\ell_k\)
    3. \(\quad (z_C^{(k)}, z_N^{(k)}, z_E^{(k)}) \leftarrow \text{NLI}(\text{premise} = x,\ \text{hypothesis} = H_k)\) // batch all K pairs in one call
    4. if mode is single-label: \(p \leftarrow \softmax_k(z_E^{(k)})\); return \(\argmax_k p_k\) // formula (145.2)
    5. else: \(p_k \leftarrow \sigma(z_E^{(k)} - z_C^{(k)})\) for each \(k\); return \(\{\ell_k : p_k \gt t\}\) // formula (145.3)

    Practical points. The label words and the template matter: “This example is about price.” and “This review complains about the price.” can give quite different scores [Likely]. Tune them on 50 to 100 labeled examples if you have them. The cost is \(K\) forward passes per text, so 50 labels on a million texts is 50 million passes; for many labels, an embedding model that compares the text with label descriptions is cheaper (session 150). And the threshold \(t\) for multi-label needs calibration, as in session 144.

    Extractive QA: point to the answer

    In extractive QA the input is a question \(q\) and a passage \(c\) (the context), and the answer must be a span: a run of consecutive tokens copied from the passage. SQuAD (Rajpurkar et al., 2016) made this task popular: more than 100,000 questions written by crowd workers on Wikipedia articles, where each answer is a segment of the passage [Certain]. In the paper, a logistic regression model reached 51.0% F1 and humans 86.8% F1 [Certain]. Two years later BERT reached 93.2 F1 on the SQuAD 1.1 test set [Certain].

    AnalogyA student with a highlighter. She does not write an answer in her own words; she marks where it begins and where it ends in the text. Two decisions, one at each edge of the highlight.

    The model: one start vector, one end vector

    Feed [CLS] question [SEP] passage [SEP] to an encoder. Let \(\mathbf{h}_i \in \R^d\) be the final hidden state of token \(i\). BERT adds only two vectors, a start vector \(\mathbf{s}\) and an end vector \(\mathbf{e}\) (in code, one linear layer with 2 outputs). Each token gets a start logit and an end logit, and a softmax over all positions gives two distributions:

    \[ p_{\text{start}}(i) = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{s}^\top \mathbf{h}_i)}{\sum_{k} \exp(\mathbf{s}^\top \mathbf{h}_k)}, \qquad p_{\text{end}}(j) = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{e}^\top \mathbf{h}_j)}{\sum_{k} \exp(\mathbf{e}^\top \mathbf{h}_k)} \tag{145.4} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\mathbf{s}, \mathbf{e} \in \R^{d}\)
    learned start and end vectors (the only new weights: \(2d + 2\) numbers with biases)
    \(\mathbf{s}^\top \mathbf{h}_i\)
    the start logit of token \(i\): how much token \(i\) looks like the first token of the answer
    \(k\)
    runs over all positions of the input, including [CLS], which SQuAD 2.0 uses for “no answer”

    Training uses the gold start \(i^*\) and gold end \(j^*\): the loss is the average of two cross-entropies.

    \[ \mathcal{L} = -\tfrac{1}{2}\Big(\log p_{\text{start}}(i^*) + \log p_{\text{end}}(j^*)\Big) \tag{145.5} \]

    The gold positions come from the dataset as a character offset in the passage. You must map it to token positions after tokenization, using the tokenizer’s offset mapping. Off-by-one errors here silently cap your score. BERT’s SQuAD recipe fine-tuned for 3 epochs with learning rate \(5 \times 10^{-5}\) and batch size 32 [Certain].

    Decoding: the best legal span

    At test time we want the span \((i, j)\) with the highest score. A span is legal only if it starts before it ends, it lies inside the passage (not in the question), and it is not too long. The score of a span is the sum of its two logits, which is the log of \(p_{\text{start}}(i)\,p_{\text{end}}(j)\) up to a constant:

    \[ (\hat\imath, \hat\jmath) = \argmax_{\substack{i \le j \lt i + L_{\max} \\ i, j \in \text{passage}}} \; \mathbf{s}^\top \mathbf{h}_i + \mathbf{e}^\top \mathbf{h}_j \tag{145.6} \]

    \(L_{\max}\) is the maximum answer length in tokens; BERT’s code uses 30 [Certain]. Why not take the argmax of start and the argmax of end separately? Because the two are predicted independently, and the best end can lie before the best start. Figure 2 shows exactly this case.

    Worked example: the numbers in Figure 2

    With a softmax over the [CLS] slot and the 12 passage tokens, \(p_{\text{start}}(\text{1889}) = 0.658\) and \(p_{\text{start}}(\text{1887}) = 0.242\), while \(p_{\text{end}}(\text{1887}) = 0.558\) and \(p_{\text{end}}(\text{1889}) = 0.413\). The top legal spans are (1889, 1889) with \(4.0 + 3.9 = 7.9\), (1887, 1887) with \(3.0 + 4.2 = 7.2\) and (1887, 1889) with \(3.0 + 3.9 = 6.9\). A passage of 12 tokens has \(12 \cdot 13 / 2 = 78\) spans with \(i \le j\); with \(L_{\max} = 3\) only 33 remain.

    Unanswerable questions and long passages

    SQuAD 2.0: knowing when to say nothing

    A SQuAD 1.1 model always answers, because every training question has an answer. In real search, often no passage contains one. SQuAD 2.0 (Rajpurkar, Jia and Liang, 2018) added over 50,000 unanswerable questions, written by crowd workers to look like answerable ones [Certain]. In that paper, a strong system with 86% F1 on SQuAD 1.1 got only 66% F1 on SQuAD 2.0 [Certain].

    BERT handles this with the [CLS] token: the gold “span” of an unanswerable question is \((0, 0)\), the [CLS] position. At test time it compares the null score with the best real span:

    \[ \text{answer} = \begin{cases} \text{no answer} & \text{if } s_{\text{null}} - \max_{(i,j)\ \text{legal}} \big(\mathbf{s}^\top \mathbf{h}_i + \mathbf{e}^\top \mathbf{h}_j\big) \gt \tau \\ \text{best span} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}, \qquad s_{\text{null}} = \mathbf{s}^\top \mathbf{h}_{\text{CLS}} + \mathbf{e}^\top \mathbf{h}_{\text{CLS}} \tag{145.7} \]

    The threshold \(\tau\) is tuned on the dev set to maximize F1 (BERT’s code has null_score_diff_threshold, default 0.0) [Certain]. A higher \(\tau\) answers more often; a lower one abstains more often. This is the same precision-recall trade-off as any classifier threshold.

    Passages longer than the model: sliding windows

    An encoder has a maximum input length (512 tokens for BERT). BERT’s SQuAD code uses max_seq_length = 384 and splits a longer passage into overlapping windows [Certain]. Each window contains the full question plus a chunk of the passage. With \(M\) the maximum input length and \(|q|\) the question length in tokens, each window holds

    \[ m = M - |q| - 3, \qquad N_{\text{win}} = 1 + \left\lceil \frac{\max(0,\ n - m)}{r} \right\rceil \tag{145.8} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(m\)
    passage tokens per window; the 3 is for [CLS] and the two [SEP]
    \(n\)
    the passage length in tokens
    \(r\)
    the step between window starts (BERT’s doc_stride, default 128 [Certain])
    \(N_{\text{win}}\)
    the number of windows, each one a full forward pass

    Each window is scored on its own. The final answer is the best span over all windows. A token that appears in several windows is judged in the window where it has the most context on both sides (the “max context” rule of BERT’s code: the window that maximizes \(\min(\text{left}, \text{right}) + 0.01 \cdot \text{window length}\)). For SQuAD 2.0 the null score of the example is the smallest null score over its windows, because one window that contains the answer is enough [Certain].

    Watch out“Stride” means two different things. In BERT’s run_squad.py, doc_stride is the step between window starts. In the Hugging Face tokenizers, stride (with return_overflowing_tokens=True) is the number of overlapping tokens between windows [Likely]. The same number 128 gives different windows. Check which one your code uses.

    Open-domain QA: retrieve, then read

    So far someone gave us the right passage. In open-domain QA there is only the question, and the answer is somewhere in millions of documents, for example all of Wikipedia. Running a reader on every passage is impossible. Chen et al. (2017, DrQA) proposed the design that is still used: a fast retriever picks the top \(k\) passages, then a slower reader finds the answer in them. Their retriever was TF-IDF over bigram hashes, and their reader a recurrent network [Certain].

    Dense retrieval. DPR (Karpukhin et al., 2020) encodes the question and each passage separately with two BERT encoders and scores them by a dot product:

    \[ \text{sim}(q, c) = E_Q(q)^\top E_P(c) \tag{145.9} \]

    The passage vectors \(E_P(c)\) are computed once, offline, and stored in a vector index, so a query costs one encoder pass plus a nearest-neighbor search (session 151). DPR beat a strong BM25 system by 9 to 19 points of top-20 retrieval accuracy on several open-domain QA datasets [Certain]. Top-\(k\) retrieval accuracy is the fraction of questions where at least one of the \(k\) retrieved passages contains the answer. Note the contrast with NLI: the NLI model and the reader are cross-encoders (question and passage read together, accurate, slow), the retriever is a bi-encoder (encoded apart, fast, less accurate).

    The reader. An extractive reader scores spans in each passage with (145.6) and takes the best one over all \(k\) passages; a good system also adds a passage-relevance score so that a high span score in an irrelevant passage does not win. A generative reader writes the answer instead: Fusion-in-Decoder (Izacard and Grave, 2020) encodes each passage with the question separately and lets one T5 decoder attend to all of them [Certain]. RAG (Lewis et al., 2020) trains the retriever and a generator together. An LLM reading retrieved passages in its prompt is the same pattern (session 152).

    Algorithm 2: open-domain QA with a retriever and an extractive reader
    1. offline: split documents into passages (for example 100 words); compute \(E_P(c)\) for all; build an index
    2. input: question \(q\), number of passages \(k\), max answer length \(L_{\max}\)
    3. \(\mathbf{u} \leftarrow E_Q(q)\); \(\;C_k \leftarrow\) the \(k\) passages with the largest \(\mathbf{u}^\top E_P(c)\) // formula (145.9)
    4. for each \(c \in C_k\): split into windows (145.8); run the reader on [CLS] q [SEP] window [SEP]
    5. \(\quad\) best span in each window with (145.6); keep its score and the null score
    6. if every window’s null score beats its best span by more than \(\tau\): return “no answer” // formula (145.7)
    7. return the span with the highest score over all passages, with its passage id as the source

    The two stages multiply. If the answer is in the top \(k\) passages for 80% of the questions, and the reader is right 70% of the time when the answer is present, the system cannot do better than about 80%, and you should expect something near \(0.8 \times 0.7 = 0.56\) (if the two errors are independent). So always measure them separately: retrieval recall@\(k\) first, then reader accuracy on questions whose answer was retrieved. Larger \(k\) raises recall but gives the reader more distractors and costs more.

    Interview tipWhen an open-domain QA or RAG system gives wrong answers, the first question is: “Was the answer in the retrieved passages?” If not, no reader or LLM can fix it. Fix retrieval (chunking, hybrid BM25 plus dense, a cross-encoder re-ranker) before touching the reader.

    Metrics: exact match and token F1

    SQuAD defined the two metrics that most short-answer QA still uses. Both first normalize the strings, exactly as the official script does: lowercase, remove punctuation, remove the articles “a”, “an”, “the”, and collapse white space [Certain].

    Exact match (EM) is 1 if the normalized prediction equals a normalized gold answer, else 0. Token F1 treats the prediction and the gold answer as bags of words and measures their overlap:

    \[ \text{EM}(\hat a, a) = \mathbb{1}\big[\text{norm}(\hat a) = \text{norm}(a)\big] \tag{145.10} \]
    \[ P = \frac{|\hat a \cap a|}{|\hat a|}, \qquad R = \frac{|\hat a \cap a|}{|a|}, \qquad F_1 = \frac{2PR}{P + R} \tag{145.11} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\hat a, a\)
    the predicted answer and one gold answer, as lists of normalized tokens (split on spaces)
    \(|\hat a \cap a|\)
    the number of shared tokens, counting repeats (a multiset intersection); if it is 0, \(F_1 = 0\)
    \(P, R\)
    precision (what fraction of the prediction is right) and recall (what fraction of the gold answer is covered)

    Questions in the SQuAD dev and test sets have several gold answers from different annotators. The score of one question is the maximum over its gold answers, and the dataset score is the average over questions:

    \[ \text{score}(\hat a) = \max_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \; m(\hat a, a), \qquad m \in \{\text{EM}, F_1\} \tag{145.12} \]

    F1 is always at least as high as EM, because a right answer with one extra word still gets partial credit. Type any answer in Figure 5 to see both.

    Worked example: F1 by hand

    Prediction “the Eiffel Tower in Paris”, gold “Eiffel Tower”. Normalized: [eiffel, tower, in, paris] and [eiffel, tower]. Shared tokens: 2. \(P = 2/4 = 0.5\), \(R = 2/2 = 1\), \(F_1 = 2 \cdot 0.5 \cdot 1 / 1.5 = 0.667\). EM = 0. With a second gold answer “the Eiffel Tower in Paris”, EM and F1 both become 1 by (145.12).

    EM and F1 for LLM answers

    EM and F1 were made for extractive models whose answers are short spans. A chat LLM writes full sentences, so its F1 drops even when it is right, as Figure 5 shows (0.286 for a correct answer). Common fixes:

    • Ask for a short answer in the prompt (“Answer with a few words only.”) and give a few examples of the format (session 143).
    • Report answer containment (“does the normalized gold answer appear inside the normalized prediction?”). It is generous: a long answer that lists many candidates also passes.
    • Use an LLM judge or human review for semantic equivalence (“two thousand” vs “2,000”) (session 155). Then check the judge against human labels.

    Whatever you choose, report it exactly, because EM, F1 and containment numbers on the same outputs can differ by many points.

    Putting it together in PyTorch

    The code below has three parts: the SQuAD metrics (145.10 to 145.12), the best-legal-span decoder (145.6), and a tiny extractive reader trained from scratch on a synthetic task with the loss (145.5). The task: the input is [CLS] key length [SEP] passage, and the answer is the length tokens right after the key token in the passage. The model must find the key (question-passage matching) and mark the span. It runs on a CPU in about 10 seconds and is deterministic.

    import re, string, collections, torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F
torch.manual_seed(0)

# 1) SQuAD metrics: normalize, exact match, token F1, max over gold answers (145.10-145.12)
def normalize(s):
    s = s.lower()
    s = "".join(ch for ch in s if ch not in set(string.punctuation))
    s = re.sub(r"\b(a|an|the)\b", " ", s)
    return " ".join(s.split())
def em(pred, gold):
    return float(normalize(pred) == normalize(gold))
def f1(pred, gold):
    p, g = normalize(pred).split(), normalize(gold).split()
    common = sum((collections.Counter(p) & collections.Counter(g)).values())
    if common == 0:
        return 0.0
    prec, rec = common / len(p), common / len(g)
    return 2 * prec * rec / (prec + rec)
def score(pred, golds):
    return max(em(pred, g) for g in golds), max(f1(pred, g) for g in golds)
print(score("in 1889", ["1889", "in 1889 for the fair"]))   # (0.0, 0.667)
print(score("The Eiffel Tower", ["Eiffel tower!"]))        # (1.0, 1.0)

# 2) best legal span from start and end logits: i <= j < i + max_len (145.6)
def best_span(start_logits, end_logits, max_len=30):
    s = start_logits[:, None] + end_logits[None, :]           # score[i, j]
    n = s.shape[0]
    i, j = torch.meshgrid(torch.arange(n), torch.arange(n), indexing="ij")
    legal = (j >= i) & (j - i < max_len)
    s = s.masked_fill(~legal, float("-inf"))
    return divmod(s.argmax().item(), n), s.max().item()

# 3) a tiny extractive reader on a synthetic task
V, P, CLS, SEP = 30, 16, 0, 1
def batch(B):
    keys = torch.randint(10, V, (B,))
    lens = torch.randint(1, 4, (B,))
    pas = torch.randint(10, V, (B, P))
    pos = torch.randint(0, P - 3, (B,))
    for b in range(B):                                     # the key appears exactly once
        while (pas[b] == keys[b]).any():
            pas[b][pas[b] == keys[b]] = torch.randint(10, V, ((pas[b] == keys[b]).sum(),))
        pas[b, pos[b]] = keys[b]
    x = torch.cat([torch.full((B, 1), CLS), keys[:, None], (lens + 1)[:, None],
                   torch.full((B, 1), SEP), pas], 1)
    start = 4 + pos + 1
    end = start + lens - 1
    return x, start, end

class Reader(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d=64):
        super().__init__()
        self.emb = nn.Embedding(V, d); self.pos = nn.Embedding(4 + P, d)
        layer = nn.TransformerEncoderLayer(d, 4, 4 * d, dropout=0.0, batch_first=True, norm_first=True)
        self.enc = nn.TransformerEncoder(layer, 2, enable_nested_tensor=False)
        self.qa = nn.Linear(d, 2)                          # the start and end vectors s, e (145.4)
    def forward(self, x):
        h = self.enc(self.emb(x) + self.pos(torch.arange(x.shape[1])))
        s, e = self.qa(h).unbind(-1)
        return s, e

model = Reader()
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=3e-3)
for step in range(600):
    x, st, en = batch(64)
    s, e = model(x)
    loss = (F.cross_entropy(s, st) + F.cross_entropy(e, en)) / 2   # formula (145.5)
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    if step % 200 == 0:
        print(step, round(loss.item(), 3))                  # 3.41, 2.61, 0.391

model.eval(); torch.manual_seed(1)
x, st, en = batch(500)
with torch.no_grad():
    s, e = model(x)
exact = 0
for b in range(500):
    (i, j), _ = best_span(s[b, 4:], e[b, 4:], max_len=5)  # search passage tokens only
    exact += int(i + 4 == st[b] and j + 4 == en[b])
print("span exact match:", exact / 500)                   # 1.0

    The printed numbers in the comments come from running this code with PyTorch 2.8 on a CPU. Other versions may give slightly different losses. Note the plateau near 2.6 at step 200: the model first has to learn to match the key, then the loss falls fast. Also note s[b, 4:]: the decoder searches only the passage, never the question, which is the “inside the passage” rule of (145.6).

    Common mistakes

    • Wrong label order for the NLI head. facebook/bart-large-mnli uses 0 = contradiction, 1 = neutral, 2 = entailment [Certain]; other models use other orders. Read id2label from the config; never hard-code index 2.
    • Single-label scoring for multi-topic text. Softmax across labels (145.2) forces the scores to share one unit of mass, so two true labels both look weak and an off-topic text still gets a confident-looking winner.
    • Taking argmax start and argmax end separately. The end can come before the start, or the span can be 200 tokens long. Search legal pairs only (145.6).
    • Allowing spans in the question or in special tokens. Mask every position outside the passage before decoding, except [CLS] for the null score.
    • Character-to-token offset bugs. SQuAD gives character offsets. Map them with the tokenizer’s offset mapping and check by decoding the gold span back to text for a few hundred examples.
    • Truncating long passages instead of windowing. The answer is often after token 384. Use overlapping windows (145.8) and remember that “stride” means step in BERT code and overlap in Hugging Face tokenizers.
    • Comparing EM/F1 numbers computed with different normalizations. A scorer that does not drop articles and punctuation reports lower numbers for the same outputs. Use the official script.
    • Blaming the reader when the retriever failed. Measure recall@\(k\) first; the reader cannot find an answer that is not in its input.

    Interview questions

    What are the three NLI labels? Give an example of each.basic
    Given a premise taken as true, a hypothesis is entailed (it must be true: “A man plays a guitar” → “A man plays an instrument”), contradicted (it cannot be true: “The man is asleep”), or neutral (the premise does not decide it: “The man is famous”). The judgment is what a normal reader would infer, not strict logic. SNLI (570K pairs, image captions) and MultiNLI (433K pairs, ten genres, matched and mismatched test sets) are the standard datasets.
    How do you classify text into labels you have no training data for, using an NLI model?core
    Use the text as the premise and build one hypothesis per label, for example “This example is about {label}.” Run the NLI model on each pair. For a single-label task, take the softmax of the entailment logits across labels (145.2) and choose the largest. For a multi-label task, score each label alone with a softmax over its entailment and contradiction logits, \(\sigma(z_E - z_C)\) (145.3), and keep labels above a tuned threshold. Cost is one forward pass per label; the template and label wording matter, so validate on a small labeled set.
    What is a hypothesis-only baseline and why does it matter?core
    It is a classifier that sees only the hypothesis, not the premise. On SNLI such a model scored about 67% and on MultiNLI 53% (chance is 33%), because crowd workers left label-specific traces: negations for contradiction, added purpose clauses for neutral, generic words for entailment. It shows that high NLI accuracy can come from shortcuts rather than inference. Run it on any new pair-classification benchmark; harder datasets were built adversarially to reduce these artifacts.
    How does BERT do extractive QA? What are the new parameters and the loss?core
    The input is [CLS] question [SEP] passage [SEP]. BERT adds a start vector \(\mathbf{s}\) and an end vector \(\mathbf{e}\) (one linear layer with 2 outputs). Each token gets a start logit \(\mathbf{s}^\top\mathbf{h}_i\) and an end logit \(\mathbf{e}^\top\mathbf{h}_j\); softmaxes over positions give \(p_{\text{start}}\) and \(p_{\text{end}}\) (145.4). The loss is the average of the cross-entropies at the gold start and gold end (145.5). Everything else is the pretrained encoder, fine-tuned end to end (3 epochs, lr \(5\times10^{-5}\), batch 32 in the paper).
    Why not take the argmax of the start logits and the argmax of the end logits?core
    Start and end are predicted independently, so the best end may come before the best start, may lie in the question, or may give a very long span. Decode by maximizing \(s_i + e_j\) over legal pairs only: \(i \le j\), \(j - i + 1 \le L_{\max}\) (30 in BERT), both inside the passage (145.6). In practice take the top 20 starts and top 20 ends and check the 400 pairs, which is fast.
    How do you answer questions over a passage longer than the model’s input?advanced
    Split the passage into overlapping windows, each with the full question: \(m = M - |q| - 3\) passage tokens per window, step \(r\), so about \(1 + \lceil (n-m)/r \rceil\) windows (145.8). Run the reader on each, take the best span over all windows, and for tokens seen in several windows prefer the window where they have the most context on both sides. For no-answer decisions use the minimum null score over windows. Alternatives: a long-context encoder, or first retrieve the relevant chunks. Watch the meaning of “stride”: step in BERT code, overlap in Hugging Face tokenizers.
    How does a model handle unanswerable questions in SQuAD 2.0?advanced
    During training, the gold start and end of an unanswerable question both point at [CLS]. At test time compute the null score \(s_{\text{null}} = s_{\text{CLS}} + e_{\text{CLS}}\) and the best legal span score; predict “no answer” if \(s_{\text{null}}\) minus the best span score is above a threshold \(\tau\) (145.7). Tune \(\tau\) on the dev set for F1; it trades answering wrongly against abstaining wrongly. SQuAD 2.0 added over 50,000 adversarial unanswerable questions; a system with 86% F1 on SQuAD 1.1 fell to 66% on it.
    Define EM and F1. Why is F1 never below EM?basic
    Both normalize first: lowercase, strip punctuation, drop “a”, “an”, “the”, collapse spaces. EM is 1 if the normalized strings are equal. F1 treats both as bags of tokens: precision = shared / predicted tokens, recall = shared / gold tokens, F1 their harmonic mean (145.11). Each question takes the max over its gold answers (145.12). If EM is 1 the bags are equal and F1 is 1, so F1 ≥ EM for every question; F1 also gives partial credit, such as 0.667 for “in 1889” against “1889”.
    Design an open-domain QA system over 10 million documents. Where do errors come from?advanced
    Retrieve then read. Offline: chunk documents into passages, embed them with a bi-encoder (DPR-style, 145.9) and also index them with BM25; at query time take the top 50 to 100 from both, re-rank with a cross-encoder to the top 5 to 20, then run an extractive reader or an LLM. Errors multiply: end-to-end accuracy is roughly recall@k times reader accuracy given the answer is present (for example 0.8 × 0.7 = 0.56). Measure each stage alone, return the source passage with each answer, and use a null threshold or an NLI check so the system can abstain.
    With strong LLMs, is extractive QA still useful?advanced
    Yes, in several cases. An extractive model cannot invent text: its answer is always a quote with a location, which suits regulated domains and highlighting in a UI. It is small and cheap (one encoder pass per window), so it fits high-volume or on-device use. Its scores give a natural abstain rule (null score). LLMs are better at answers that need combining several facts or rephrasing; a common hybrid is an LLM answer plus an extractive or NLI check that the answer is supported by the passage.
    Your LLM gets F1 0.3 on a QA set, but people say its answers are good. What is going on?core
    EM and F1 were built for short span answers. A sentence such as “The tower was completed in the year 1889.” against gold “1889” gets precision 1/6, recall 1, F1 0.286, although it is correct. Fix the measurement: prompt for short answers with few-shot format examples, report answer containment as well, and use an LLM judge checked against human labels for semantic equivalence. Report exactly which metric you used.

    Key takeaways

    • NLI labels a premise-hypothesis pair as entailment, neutral or contradiction with a cross-encoder and a 3-way head (145.1). SNLI and MultiNLI are the standard data; hypothesis-only models score 67% and 53%, so check for shortcuts.
    • An NLI model is a zero-shot classifier: one hypothesis per label, softmax across labels for single-label (145.2), \(\sigma(z_E - z_C)\) per label for multi-label (145.3).
    • Extractive QA adds a start and an end vector (145.4), trains with two cross-entropies (145.5), and decodes the best legal span (145.6).
    • Long passages are split into overlapping windows (145.8); unanswerable questions use the [CLS] null score and a tuned threshold (145.7).
    • Open-domain QA retrieves with a fast bi-encoder (145.9) and reads with a slow cross-encoder or a generator; the stages multiply, so measure recall first.
    • EM and F1 normalize, compare tokens, and take the max over gold answers (145.10 to 145.12). They undervalue long LLM answers, so control the answer format or add a judge.
