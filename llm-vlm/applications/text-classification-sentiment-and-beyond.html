    Text classification: sentiment and beyond

    Putting a label on a text is the most common job for language models in real products: sentiment, topic, intent, spam, safety. This session builds the three ways to do it (an encoder with a head, a decoder that compares label words, and embeddings with logistic regression), shows how to remove a prompt's bias with calibration, how to measure a classifier with F1, ROC-AUC and a well-chosen threshold, and how to classify documents longer than the model's window.

    Time: 2.5 to 3 hours (reading 2 hours, exercises 45 minutes)Level: Beginner to interview levelBefore this: Sessions 33, 55 and 143

    What you will learn

    • Three ways to build a text classifier: encoder + [CLS] head, decoder + label words, frozen embeddings + logistic regression, and what each one trains and costs
    • Zero-shot classification by scoring label words instead of generating, including label tokenization and multi-token labels
    • Why prompts bias label scores (majority label, recency, common token) and how contextual calibration removes the bias, with real numbers from two small models on IMDb
    • Probability calibration with temperature scaling and the expected calibration error
    • Accuracy, F1, ROC-AUC and PR curves: what each measures, why ROC-AUC ignores class imbalance and precision does not
    • Choosing a decision threshold by metric, by constraint, or by cost
    • Long documents: head+tail truncation, overlapping windows, and the right rule to combine window scores

    Why this matters

    Most LLM work in companies is not chat. It is putting a label on a piece of text: is this review positive, is this message spam, which team should get this ticket, is this post safe to show? This is text classification.

    A classifier takes a text \(x\) and returns one label \(y\) from a fixed set (for example {negative, positive}), usually together with a score. Sentiment is the classic first example, but the same tools give you topic labels, intent detection for a chatbot, content moderation, routing of support tickets, and the safety filters that sit in front of almost every LLM product.

    Today you have three very different ways to build one. You can fine-tune a small encoder with a classification head. You can ask a decoder LLM and read the probabilities of the label words. Or you can turn texts into embedding vectors and train a tiny logistic regression on top. They differ in cost by orders of magnitude, in how much labeled data they need, and in how they fail. This session builds all three, shows how to make the zero-shot one reliable (calibration), how to measure a classifier honestly (F1, ROC-AUC, thresholds), and what to do when the text is longer than the model’s window.

    Interview tipTypical questions: “How would you do zero-shot classification with an LLM without generating text?”, “Your model has ROC-AUC 0.95 but precision in production is terrible. Why?”, “How do you classify a 20,000-token document with a 512-token model?”. All three are answered with numbers below.

    Three ways to build a classifier

    Every text classifier has the same three parts: a model that reads the text, a place where we read out a summary of it, and a small rule that turns that summary into a label. The three families differ in which model and which rule.

    1. Encoder + classification head

    BERT-style encoders put a special token [CLS] at the start of every input. Because attention in an encoder is bidirectional, the final hidden state of that position can see the whole text. We add one linear layer (the head) and a softmax:

    \[ p(y \mid x) = \softmax\big(W\, \mathbf{h}_{\texttt{[CLS]}}(x) + \mathbf{b}\big)_y, \qquad \mathcal{L} = -\log p(y^\ast \mid x) \tag{144.1} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\mathbf{h}_{\texttt{[CLS]}}(x)\)
    the final hidden state at the [CLS] position, a vector of size \(d\) (768 for BERT-base)
    \(W, \mathbf{b}\)
    the new head: a \(K \times d\) matrix and \(K\) biases, for \(K\) classes
    \(y^\ast\)
    the correct label of the training example; \(\mathcal{L}\) is the usual cross-entropy

    For BERT-base and two classes the head has \(768 \times 2 + 2 = 1{,}538\) numbers. Usually we fine-tune the whole encoder (about 110 M weights) together with the head, with a small learning rate. The input is limited to 512 tokens. [Certain] With a decoder model the same idea works if you read the hidden state of the last token instead, because only the last token has seen the whole text (session 55).

    2. Decoder + label words

    A decoder LLM already knows what “positive” means. Write the text into a prompt that ends where the label should come, for example Review: … Sentiment (positive or negative):, and look at the probability the model gives to each label word as the next token. Nothing is trained. The next section explains this in detail.

    3. Embeddings + logistic regression

    An embedding model turns a whole text into one vector \(\mathbf{e}(x) \in \R^d\) (session 60). Freeze it, compute vectors for your labeled examples once, and train a logistic regression: a single linear layer with a sigmoid, the oldest classifier in NLP (SLP chapter 4):

    \[ p(y = 1 \mid x) = \sigma\big(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{e}(x) + b\big), \qquad \min_{\mathbf{w}, b}\; -\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log p(y_i \mid x_i) + \frac{\lambda}{2} \lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert^2 \tag{144.2} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\sigma(z)\)
    the sigmoid \(1/(1 + e^{-z})\), which turns any number into a probability
    \(\mathbf{w}, b\)
    \(d\) weights and one bias: the only trainable numbers
    \(\lambda\)
    the strength of L2 regularization; it stops the weights from growing huge when \(d\) is close to \(n\)

    With \(K\) classes, replace the sigmoid by a softmax over \(K\) scores. Training takes seconds on a CPU, and the same stored vectors can feed ten different classifiers and a search index. SetFit (Tunstall et al., 2022) improves this recipe by first fine-tuning the embedding model contrastively on pairs built from the few labeled examples. [Certain]

    AnalogyThree ways to sort mail. The encoder + head is a clerk you train for weeks on your company’s letters. The decoder + label words is a very well-read stranger: you only ask “is this a complaint?”, and you must phrase the question carefully. Embeddings + logistic regression is a machine that writes a short summary code on each envelope; you teach a simple rule on top of the codes in an afternoon.

    Beyond sentiment: many classes and many labels

    Nothing above is special to two classes. Topic and intent tasks have tens or hundreds of classes: the head simply has more rows. Some tasks are multi-label: a post can be both “spam” and “offensive”. Then each label gets its own sigmoid and its own threshold, and the loss is a sum of binary cross-entropies, one per label. Do not use a softmax for multi-label tasks: it forces the labels to compete.

    Zero-shot: compare label likelihoods

    The decoder approach turns a classification task into a fill-in-the-blank. A template wraps the input; a verbalizer maps every class \(y\) to a word or short phrase \(v_y\) (the name comes from PET, Schick and Schütze, 2020). We do not let the model generate; we score every label phrase:

    \[ s(y) = \log P_\theta\big(v_y \mid \text{prompt}(x)\big) = \sum_{j=1}^{|v_y|} \log P_\theta\big(v_{y,j} \mid \text{prompt}(x),\, v_{y,1..j-1}\big), \qquad \hat y = \argmax_y s(y) \tag{144.3} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(v_y\)
    the label phrase for class \(y\), for example the token “ positive” (with its leading space)
    \(v_{y,j}\)
    its \(j\)-th token, if the phrase has more than one token
    \(s(y)\)
    the total log-probability of the phrase; a softmax over the \(s(y)\) of all classes gives \(p(y \mid x)\)

    Why score instead of generate? Generation can answer “Positive.”, “The review is positive”, or something off-topic, and you then need a parser. Scoring always returns one of your classes, gives a probability for each, and costs one forward pass when every label is a single token: all label probabilities come from the same next-token distribution. This is how GPT-3 was evaluated on classification tasks. [Likely]

    Watch outTokenization matters. In most BPE vocabularies “ positive” (with a space) and “positive” are different tokens. If the prompt ends with a colon and no space, the label must include the leading space. Check with the tokenizer that each label is the token you think it is (session 9).

    Labels with several tokens

    If one label is “positive” (1 token) and another is “mixed feelings” (2 tokens), the sum in (144.3) punishes the longer label: every extra token adds a negative log-probability. Say the model gives \(\log P(\text{“ positive”}) = -1.6\), and \(-2.0\) and \(-0.5\) to the two tokens of “ mixed feelings”. The sum gives \(-1.6\) against \(-2.5\): “positive” wins. The average per token gives \(-1.6\) against \(-1.25\): “mixed feelings” wins. There is no rule that is always right. The simplest fix is to choose single-token labels. Another is to compare against how likely each label is with no input at all, which leads to calibration.

    Calibration: removing the model’s bias

    The word “calibration” has two meanings in this session. Both matter.

    Meaning 1: bias in zero-shot and few-shot prompts

    Zhao et al. (2021) showed that few-shot accuracy of GPT-3 changes a lot with the choice and the order of examples, and traced it to a bias of the model toward some answers. They name three: majority label bias (the label seen most often in the prompt), recency bias (the label of the last example), and common token bias (labels that are frequent words in pretraining data). [Certain] The model’s score for a label is “evidence from the text” plus “a prior for that label in this prompt”. We want only the first part.

    Contextual calibration estimates the prior with a content-free input: the same prompt with the text replaced by “N/A”, an empty string, or “[MASK]”. Such an input carries no evidence, so whatever the model prefers there is pure bias. Let \(\mathbf{p}_{\text{cf}}\) be the label probabilities for the content-free input (averaged over the three). Then divide it out:

    \[ q(y \mid x) = \frac{p(y \mid x) / p_{\text{cf}}(y)}{\sum_{y'} p(y' \mid x) / p_{\text{cf}}(y')} \tag{144.4} \]

    The paper writes this as \(\hat{\mathbf{q}} = \softmax(W\hat{\mathbf{p}} + \mathbf{b})\) with \(W = \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{p}_{\text{cf}})^{-1}\) and \(\mathbf{b} = 0\), and takes the argmax, which is the same decision. [Certain] For two classes it becomes very simple. Take the log of the odds:

    \[ \log \frac{q(1 \mid x)}{q(0 \mid x)} = \log \frac{p(1 \mid x)}{p(0 \mid x)} - \log \frac{p_{\text{cf}}(1)}{p_{\text{cf}}(0)} \tag{144.5} \]

    So, for a binary task, contextual calibration subtracts one constant from every log-odds. It moves the decision threshold from 0 to the content-free log-odds. It cannot change the order of the examples, so the ROC-AUC (Section 5) stays exactly the same. With many classes it can change which label wins in more complex ways. A related idea, domain-conditional PMI (Holtzman et al., 2021), scores each answer by \(\log P(v_y \mid \text{prompt}(x)) - \log P(v_y \mid \text{prompt without } x)\): again “how much more likely did the text make this label”. [Certain]

    Worked example: one review, real bias numbers

    For our zero-shot prompt (below), Qwen2.5-0.5B gives the content-free inputs on average \(p_{\text{cf}}(\text{neg}) = 0.723\) and \(p_{\text{cf}}(\text{pos}) = 0.277\). [Certain: our run] The model leans to “negative” before it reads anything.

    A review gets raw \(p(\text{pos}) = 0.40\), \(p(\text{neg}) = 0.60\): the raw answer is “negative”. Formula (144.4): \(0.40 / 0.277 = 1.444\) and \(0.60 / 0.723 = 0.830\), so \(q(\text{pos}) = 1.444 / (1.444 + 0.830) = 0.635\): now “positive”.

    Same with (144.5): raw log-odds \(\ln(0.40/0.60) = -0.405\), content-free log-odds \(\ln(0.277/0.723) = -0.957\), calibrated log-odds \(-0.405 + 0.957 = 0.552\), and \(\sigma(0.552) = 0.635\).

    Figure 2 simulates this. Each review has some evidence for “positive”; the model adds the same bias \(\beta\) to all of them. Move the bias and watch accuracy fall while the ranking (AUC) stays fixed. Then turn on calibration.

    Meaning 2: do the probabilities mean what they say?

    A classifier is calibrated in the probability sense if, among all examples where it says 0.9, about 90% are really positive. Modern networks are often over-confident. Guo et al. (2017) showed that temperature scaling, dividing all logits by one number \(T\) fitted on a validation set, is “surprisingly effective”. [Certain] The usual measure is the expected calibration error:

    \[ p_T(y \mid x) = \softmax\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{z}(x)}{T}\right)_y, \qquad \mathrm{ECE} = \sum_{b=1}^{M} \frac{n_b}{n}\, \big| \operatorname{acc}(b) - \operatorname{conf}(b) \big| \tag{144.6} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\mathbf{z}(x)\), \(T\)
    the logits, and the temperature (\(T \gt 1\) makes the model less confident); \(T\) is chosen to minimize the validation loss
    \(M\), \(n_b\)
    the confidence range is cut into \(M\) bins (often 10 or 15); \(n_b\) examples fall in bin \(b\)
    \(\operatorname{acc}(b)\), \(\operatorname{conf}(b)\)
    the real accuracy and the average predicted confidence inside bin \(b\)

    Example: a bin holds 100 predictions with average confidence 0.90 but only 75 are right: that bin contributes a gap of 0.15. Temperature scaling does not change the argmax, so accuracy stays the same; it only makes the probabilities honest. That matters as soon as you use a threshold or combine scores, which is the next two sections.

    Measuring a classifier: accuracy, F1, ROC-AUC

    Every evaluation starts from the confusion matrix: true positives (TP), false positives (FP), true negatives (TN), false negatives (FN). Accuracy is \((TP + TN)/n\). Precision \(TP/(TP + FP)\), recall \(TP/(TP + FN)\) and their harmonic mean F1 were built in session 55, together with the “always negative” model that has 90% accuracy and F1 = 0. Here we add the tools that look at all thresholds at once.

    The ROC curve and ROC-AUC

    A classifier gives a score \(s(x)\); we say “positive” when \(s(x) \gt t\). Each threshold \(t\) gives two rates:

    \[ \mathrm{TPR}(t) = \frac{TP}{TP + FN} = P\big(s \gt t \mid y = 1\big), \qquad \mathrm{FPR}(t) = \frac{FP}{FP + TN} = P\big(s \gt t \mid y = 0\big) \tag{144.7} \]

    Sweep \(t\) from high to low and plot TPR against FPR: that is the ROC curve. A random scorer gives the diagonal; a perfect one goes straight up to (0, 1). The area under it has a beautiful meaning:

    \[ \mathrm{AUC} = P\big(s(x^+) \gt s(x^-)\big) = \frac{1}{n_+ n_-} \sum_{i:\, y_i = 1}\; \sum_{j:\, y_j = 0} \Big( \mathbb{1}[s_i \gt s_j] + \tfrac12\, \mathbb{1}[s_i = s_j] \Big) \tag{144.8} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(x^+, x^-\)
    a random positive and a random negative example
    \(n_+, n_-\)
    the number of positives and negatives
    \(\mathbb{1}[\cdot]\)
    1 if the condition is true, else 0; ties count one half

    AUC is the chance that the model ranks a random positive above a random negative. It does not depend on any threshold, and, because TPR and FPR are each computed inside one class, it does not depend on the share of positives. [Certain] For two Gaussian score distributions with equal variance and means \(d\) standard deviations apart, \(\mathrm{AUC} = \Phi(d/\sqrt{2})\): 0.760 for \(d = 1\), 0.921 for \(d = 2\), 0.983 for \(d = 3\).

    Worked example: AUC by counting pairs

    Positives score 0.9, 0.8, 0.55, 0.4; negatives score 0.7, 0.35, 0.2, 0.1. There are \(4 \times 4 = 16\) pairs. 0.9 and 0.8 beat all four negatives (8 pairs). 0.55 and 0.4 beat 0.35, 0.2 and 0.1 but lose to 0.7 (6 pairs). AUC = 14/16 = 0.875. No threshold was needed.

    Why AUC can look great while precision is terrible

    Precision mixes the two classes, so it depends on the share of positives \(\pi\) (the prevalence). From (144.7):

    \[ \text{precision}(t) = \frac{\pi\, \mathrm{TPR}(t)}{\pi\, \mathrm{TPR}(t) + (1 - \pi)\, \mathrm{FPR}(t)} \tag{144.9} \]

    Take a good classifier with TPR = 0.90 and FPR = 0.05. On a balanced test set (\(\pi = 0.5\)) precision is 0.947. In production, where only 1% of messages are spam (\(\pi = 0.01\)), the same classifier has precision 0.154: six out of seven alarms are false, although accuracy is 95%. The ROC curve did not change at all. That is why, for rare positives, we also look at the precision-recall (PR) curve and its area, which do change with \(\pi\). Play with this in Figure 3.

    Choosing the threshold

    The model gives scores; a product needs yes or no. Three honest ways to pick the threshold, all on a validation set, never on the test set:

    1. Maximize the metric you report (for example F1), as the button in Figure 3 does.
    2. Fix one rate: “recall at least 95%” for a safety filter, or “at most 1% false positive rate” for a spam filter, and take the threshold that meets it.
    3. Minimize the expected cost, if the probabilities are calibrated (meaning 2). Say “positive” when the expected cost of saying it is lower: \((1 - p)\, C_{FP} \lt p\, C_{FN}\). That gives
    \[ \text{predict positive} \iff p(y = 1 \mid x) \gt t^\ast = \frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FP} + C_{FN}} \tag{144.10} \]

    If missing a fraudulent payment costs 10 times more than checking a good one (\(C_{FN} = 10\), \(C_{FP} = 1\)), then \(t^\ast = 1/11 = 0.091\). A payment with \(p = 0.2\) is flagged: flagging costs \(0.8 \times 1 = 0.8\) on average, not flagging costs \(0.2 \times 10 = 2.0\). The 0.5 threshold is only right when the two mistakes cost the same and the probabilities are calibrated.

    Long documents: split, classify, combine

    A BERT-style encoder reads at most 512 tokens; a contract, a support thread or a book chapter is much longer. Even a long-context LLM pays for every token, and it may use the middle of a long input poorly (session 28). Three options:

    1. Truncate. Keep only part of the text. Sun et al. (2019) compared on IMDb with BERT-base: keep the first 510 tokens (test error 5.63%), the last 510 (5.44%), or the first 128 plus the last 382 (5.42%, the best of all their methods). [Certain] Reviews put their verdict at the start and the end.
    2. Split, classify, combine. Cut the text into windows of \(w\) tokens that overlap by \(o\) tokens, classify each window, and combine the window scores with a fixed rule.
    3. Hierarchical model. Encode each window, then learn how to combine the window vectors (mean, max, or a small attention layer on top). In the same study, mean pooling (5.89%), max pooling (5.71%) and self-attention (5.49%) did not beat head+tail on IMDb. [Certain] On tasks where the evidence is in the middle, they can.

    With step \(w - o\), a document of \(L\) tokens needs

    \[ n = \left\lceil \frac{L - w}{w - o} \right\rceil + 1 \quad (L \gt w) \tag{144.11} \]

    windows. For \(L = 5{,}000\), \(w = 510\) (512 minus [CLS] and [SEP]) and \(o = 128\): \(n = \lceil 4490/382 \rceil + 1 = 13\) windows, so the model reads 6,630 tokens, 1.33 times the document. The overlap makes sure a sentence cut at a border is seen whole in at least one window.

    How to combine the window probabilities \(p_1, \dots, p_n\) depends on what the label means:

    \[ \bar p = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i} p_i, \qquad p_{\max} = \max_i p_i, \qquad p_{\text{OR}} = 1 - \prod_{i} (1 - p_i), \qquad p_{\text{logit}} = \sigma\!\left( \frac{1}{n} \sum_i \log \frac{p_i}{1 - p_i} \right) \tag{144.12} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\bar p\)
    the mean: right when the label is about the whole text (overall sentiment, topic)
    \(p_{\max}\)
    the max: right when one window is enough (“contains a threat”, “has a non-compete clause”)
    \(p_{\text{OR}}\)
    noisy-OR: the chance that at least one window is positive, if windows were independent; many weak signals add up
    \(p_{\text{logit}}\)
    the mean of log-odds: like a vote where very confident windows count more
    Worked example: one toxic paragraph in a long post

    12 windows; 11 have \(p_i = 0.05\) and one has \(p_i = 0.95\). Mean: \(\bar p = 0.125\), the post passes. Mean of logits: 0.079, passes. Max: 0.950, blocked. Noisy-OR: \(1 - 0.95^{11} \times 0.05 = 0.972\), blocked. For a moderation label, max or noisy-OR is right; the mean hides the bad paragraph. But with 12 windows all at 0.15, noisy-OR gives \(1 - 0.85^{12} = 0.858\) and blocks a harmless post, while max gives 0.15.

    Putting it together

    Algorithm 1: calibrated zero-shot classification with a decoder LLM
    1. input: model \(P_\theta\), template \(\text{prompt}(\cdot)\), label phrases \(v_1, \dots, v_K\), texts \(x_1, \dots, x_n\)
    2. check with the tokenizer that each \(v_k\) is one token (with the leading space); if not, decide sum or average for (144.3)
    3. for \(c\) in {“N/A”, “”, “[MASK]”}: \(\mathbf{p}_c \leftarrow \softmax_k\, s(k)\) for \(\text{prompt}(c)\) // content-free inputs
    4. \(\mathbf{p}_{\text{cf}} \leftarrow\) mean of the \(\mathbf{p}_c\)
    5. for each text \(x_i\): one forward pass on \(\text{prompt}(x_i)\); \(\mathbf{p}_i \leftarrow \softmax_k\, s(k)\) // formula (144.3)
    6. \(\mathbf{q}_i \leftarrow (\mathbf{p}_i / \mathbf{p}_{\text{cf}})\) normalized to sum 1 // formula (144.4)
    7. return \(\argmax_k q_{i,k}\), and \(\mathbf{q}_i\) as scores for thresholds and AUC
    Algorithm 2: classify a long document
    1. input: token ids of length \(L\), window \(w\), overlap \(o\), window classifier \(f\), combine rule \(g\), threshold \(t\)
    2. if \(L \le w\): return \(f(\text{ids}) \gt t\)
    3. starts \(\leftarrow 0, (w - o), 2(w - o), \dots\) while the window still adds new tokens // \(n\) windows, formula (144.11)
    4. for each start \(a\): \(p_a \leftarrow f(\text{ids}[a : a + w])\) // batch the windows in one forward pass
    5. \(p_{\text{doc}} \leftarrow g(p_1, \dots, p_n)\) // mean for whole-text labels, max for “contains X”, formula (144.12)
    6. return \(p_{\text{doc}} \gt t\), with \(t\) tuned on validation documents (not on windows)

    The code we ran for the table above (it needs transformers and downloads the 0.5B model the first time; greedy scoring, so it is deterministic):

    import torch
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForCausalLM
torch.manual_seed(0)
name = "Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B"
tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(name)
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(name, dtype=torch.float32).eval()

labels = [" negative", " positive"]                       # note the leading space
ids = [tok(l, add_special_tokens=False).input_ids for l in labels]
assert all(len(i) == 1 for i in ids)                      # one token each, so one forward pass
ids = [i[0] for i in ids]
template = "Review: {x}\nSentiment (positive or negative):"

@torch.no_grad()
def label_probs(text):                                  # formula (144.3), renormalized over the labels
    x = tok(template.format(x=text), return_tensors="pt").input_ids
    logp = model(x).logits[0, -1].log_softmax(-1)
    return logp[ids].softmax(-1)

# contextual calibration, formula (144.4)
p_cf = torch.stack([label_probs(c) for c in ["N/A", "", "[MASK]"]]).mean(0)
def classify(text):
    p = label_probs(text)
    q = p / p_cf; q = q / q.sum()
    return p, q

print("content-free:", p_cf)                           # our run: tensor([0.7225, 0.2775])
for r in ["I did not expect much, but it was a lovely film.",
          "Two hours of my life I will never get back."]:
    p, q = classify(r)
    print(labels[p.argmax()], labels[q.argmax()], q)

    import math, torch
torch.manual_seed(0)

# 1) a validation set: 2,000 scores, 10% positives, separation d = 2
n, pi, d = 2000, 0.1, 2.0
y = (torch.rand(n) < pi).long()
s = torch.randn(n) + d * y                       # higher score = more likely positive

# 2) ROC-AUC = P(random positive scores above random negative), via ranks
def roc_auc(s, y):
    r = s.argsort().argsort().double() + 1       # ranks 1..n (continuous scores: no ties)
    n1 = int(y.sum()); n0 = len(y) - n1
    return ((r[y == 1].sum() - n1 * (n1 + 1) / 2) / (n1 * n0)).item()

# 3) precision, recall, F1 at a threshold, and the best-F1 threshold
def prf(s, y, t):
    p = (s > t).long()
    tp = int(((p == 1) & (y == 1)).sum()); fp = int(((p == 1) & (y == 0)).sum()); fn = int(((p == 0) & (y == 1)).sum())
    P = tp / max(1, tp + fp); R = tp / max(1, tp + fn)
    return P, R, 2 * P * R / max(1e-12, P + R)

auc = roc_auc(s, y)
print(f"positives {int(y.sum())}, ROC-AUC {auc:.3f} (theory {0.5*(1+math.erf(d/2)):.3f})")
print("at t=0:  P %.3f R %.3f F1 %.3f" % prf(s, y, 0.0))
ts = torch.linspace(-2, 5, 701)
best = max(ts.tolist(), key=lambda t: prf(s, y, t)[2])
print("best t=%.2f: P %.3f R %.3f F1 %.3f" % ((best,) + prf(s, y, best)))

# 4) cost-based threshold for calibrated probabilities: a miss costs 10x a false alarm
c_fp, c_fn = 1.0, 10.0
print("cost threshold", round(c_fp / (c_fp + c_fn), 3))

# 5) long documents: overlapping windows, classify each, combine
def windows(ids, w=510, overlap=128):
    step = w - overlap
    starts = list(range(0, max(1, len(ids) - overlap), step))
    return [ids[i:i + w] for i in starts]
doc = list(range(5000))                          # stand-in for 5,000 token ids
ch = windows(doc)
print("chunks", len(ch), "last chunk covers", ch[-1][0], "to", ch[-1][-1])
p = torch.tensor([0.05] * 7 + [0.95] + [0.05] * 4)  # per-chunk P(positive), 12 chunks
logit = torch.log(p / (1 - p))
print("mean %.3f  mean-logit %.3f  max %.3f  noisy-OR %.3f" % (
    p.mean(), torch.sigmoid(logit.mean()), p.max(), 1 - torch.prod(1 - p)))
# Output (PyTorch 2.x, CPU):
# positives 202, ROC-AUC 0.931 (theory 0.921)
# at t=0:  P 0.188 R 0.995 F1 0.317
# best t=1.46: P 0.571 R 0.698 F1 0.628
# cost threshold 0.091
# chunks 13 last chunk covers 4584 to 4999
# mean 0.125  mean-logit 0.079  max 0.950  noisy-OR 0.972

    Note the second line of output: with 10% positives, the threshold 0 finds almost every positive (recall 0.995) but precision is only 0.188. Tuning the threshold doubles F1, with the same model.

    Common mistakes

    • Label tokens that are not what you think. “positive” vs “ positive”, or a label that splits into three tokens. Print the token ids of every label before running anything.
    • Generating and parsing instead of scoring. Free generation gives answers outside your label set and no probabilities. Score the label words (144.3), or use constrained decoding.
    • Trusting raw zero-shot probabilities. The prior of the prompt can shift a balanced dataset to 40/60 predictions. Check the share of each predicted label, and calibrate (144.4).
    • Reporting accuracy or ROC-AUC alone with rare positives. AUC ignores prevalence (144.9). Report precision, recall and F1 at the chosen threshold, and the PR curve.
    • Choosing the threshold on the test set. That is training on the test set. Tune on validation, then report test once.
    • Using a 0.5 threshold on uncalibrated or class-weighted outputs. Weighted training already moves the threshold (session 55); fit temperature (144.6) or tune the threshold directly.
    • Silent truncation of long documents. Tokenizers cut at 512 tokens without an error. Log how many inputs were cut, and pick head+tail or windows on purpose.
    • Mean-pooling a decoder’s hidden states and calling it an embedding. It works somewhat (81.6–83.4% in our run), but a model trained to produce embeddings is the right tool for approach 3.

    Interview questions

    Name three ways to build a sentiment classifier with modern models. When would you pick each?basic
    (1) Fine-tune an encoder (BERT, RoBERTa, ModernBERT) with a linear head on [CLS]: best accuracy per dollar when you have hundreds to thousands of labels and need high throughput. (2) Prompt a decoder LLM and compare the probabilities of the label words: no labels needed, good for prototypes, rare classes and changing label sets, but expensive per call and biased by the prompt. (3) Freeze an embedding model and train logistic regression: very cheap, needs a modest amount of labels, and the same vectors serve search and many classifiers. A common path is (2) to bootstrap labels, then (1) or (3) in production.
    How do you do zero-shot classification with a decoder LLM without generating any text?basic
    Put the text into a template ending where the label should come, run one forward pass, and read the log-probabilities of the label tokens at the last position (formula 144.3). Take the argmax, or a softmax over the label scores to get probabilities. If labels are single tokens, all classes cost one forward pass; multi-token labels need the sum (or average) of token log-probs, computed with teacher forcing.
    Two labels have different numbers of tokens. What goes wrong and how do you fix it?core
    The sum of log-probs punishes the longer label, because every extra token adds a negative term. Averaging per token removes the length effect but can over-reward long phrases whose later tokens are easy. Fixes: pick single-token verbalizers; or normalize each label by its probability under a content-free or no-input prompt (contextual calibration, domain-conditional PMI); or use a fine-tuned head instead.
    What is contextual calibration, and why does it work?core
    Zhao et al. (2021) observed that LLM label scores carry a prompt-dependent prior: majority label, recency and common token biases. They feed a content-free input (“N/A”, empty, “[MASK]”) through the same prompt, average the label probabilities to get \(\mathbf{p}_{\text{cf}}\), and divide each prediction by it (W = diag(p_cf)⁻¹, b = 0), then take the argmax. Since a content-free input contains no evidence, its preferences estimate the bias. In our run on IMDb it raised zero-shot accuracy of Qwen2.5-0.5B from 85.6% to 91.2%, and of SmolLM2-135M from 58.8% to 65.0%.
    Why can contextual calibration not change ROC-AUC for a binary task?advanced
    In log-odds form (144.5), calibration subtracts the same constant \(\log(p_{\text{cf}}(1)/p_{\text{cf}}(0))\) from every example. A constant shift keeps the order of all scores, and AUC depends only on the order (144.8). So calibration only moves the threshold. It can fix accuracy and the predicted label share, but it cannot fix a bad ranking; for that you need a better prompt, demonstrations, or a better model. With more than two classes, per-class scaling can change the ranking within each class’s scores and the argmax in more complex ways.
    Accuracy, F1, ROC-AUC: which do you report for a fraud model with 0.5% positives?core
    Not accuracy: “never fraud” already has 99.5%. ROC-AUC is useful to compare rankings but hides the false-alarm rate in production, because it does not depend on prevalence. Report precision and recall (or F1) at the operating threshold, recall at a fixed false-positive budget, and the PR curve or PR-AUC, all measured with the real prevalence. Add per-class numbers and confidence intervals, since few positives make the estimates noisy.
    Your classifier has ROC-AUC 0.95 offline, but in production most alarms are false. Explain.core
    Precision depends on the share of positives: precision = πTPR / (πTPR + (1 − π)FPR), formula (144.9). The offline test set was probably balanced or richer in positives than production. With TPR 0.90 and FPR 0.05, precision is 0.947 at π = 0.5 but 0.154 at π = 0.01, with the same ROC curve. Fix: evaluate on data with the production prevalence, raise the threshold to reach the needed precision, or improve the model at the low-FPR end of the ROC curve.
    How do you choose the decision threshold?core
    On a validation set with realistic prevalence: maximize the reported metric (F1), or meet a constraint (recall ≥ 95%, FPR ≤ 1%), or, if probabilities are calibrated, minimize expected cost with \(t^\ast = C_{FP}/(C_{FP} + C_{FN})\) (144.10). With \(C_{FN} = 10\,C_{FP}\), \(t^\ast \approx 0.09\). Never tune on the test set, and re-check the threshold when the data or the model changes.
    How would you classify a 20,000-token document with a 512-token encoder?core
    Options: truncate wisely (head+tail, best on IMDb in Sun et al. 2019); split into overlapping windows (510 content tokens, overlap about 128: \(\lceil 19490/382 \rceil + 1 = 53\) windows), classify each in one batch, and combine with mean for whole-document labels or max/noisy-OR for “contains X” labels; or train a hierarchical model with attention over window vectors. Tune the threshold on document-level labels. Alternatives: a long-context encoder or LLM, at a higher cost per document.
    Your fine-tuned classifier says 0.99 on wrong examples. How do you fix its probabilities?advanced
    It is over-confident: not calibrated in the probability sense. Measure ECE and a reliability diagram on validation data (144.6). Fit temperature scaling: divide logits by a single \(T \gt 1\) chosen to minimize validation NLL. It keeps the argmax and accuracy and usually reduces ECE a lot (Guo et al., 2017). Other options: Platt scaling (a logistic regression on the logit), isotonic regression, label smoothing during training. Re-calibrate after any change in data distribution.
    A product team wants to use GPT-class LLM calls for a 50M-messages-per-day spam filter. What do you suggest?advanced
    Use the LLM to label data, not to serve. Zero-shot or few-shot LLM labels (with calibration and human checks on a sample) can create a training set quickly; then fine-tune a small encoder or train logistic regression on embeddings. The small model handles 50M messages per day at a tiny fraction of the cost and latency, gives stable probabilities, and can be calibrated and thresholded. Keep the LLM for the uncertain band near the threshold, or for new categories, and monitor drift.

    Key takeaways

    • Three families: encoder + head on [CLS] (144.1), decoder + label words (144.3), frozen embeddings + logistic regression (144.2). They trade labeled data, cost and robustness.
    • Zero-shot with an LLM means scoring label words, not generating. Check label tokenization; multi-token labels need a length rule.
    • Prompts add a prior to the label scores. Contextual calibration divides it out with a content-free input (144.4); for two classes it is a pure threshold shift (144.5), so it fixes accuracy but not AUC. In our run: 85.6% → 91.2% on IMDb for Qwen2.5-0.5B.
    • ROC-AUC is the chance a positive outranks a negative (144.8) and ignores prevalence; precision does not (144.9). Choose thresholds on validation data, by metric, by constraint, or by cost (144.10). Make probabilities honest with temperature scaling (144.6).
    • Long documents: truncate with head+tail, or split into overlapping windows (144.11) and combine with the rule that matches the label: mean for whole-text labels, max or noisy-OR for “contains X” (144.12).
