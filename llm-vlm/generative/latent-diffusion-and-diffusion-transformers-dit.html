    Latent diffusion and diffusion transformers (DiT)

    Modern image and video generators do not run diffusion on pixels. They first compress the image with an autoencoder and run diffusion on the small latent, then decode once at the end. This session builds latent diffusion step by step, compares the U-Net backbone with the Diffusion Transformer (DiT), explains patchify and adaLN-Zero conditioning, and reads the DiT scaling results with real numbers.

    Time: 3 to 4 hours (reading 2 hours, exercises 1 to 2 hours)Level: Intermediate to interview levelBefore this: Diffusion 1 and 2 (sessions 225 and 226), variational autoencoders (session 223), the Vision Transformer (session 164); basic PyTorch

    What you will learn

    • Why diffusing in a compressed latent space is cheaper and better: perceptual vs semantic compression, and how to count the 48× saving of the Stable Diffusion autoencoder
    • How the autoencoder is trained (pixel, perceptual, adversarial and tiny KL losses), why the downsampling factor f = 4 to 8 works best, and why the latent scale 0.18215 matters
    • The latent diffusion training and sampling algorithms, and where the encoder, denoiser and decoder each run
    • The U-Net backbone with cross-attention to text, and its inductive biases
    • The Diffusion Transformer: patchify, the token count T = (I/p)², adaLN and adaLN-Zero conditioning, and the 2C-channel output
    • How to estimate the Gflops and parameters of a DiT from its config, and the DiT scaling result: compute, not parameter count, predicts FID
    • How DiT led to text-to-image and video transformers (PixArt-α, MM-DiT, spacetime patches)

    Why this matters

    Almost every well-known image and video generator since 2022 does its diffusion in a small latent space, not on pixels. Stable Diffusion, SDXL, PixArt, Stable Diffusion 3, FLUX and Sora all follow this plan. And since 2023, most new ones also replaced the convolutional U-Net with a plain transformer, the Diffusion Transformer (DiT).

    In session 225 and session 226 we learned to train a network that predicts noise, and to sample by removing noise step by step. Two problems remain. First, cost: a 512×512 RGB image has 786,432 numbers, and the network runs on all of them at every one of tens or hundreds of sampling steps. Second, most of those numbers are tiny details (exact texture, sensor noise) that the eye barely notices but that a pixel-space model must still spend capacity on. This session fixes both. We first compress the image with an autoencoder, then run diffusion on the small code. Then we change the denoiser from a U-Net to a transformer, which brings the scaling rules we know from LLMs to image generation.

    Interview tipTypical questions: “Why diffuse in latent space?”, “What does the VAE in Stable Diffusion do, and what are the latent shapes?”, “How does DiT turn a latent into tokens?”, “What is adaLN-Zero and why the zero?”, “What predicts the quality of a DiT model?”. Each one is answered below with numbers.

    The two papers behind this session are Rombach et al., High-Resolution Image Synthesis with Latent Diffusion Models (LDM, 2021), and Peebles & Xie, Scalable Diffusion Models with Transformers (DiT, 2022). We will read their key tables with real numbers.

    Step 1: compress first, then diffuse

    Think about what a generator must learn. Rombach et al. split it into two kinds of work. Perceptual compression removes high-frequency details that are hard to see but expensive to model. Semantic compression is the real creative part: which objects are in the scene, where, and how they relate. A pixel-space diffusion model does both jobs with one network. LDM gives the first job to an autoencoder that is trained once, and the second job to the diffusion model. [Certain]

    AnalogyAn architect does not draw every brick when designing a house. She works on a floor plan (the latent), and a builder (the decoder) fills in the bricks later in a standard way. The plan is small, so she can try many ideas quickly.

    The autoencoder has an encoder \(\mathcal{E}\) and a decoder \(\mathcal{D}\). The encoder shrinks the height and width by a downsampling factor \(f\) and outputs \(c\) channels:

    \[ \mathbf{z}_0 = s \cdot \mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}) \in \R^{\frac{H}{f} \times \frac{W}{f} \times c}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{x}} = \mathcal{D}(\mathbf{z}_0 / s) \in \R^{H \times W \times 3} \tag{228.1} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\mathbf{x}\)
    the image, \(H \times W\) pixels with 3 colors, scaled to \([-1, 1]\)
    \(\mathcal{E}, \mathcal{D}\)
    the encoder and decoder of the autoencoder; both are convolutional networks, frozen during diffusion training
    \(f\)
    the downsampling factor: each latent position covers an \(f \times f\) block of pixels
    \(c\)
    the number of latent channels (4 in Stable Diffusion 1.x and 2.x and SDXL)
    \(s\)
    a fixed scale factor that makes the latent values have standard deviation near 1 (explained below)
    \(\mathbf{z}_0\)
    the clean latent: the “data” for the diffusion model

    The number of values shrinks by

    \[ \text{compression} = \frac{H \cdot W \cdot 3}{\frac{H}{f} \cdot \frac{W}{f} \cdot c} = \frac{3 f^2}{c} \tag{228.2} \]
    Worked example: the Stable Diffusion autoencoder

    Stable Diffusion v1 uses \(f = 8\) and \(c = 4\). [Certain] A 512×512 image has \(512 \cdot 512 \cdot 3 = 786{,}432\) values. Its latent is \(64 \times 64 \times 4 = 16{,}384\) values. The ratio is \(3 \cdot 8^2 / 4 = 48\). For the 256×256 images used by DiT, the latent is \(32 \times 32 \times 4 = 4{,}096\) values instead of \(196{,}608\). Again 48 times fewer.

    Note that the spatial grid shrinks by \(f^2 = 64\), but the channel count grows from 3 to 4. So the latent is not 64 times smaller, “only” 48. Newer autoencoders (SD3, FLUX) keep \(f = 8\) but use \(c = 16\) to keep more detail, which gives \(3 \cdot 64/16 = 12\). [Likely]

    How the autoencoder is trained

    The autoencoder is a VAE-like network (session 223) but tuned for sharp reconstructions, much like VQGAN (session 224). Its loss mixes a pixel loss, a perceptual loss (distance between deep features of \(\mathbf{x}\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\)), a patch-based adversarial loss (a small discriminator that judges local realism), and a tiny regularizer on the latent:

    \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{AE}} = \lVert \mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}} \rVert_1 + \lambda_{P}\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{perc}}(\mathbf{x}, \hat{\mathbf{x}}) + \lambda_{G}\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{GAN}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}) + \lambda_{\text{KL}}\, \KL\big(q(\mathbf{z} \mid \mathbf{x}) \,\Vert\, \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, I)\big) \tag{228.3} \]

    The KL weight is very small (about \(10^{-6}\) in the released configs [Likely]). The KL term only stops the latent from drifting to huge values; it does not force the latent to be a nice Gaussian like in a real VAE. LDM also has a second option, a VQ layer instead of the KL term (“VQ-reg”). [Certain]

    The scale factor \(s\)

    Because the KL term is so weak, the raw latent has some arbitrary spread. The noise schedule from session 225 assumes data with a standard deviation near 1: at step \(t\), the signal-to-noise ratio is \(\bar\alpha_t / (1 - \bar\alpha_t)\) times the data variance. If the latent is 5 times too large, every noise level is effectively 25 times weaker than planned. So LDM measures the standard deviation of the latents on a batch of images and multiplies by \(s = 1/\text{std}\). For the Stable Diffusion 1.x VAE, \(s = 0.18215\); for the SDXL VAE, \(s = 0.13025\). [Certain] Forgetting \(s\), or dividing by it twice at decode time, is a classic bug.

    How much to compress: the factor \(f\)

    LDM tried \(f \in \{1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32\}\) on ImageNet. With \(f = 1\) or 2 (almost pixels), training is slow. With \(f = 32\), quality stops improving early: the autoencoder has thrown away detail that no diffusion model can bring back. \(f = 4\) to 16 is the good range, with \(f = 4\) and 8 best overall. After 2 million steps, LDM-8 had an FID about 38 points better than the pixel-space LDM-1. [Certain] FID (Fréchet Inception Distance) compares statistics of deep features of real and generated images; lower is better.

    Step 2: diffusion in the latent space

    Once the autoencoder is frozen, latent diffusion is exactly the DDPM of session 225, with \(\mathbf{x}_0\) replaced by \(\mathbf{z}_0\). We add noise, \(\mathbf{z}_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{z}_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon\), and train the denoiser to predict \(\varepsilon\):

    \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{LDM}} = \E_{\mathbf{x},\, y,\, \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, I),\, t} \Big[ \big\lVert \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta\big(\mathbf{z}_t,\, t,\, \tau_\phi(y)\big) \big\rVert_2^2 \Big], \qquad \mathbf{z}_0 = s\,\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}) \tag{228.4} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\varepsilon_\theta\)
    the denoiser (a U-Net or a DiT); it sees the noisy latent, the step and the condition
    \(y\)
    the condition: a class label, a text prompt, a depth map, ...
    \(\tau_\phi\)
    the condition encoder, for example a frozen CLIP text encoder that turns the prompt into a sequence of vectors
    \(t\)
    the noise step, drawn uniformly from \(1, \dots, 1000\)

    Nothing about the diffusion math changes. That is the beauty of the design: the autoencoder is a plug-in. You can train it once and reuse it for many diffusion models. DiT, for example, did not train its own autoencoder at all; it used the off-the-shelf Stable Diffusion VAE. [Certain] Sampling also stays the same (DDPM, DDIM, or the fast solvers of session 226), and only at the very end do we call the decoder once.

    Algorithm 1: one training step of latent diffusion
    1. input: a batch of images \(\mathbf{x}\) and conditions \(y\); frozen \(\mathcal{E}\), scale \(s\); denoiser \(\varepsilon_\theta\); schedule \(\bar\alpha_1, \dots, \bar\alpha_{1000}\)
    2. \(\mathbf{z}_0 \leftarrow s \cdot \mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x})\) // no gradient; often pre-computed once for the whole dataset
    3. with probability 0.1, replace \(y\) by the empty condition \(\varnothing\) // enables classifier-free guidance
    4. \(t \sim \text{Uniform}\{1, \dots, 1000\}\), \(\;\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, I)\)
    5. \(\mathbf{z}_t \leftarrow \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{z}_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon\)
    6. \(\mathcal{L} \leftarrow \lVert \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t, t, \tau_\phi(y)) \rVert^2\) // formula (228.4)
    7. update \(\theta\) with AdamW; update an EMA copy of \(\theta\) // sample with the EMA weights
    Algorithm 2: sampling an image with latent diffusion and guidance
    1. input: condition \(y\), guidance scale \(w\), number of steps \(K\), decoder \(\mathcal{D}\), scale \(s\)
    2. \(\mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, I)\) with the latent shape, e.g. \(4 \times 64 \times 64\) for a 512×512 image
    3. for \(k = K, \dots, 1\) (steps \(t_K \gt \dots \gt t_1\)):
    4.   \(\hat\varepsilon \leftarrow \varepsilon_\theta(\mathbf{z}, t_k, \varnothing) + w\,\big(\varepsilon_\theta(\mathbf{z}, t_k, y) - \varepsilon_\theta(\mathbf{z}, t_k, \varnothing)\big)\) // classifier-free guidance
    5.   \(\mathbf{z} \leftarrow \text{SolverStep}(\mathbf{z}, \hat\varepsilon, t_k, t_{k-1})\) // DDPM, DDIM or DPM-Solver step
    6. return \(\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{z} / s)\) // the decoder runs once, at the end

    Figure 2 walks through both algorithms with the real shapes of a 256×256 DiT.

    Watch outThe decoder is not free. For a 1024×1024 image, the VAE decoder works at full resolution and can take a noticeable share of the time and much of the peak memory, especially for video. Tiled decoding is a common fix. [Likely]

    The first backbone: a U-Net with cross-attention

    The original LDM and all Stable Diffusion 1.x/2.x/XL models use a U-Net as \(\varepsilon_\theta\). A U-Net is a convolutional encoder–decoder. The down path halves the resolution several times (for a 64×64 latent: 64, 32, 16, 8) while adding channels. The up path doubles it back. Skip connections copy features from each down level to the matching up level, so fine details do not have to pass through the small bottleneck. Each level has residual conv blocks; the noise step \(t\) enters every block as an added embedding. At the lower resolutions there are also attention layers.

    The condition enters through cross-attention (session 20): the queries come from the image features, and the keys and values come from the text vectors \(\tau_\phi(y)\):

    \[ \text{CrossAttn}(\varphi, \tau) = \softmax\!\left( \frac{(\varphi W_Q)(\tau W_K)^\top}{\sqrt{d}} \right) (\tau W_V) \tag{228.5} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\varphi\)
    the U-Net features at one level, flattened into a list of \(N\) spatial positions, shape \(N \times d_\varphi\)
    \(\tau = \tau_\phi(y)\)
    the text vectors, shape \(M \times d_\tau\) (77 tokens × 768 for CLIP ViT-L/14 in SD v1)
    \(W_Q, W_K, W_V\)
    learned projections into a shared width \(d\)

    Each image position asks “which words are about me?” and pulls in their meaning. Stable Diffusion v1 has an 860M-parameter U-Net and a 123M-parameter CLIP ViT-L/14 text encoder, and works at \(f = 8\). [Certain] SDXL made the U-Net about three times larger, mostly through more attention blocks and a wider cross-attention context from a second text encoder. [Certain]

    The U-Net has strong inductive biases: built-in assumptions about images. Convolutions are local and treat every position the same way, and the multi-scale design matches the fact that images have structure at many sizes. These biases help with little data. But they also make the network a hand-designed object: how many levels, where to put attention, how many channels at each level. Scaling it up is a craft.

    The second backbone: the Diffusion Transformer (DiT)

    Peebles & Xie asked a simple question: does the denoiser need the U-Net’s image-specific design at all? They took a standard Vision Transformer (session 164), changed as little as possible, and trained it on latents. The answer was no: with enough compute, the plain transformer is better, and its quality follows a clean scaling rule. [Certain]

    AnalogyA U-Net is a custom-built machine for one job. A transformer is a general engine. Once people learned how to make the engine bigger in a predictable way (for LLMs), it became easier to just use a bigger engine than to keep tuning the custom machine.

    Patchify: from latent to tokens

    The input is the noisy latent, \(I \times I \times C\) with \(I = 32\) and \(C = 4\) for 256×256 images. DiT cuts it into non-overlapping \(p \times p\) patches, flattens each patch into a vector of \(p^2 C\) numbers, and maps it to width \(d\) with one linear layer. Fixed sine-cosine position embeddings are added. The number of tokens is

    \[ T = \left( \frac{I}{p} \right)^{2}, \qquad \text{patch vector length} = p^2 C \tag{228.6} \]

    DiT tries \(p \in \{2, 4, 8\}\), giving \(T = 256, 64, 16\). Halving \(p\) multiplies \(T\) by 4 and the compute by at least 4, but leaves the parameter count almost unchanged: only the tiny input and output layers depend on \(p\). [Certain] So the patch size is a pure “compute knob”. Explore the order of the cut in Figure 3.

    The DiT block and how the condition gets in

    A DiT block is a standard pre-norm transformer block: LayerNorm, self-attention, residual add, LayerNorm, MLP (width \(4d\), GELU), residual add (session 22). The question is how to feed in the step \(t\) and the class \(y\). First both become one vector per image:

    \[ \mathbf{c} = \text{MLP}\big(\text{sinusoid}(t)\big) + E_{\text{class}}[y] \in \R^{d} \tag{228.7} \]

    DiT compared four ways of using \(\mathbf{c}\), all on DiT-XL/2 [Certain]:

    1. In-context: append \(\mathbf{c}\) as two extra tokens, like a [CLS] token. Almost free.
    2. Cross-attention: add a cross-attention layer to each block that attends to \(\mathbf{c}\) (as in the U-Net with text). Costs about 15% more compute.
    3. adaLN: replace the fixed LayerNorm gain and bias by values computed from \(\mathbf{c}\).
    4. adaLN-Zero: adaLN plus a learned gate on each residual branch, initialized to zero.

    Adaptive LayerNorm (adaLN) is the same idea as FiLM: normalize the token, then scale and shift it with numbers that depend on the condition. A small network (SiLU then one linear layer) maps \(\mathbf{c}\) to a shift \(\beta\), a scale \(\gamma\), and a gate \(\alpha\), each of size \(d\), separately for the attention branch and the MLP branch (6 vectors per block):

    \[ \text{adaLN}(\mathbf{h}; \mathbf{c}) = \text{LN}(\mathbf{h}) \odot \big(1 + \gamma(\mathbf{c})\big) + \beta(\mathbf{c}) \tag{228.8} \]
    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{h} &\leftarrow \mathbf{h} + \alpha_1(\mathbf{c}) \odot \text{Attn}\big(\text{adaLN}_1(\mathbf{h}; \mathbf{c})\big) \\ \mathbf{h} &\leftarrow \mathbf{h} + \alpha_2(\mathbf{c}) \odot \text{MLP}\big(\text{adaLN}_2(\mathbf{h}; \mathbf{c})\big) \end{aligned} \tag{228.9} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\text{LN}\)
    LayerNorm without its own learned gain and bias: subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation
    \(\gamma, \beta\)
    per-channel scale and shift, computed from \(\mathbf{c}\); the same for all \(T\) tokens of one image
    \(\alpha\)
    per-channel gate on the residual branch; its producing layer starts at zero
    \(\odot\)
    element-wise multiplication

    Why the zero? With \(\alpha = 0\) at the start, every block outputs its input unchanged: the whole network is the identity function. This trick is well known from ResNets (start each residual branch at zero) and makes large models train stably. DiT also zero-initializes the final output layer, so the first prediction is exactly zero. [Certain] Why adaLN instead of cross-attention? The condition is one vector per image, so attending to it adds little; scaling the normalized features is a cheaper and more direct way to say “we are at a high noise level, act accordingly”.

    Worked example: adaLN on one token

    Take a token \(\mathbf{h} = (1, 2, 3, 4)\). LayerNorm: mean 2.5, variance 1.25, so \(\text{LN}(\mathbf{h}) = (-1.342, -0.447, 0.447, 1.342)\). Suppose the condition gives \(\gamma = 0.5\) and \(\beta = 0.1\) in every channel. Then formula (228.8) gives \(1.5 \cdot \text{LN}(\mathbf{h}) + 0.1 = (-1.912, -0.571, 0.771, 2.112)\). At a different step \(t\), \(\mathbf{c}\) changes, so \(\gamma\) and \(\beta\) change, and the same weights process the token differently. If the gate \(\alpha = 0\), the block output is \(\mathbf{h} = (1, 2, 3, 4)\) again, whatever the attention or MLP computes.

    The output layer

    After the last block, a final adaLN and a linear layer map each token back to \(p \times p \times 2C\) numbers, and unpatchify puts them back into the latent grid. Why \(2C\) and not \(C\)? DiT predicts both the noise \(\varepsilon\) (\(C\) channels) and the variance of each reverse step (another \(C\) channels), as in Improved DDPM (Nichol & Dhariwal). The noise part is trained with the simple loss (228.4); the variance part with the full variational bound. [Certain] The Hugging Face config of DiT-XL-2-256 shows exactly this: in_channels: 4, out_channels: 8, patch_size: 2, num_layers: 28, 16 heads of size 72 (so \(d = 1152\)), and norm_type: ada_norm_zero. [Certain]

    Counting the cost of a DiT

    A DiT is a ViT, so we can count its cost with the same rules as an LLM (session 25). Per block, the attention projections have \(4d^2\) weights and the MLP \(8d^2\); every token uses each weight once. The attention scores and the weighted sum add \(2T^2 d\). DiT reports compute in “Gflops” that match multiply-add counts, so per forward pass:

    \[ \text{Gflops} \approx L \left( 12\, T d^2 + 2\, T^2 d \right) \cdot 10^{-9} \tag{228.10} \]
    \[ \text{params} \approx L \cdot (12 d^2 + 6 d^2) = 18\, L\, d^2 \tag{228.11} \]

    The extra \(6d^2\) per block is the adaLN layer that maps \(\mathbf{c}\) to six vectors. It runs once per image, not per token, so it adds parameters but almost no compute.

    Worked example: DiT-XL/2

    DiT-XL has \(L = 28\), \(d = 1152\); with \(p = 2\), \(T = 256\). Per block: \(12 \cdot 256 \cdot 1152^2 = 4.077 \times 10^9\) for the weights and \(2 \cdot 256^2 \cdot 1152 = 0.151 \times 10^9\) for attention. Times 28 blocks: 118.4 Gflops. The paper reports 118.6 (the difference is the patch embedding, output layer and small terms). Parameters: \(18 \cdot 28 \cdot 1152^2 = 668.9\)M in the blocks; with the embeddings and final layer our count is 675.0M, and the paper says 675M (not counting the 84M VAE). [Certain]

    At 512×512 the latent is 64×64, so \(T = 1024\) and the same model needs 524.6 Gflops (formula 228.10 gives 524.3). The pixel-space U-Net ADM needs 1120 Gflops at 256×256 and 1983 at 512×512. [Certain] Sampling with 250 DDPM steps costs \(250 \times 118.6 \approx 29.7\) Tflops per 256×256 image, and twice that with classifier-free guidance.

    Scaling behavior of DiT

    DiT trained 12 models: four sizes (S, B, L, XL) times three patch sizes (8, 4, 2), all for 400K steps at batch 256 with the same recipe (AdamW, constant learning rate \(10^{-4}\), no weight decay, EMA 0.9999). [Certain]

    Three findings matter [Certain]:

    1. Compute predicts quality. FID falls steadily as Gflops grow, and it does not matter much whether the Gflops come from a bigger model or from smaller patches (more tokens). The paper calls it a strong negative correlation between Gflops and FID.
    2. Parameters alone do not. DiT-XL/8 has about 675M parameters but only 7.39 Gflops, and its FID is 106.41. DiT-S/2 has about 20 times fewer parameters (33M) and 6.06 Gflops, and its FID is 68.40. Smaller patches, which give more tokens per image, matter more than parameters here.
    3. Bigger models use training compute better. At the same total training compute, larger DiTs reach lower FID. And extra sampling steps cannot make up for a small model: “scaling-up sampling compute cannot compensate for a lack of model compute.”
    Reading the fit

    A least-squares line through the 12 points in log-log space has slope \(-0.365\) and correlation \(-0.94\). So, in this range, every 10× more forward compute divides FID by about \(10^{0.365} \approx 2.3\). This is our fit on the paper’s 12 numbers, not a law from the paper, and it holds only for this recipe and training length.

    With long training (7M steps) and classifier-free guidance at scale 1.5, DiT-XL/2 reached FID 2.27 on ImageNet 256×256, better than LDM-4-G (3.60) and pixel-space ADM. At 512×512 it reached 3.04 after 3M steps. [Certain] Guidance trades diversity for quality, so the guided model has higher precision (0.83) and lower recall (0.57) than the unguided one (0.67 and 0.67). [Certain] More on this trade-off in session 227.

    Interview tipIf asked “why did the field move from U-Nets to DiTs?”, give three reasons: (1) quality follows compute in a predictable way, so you can plan big runs like LLM runs; (2) one standard block, so all the transformer tooling (FlashAttention, tensor parallelism, FSDP) works unchanged; (3) tokens make it easy to mix modalities: text tokens, image tokens and video tokens in one sequence.

    After DiT: text, video and joint models

    DiT was class-conditional: the condition was one label. Text needs more than one vector, so later models put cross-attention back in. PixArt-\(\alpha\) adds a cross-attention layer to each DiT block for T5 text features and reports training at about 10.8% of the time of Stable Diffusion v1.5 (675 vs 6,250 A100 GPU-days). [Certain] Stable Diffusion 3 and FLUX go further: text tokens and image tokens are processed in one joint attention over both (MM-DiT), still with adaLN for the timestep, and they train with rectified flow instead of DDPM (session 229). [Likely] U-ViT (Bao et al., 2022) independently showed that a ViT with long skip connections works as a diffusion backbone. [Certain]

    For video, the same recipe extends naturally. A video autoencoder compresses both space and time, and the latent is cut into spacetime patches (for example \(2 \times 2\) in space and a few frames in time), giving one long token sequence. OpenAI described Sora this way: a diffusion transformer on spacetime patches of a compressed video latent. [Likely] Because tokens are just a sequence, the same model can train on clips of different lengths, sizes and aspect ratios.

    A minimal DiT in PyTorch

    The code below builds a small DiT with patchify, adaLN-Zero blocks, the zero-initialized output, and trains it on toy “latents” (one fixed 4×8×8 pattern per class plus noise) with the DDPM loss. Then it samples with DDIM and classifier-free guidance, and checks that each sample is closest to the pattern of its class. In a real model, z0 would be s * vae.encode(x) and the samples would go through vae.decode(z / s). It runs on a CPU in about 15 seconds.

    import math, torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F
torch.manual_seed(0)

def patchify(z, p):                        # (B, C, H, W) -> (B, T, p*p*C), formula (228.6)
    B, C, H, W = z.shape
    z = z.reshape(B, C, H // p, p, W // p, p).permute(0, 2, 4, 3, 5, 1)
    return z.reshape(B, (H // p) * (W // p), p * p * C)

def unpatchify(x, p, C, H, W):             # (B, T, p*p*C) -> (B, C, H, W)
    x = x.reshape(x.shape[0], H // p, W // p, p, p, C).permute(0, 5, 1, 3, 2, 4)
    return x.reshape(x.shape[0], C, H, W)

def t_embed(t, dim=256):                   # sinusoidal timestep features
    half = dim // 2
    freqs = torch.exp(-math.log(10000) * torch.arange(half) / half)
    a = t[:, None].float() * freqs[None]
    return torch.cat([torch.cos(a), torch.sin(a)], dim=-1)

def modulate(x, shift, scale):             # formula (228.8)
    return x * (1 + scale[:, None]) + shift[:, None]

class DiTBlock(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d, heads):
        super().__init__()
        self.n1 = nn.LayerNorm(d, elementwise_affine=False, eps=1e-6)
        self.n2 = nn.LayerNorm(d, elementwise_affine=False, eps=1e-6)
        self.attn = nn.MultiheadAttention(d, heads, batch_first=True)
        self.mlp = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(d, 4 * d), nn.GELU(approximate="tanh"), nn.Linear(4 * d, d))
        self.ada = nn.Sequential(nn.SiLU(), nn.Linear(d, 6 * d))
        nn.init.zeros_(self.ada[1].weight); nn.init.zeros_(self.ada[1].bias)   # adaLN-Zero

    def forward(self, x, c):                # formula (228.9)
        b1, g1, a1, b2, g2, a2 = self.ada(c).chunk(6, dim=-1)
        h = modulate(self.n1(x), b1, g1)
        x = x + a1[:, None] * self.attn(h, h, h, need_weights=False)[0]
        x = x + a2[:, None] * self.mlp(modulate(self.n2(x), b2, g2))
        return x

class TinyDiT(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, I=8, C=4, p=2, d=64, depth=4, heads=4, n_cls=10):
        super().__init__()
        self.I, self.C, self.p = I, C, p
        T = (I // p) ** 2
        self.x_emb = nn.Linear(p * p * C, d)
        self.pos = nn.Parameter(0.02 * torch.randn(1, T, d))
        self.t_mlp = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(256, d), nn.SiLU(), nn.Linear(d, d))
        self.y_emb = nn.Embedding(n_cls + 1, d)              # last id = "no class" (for CFG)
        self.blocks = nn.ModuleList([DiTBlock(d, heads) for _ in range(depth)])
        self.n_out = nn.LayerNorm(d, elementwise_affine=False, eps=1e-6)
        self.ada_out = nn.Sequential(nn.SiLU(), nn.Linear(d, 2 * d))
        self.out = nn.Linear(d, p * p * C)                   # real DiT: p*p*2C (noise + variance)
        for m in (self.ada_out[1], self.out):                # zero init: output starts at 0
            nn.init.zeros_(m.weight); nn.init.zeros_(m.bias)

    def forward(self, z, t, y):
        x = self.x_emb(patchify(z, self.p)) + self.pos       # (B, T, d)
        c = self.t_mlp(t_embed(t)) + self.y_emb(y)           # (B, d), formula (228.7)
        for blk in self.blocks:
            x = blk(x, c)
        b, g = self.ada_out(c).chunk(2, dim=-1)
        x = self.out(modulate(self.n_out(x), b, g))          # (B, T, p*p*C)
        return unpatchify(x, self.p, self.C, self.I, self.I) # predicted noise

# DDPM schedule as in DiT: 1000 steps, linear betas from 1e-4 to 2e-2
betas = torch.linspace(1e-4, 2e-2, 1000)
abar = torch.cumprod(1 - betas, dim=0)

model = TinyDiT()
print("params:", sum(q.numel() for q in model.parameters()))        # 331536
z = torch.randn(2, 4, 8, 8)
print("round trip:", torch.equal(unpatchify(patchify(z, 2), 2, 4, 8, 8), z))  # True
print("output at init:", model(z, torch.tensor([10, 500]), torch.tensor([3, 10])).abs().max().item())  # 0.0

# toy "latents": each of 10 classes is a fixed 4x8x8 pattern plus a little noise
protos = torch.randn(10, 4, 8, 8)
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3, weight_decay=0.0)
for step in range(1001):
    y = torch.randint(0, 10, (64,))
    z0 = protos[y] + 0.1 * torch.randn(64, 4, 8, 8)       # real LDM: z0 = s * vae.encode(x)
    y_in = torch.where(torch.rand(64) < 0.1, torch.full_like(y, 10), y)   # drop the label 10% of the time
    t = torch.randint(0, 1000, (64,))
    eps = torch.randn_like(z0)
    a = abar[t].view(-1, 1, 1, 1)
    zt = a.sqrt() * z0 + (1 - a).sqrt() * eps              # forward noising, in latent space
    loss = F.mse_loss(model(zt, t, y_in), eps)             # formula (228.4)
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    if step % 250 == 0:
        print(step, round(loss.item(), 4))                 # 0.9967 at step 0, below 0.1 by step 1000

# sampling: deterministic DDIM, 50 steps, classifier-free guidance w = 2
@torch.no_grad()
def sample(y, w=2.0, steps=50):
    ts = torch.linspace(999, 0, steps).long()
    zt = torch.randn(len(y), 4, 8, 8)
    for i, t in enumerate(ts):
        tt = t.repeat(len(y))
        e_c = model(zt, tt, y)
        e_u = model(zt, tt, torch.full_like(y, 10))
        e = e_u + w * (e_c - e_u)
        a = abar[t]
        z0_hat = (zt - (1 - a).sqrt() * e) / a.sqrt()
        a_prev = abar[ts[i + 1]] if i + 1 < steps else torch.tensor(1.0)
        zt = a_prev.sqrt() * z0_hat + (1 - a_prev).sqrt() * e
    return zt                                              # real LDM: x = vae.decode(zt / s)

y = torch.arange(10)
zs = sample(y)
dist = ((zs[:, None] - protos[None]) ** 2).mean(dim=(2, 3, 4))   # (10 samples, 10 patterns)
print("nearest pattern:", dist.argmin(1).tolist())             # [0, 1, 2, ..., 9]
print("error to own pattern:", round(dist.diag().mean().item(), 4))  # about 0.018 (data noise alone: 0.01)

    Three checks in the output: the patchify round trip is exact, the untrained model outputs exactly 0 (adaLN-Zero plus the zero output layer), and every one of the 10 samples lands nearest to the pattern of the class it was asked for. The loss falls from 0.9967 at step 0 to below 0.1 by step 1,000 (CPU run, PyTorch 2.x; exact later values can differ slightly on other hardware).

    Common mistakes

    • Forgetting the latent scale \(s\). Training on raw VAE latents (std far from 1) silently changes every noise level of the schedule. Multiply by \(s\) after encoding and divide by \(s\) before decoding, exactly once each.
    • Mixing VAEs. A denoiser trained on one autoencoder’s latents is useless with another autoencoder (for example the 4-channel SD VAE vs a 16-channel VAE), even if the shapes match.
    • Thinking the latent is a nice Gaussian. With KL weight around \(10^{-6}\), the latent is closer to a plain autoencoder code. Interpolating or sampling it directly does not give images; only the diffusion model can.
    • Choosing \(f\) too large. Details lost by the encoder (small text, faces far away) cannot be recovered by the denoiser. When a generator draws bad small text, check the autoencoder’s reconstruction first.
    • Comparing DiTs by parameter count. Patch size changes compute 4× per halving with almost no change in parameters; compare by Gflops (formula 228.10).
    • Using a normal random init for the adaLN layers. Without the zero init, deep DiTs start far from the identity and train worse; the paper’s FID gap between adaLN and adaLN-Zero is 25.21 vs 19.47.
    • Wrong patch order. Patchify and unpatchify must be exact inverses. A mismatched reshape still trains, but the model must learn to undo the scramble, and position embeddings no longer match the content.
    • Running the VAE in FP16 when it overflows. Some VAEs (the SDXL VAE config sets force_upcast: true) produce NaNs in half precision; decode in FP32 or use a fixed VAE. [Likely]

    Interview questions

    Why do latent diffusion models diffuse in a latent space instead of pixels?basic
    Cost and focus. With \(f = 8\), \(c = 4\) the denoiser works on 48 times fewer values, and it runs at every sampling step, so the saving is paid back many times. And most pixel information is perceptual detail that a pretrained autoencoder (trained with perceptual and adversarial losses) can handle once; the diffusion model can then spend its capacity on semantic structure. LDM showed that \(f = 4\) to 8 gives the best trade-off; \(f = 32\) loses too much detail.
    Walk me through the shapes in Stable Diffusion v1 for a 512×512 image.basic
    Image \(3 \times 512 \times 512\) → VAE encoder (\(f = 8\)) → latent \(4 \times 64 \times 64\), multiplied by 0.18215. Prompt → CLIP ViT-L/14 text encoder → \(77 \times 768\). The U-Net takes the noisy latent \(4 \times 64 \times 64\), the step and the text (via cross-attention), and outputs predicted noise of the same shape. After sampling, divide by 0.18215 and decode back to \(3 \times 512 \times 512\).
    What does the scale factor 0.18215 do, and what happens if you forget it?core
    It rescales the latent to roughly unit standard deviation, which the noise schedule assumes. Without it, the latent has a larger spread, so at every step the signal-to-noise ratio is much higher than planned: the “fully noised” \(\mathbf{z}_T\) still contains signal, and the start of sampling from pure \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\) does not match training. Results are washed out or broken. If you forget to divide by it before decoding, the decoder gets latents about 5.5 times too small.
    How does DiT turn a latent into tokens, and what does the patch size control?core
    It cuts the \(I \times I \times C\) latent into \(p \times p\) patches, flattens each to \(p^2 C\) numbers and projects to width \(d\), then adds fixed sine-cosine position embeddings. \(T = (I/p)^2\): 256 tokens for \(I = 32\), \(p = 2\). Halving \(p\) gives 4× the tokens and at least 4× the compute while parameters stay almost the same, so \(p\) is a compute knob; smaller patches gave much better FID.
    Explain adaLN-Zero. Why is it better than cross-attention for class conditioning in DiT?core
    The timestep and class embeddings are summed into one vector \(\mathbf{c}\). A linear layer maps \(\mathbf{c}\) to shift, scale and gate vectors for each block. LayerNorm without its own affine parameters is followed by \(\cdot(1 + \gamma) + \beta\), and each residual branch output is multiplied by the gate \(\alpha\). The layer producing these is initialized to zero, so every block starts as the identity. Cross-attention to a single vector adds about 15% compute and brings little; adaLN-Zero is the cheapest and gave the best FID (19.47 vs 26.14 for cross-attention, DiT-XL/2 at 400K steps).
    What is the scaling result of the DiT paper?core
    Across 12 models (S, B, L, XL × patch 8, 4, 2), FID is strongly predicted by forward Gflops, whether compute comes from depth and width or from more tokens. Parameters alone are a poor predictor: XL/8 (675M params, 7.4 Gflops) has FID 106, worse than S/2 (33M, 6.1 Gflops) at 68. Larger models are more training-compute-efficient, and more sampling steps cannot make up for a smaller model.
    Estimate the forward cost of DiT-XL/2 from its config.advanced
    \(L = 28\), \(d = 1152\), \(T = 256\). Per block: weights \(12 T d^2 \approx 4.08\)G multiply-adds, attention \(2T^2 d \approx 0.15\)G. Total \(28 \times 4.23 \approx 118.4\) Gflops vs 118.6 reported. Parameters \(\approx 18 L d^2 = 669\)M plus embeddings ≈ 675M. At 512×512, \(T = 1024\) and the attention term grows 16×, giving about 524 Gflops.
    Why does DiT output 2C channels instead of C?advanced
    It predicts the noise (C channels) and also a per-element variance for the reverse step (C channels), following Improved DDPM. The noise part is trained with the simple MSE; the variance part with the variational lower bound. Learned variances let DDPM sampling use fewer steps with good quality. Models trained with flow matching or used only with DDIM-style samplers usually drop the variance output.
    U-Net vs DiT: what are the trade-offs?core
    The U-Net has image priors (locality, multi-scale, skips), which help with little data and small compute, and its cost grows roughly linearly with pixels. DiT has few priors; global attention costs \(O(T^2)\) and it needs more data and compute, but it scales predictably, reuses all transformer infrastructure, and handles text and video tokens in the same sequence. At large scale DiTs win; that is why SD3, FLUX and video models use them.
    How would you condition a DiT on a text prompt instead of a class?advanced
    A prompt is a sequence of vectors, not one vector. Options: (1) add cross-attention to each block, with image tokens as queries and text tokens as keys and values (PixArt-\(\alpha\)); (2) concatenate text and image tokens and use joint self-attention with separate weights per modality (MM-DiT in SD3 and FLUX). Keep adaLN for the timestep and often a pooled text vector. For classifier-free guidance, drop the text (use an empty prompt) for about 10% of training examples.
    Your latent diffusion model draws blurry small text and faces. Where do you look first?advanced
    At the autoencoder: encode and decode real images and inspect the reconstructions. If small text is already broken there, no denoiser can fix it; you need a better autoencoder (more channels, e.g. 16, or smaller \(f\)), or a fine-tuned decoder. If reconstructions are fine, look at the denoiser: resolution of training data, guidance scale, number of steps, model size.

    Key takeaways

    • Latent diffusion splits generation into perceptual compression (an autoencoder, trained once) and semantic generation (diffusion on the small latent), formula (228.1). Stable Diffusion: \(f = 8\), \(c = 4\), 48× fewer values (228.2).
    • The diffusion math is unchanged; only the data is \(\mathbf{z}_0 = s\,\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x})\) (228.4). The scale \(s\) (0.18215 for SD 1.x) keeps the noise schedule valid.
    • The U-Net backbone uses convolutions, skips and cross-attention (228.5); DiT replaces it with a plain ViT on latent patches (228.6).
    • DiT conditions through adaLN-Zero (228.8, 228.9): scale, shift and gate computed from the timestep and class; zero init makes every block start as the identity.
    • DiT quality follows forward compute (228.10), not parameter count; smaller patches and bigger models both help, and bigger models use training compute better. DiT-XL/2: 675M params, 118.6 Gflops, FID 2.27 with guidance.

    Key formulas

    \[ \frac{3f^2}{c}, \qquad \mathcal{L} = \lVert \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t, t, y) \rVert^2, \qquad T = \Big(\frac{I}{p}\Big)^2, \qquad \text{LN}(\mathbf{h})(1 + \gamma) + \beta, \qquad L(12Td^2 + 2T^2 d) \]
