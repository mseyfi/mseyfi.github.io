    Training recipe and stability

    A large training run is a long chain of small risks. This session explains the recipe that makes runs work: how to initialize the weights so signals keep their size, how to shape the learning rate over time, what causes loss spikes and how to stop them, and how μP lets you tune settings on a small model and reuse them on a big one.

    Time: 3 to 4 hours (reading 2 hours, exercises 1 to 2 hours)Level: IntermediateBefore this: Sessions 4 and 22 (optimization and AdamW, the residual stream and normalization); Session 35 (scaling laws) helps

    What you will learn

    • Why initialization variance must depend on width, and why the popular std 0.02 is only right for one width
    • How scaled residual initialization keeps the residual stream from growing with depth
    • Warmup, cosine, warmup-stable-decay and multi-step schedules, and why decaying the learning rate lowers the loss
    • The main causes of loss spikes: Adam after a quiet period, growing attention logits, drifting output logits, bad batches
    • The fixes and when each one helps: gradient clipping, lower β₂ and learning rate, z-loss, QK-norm, restart and skip
    • μP: why the best learning rate moves with width in standard practice, the μP scaling rules, and the μTransfer workflow
    • The real recipes of Llama 3, OLMo 2, PaLM and DeepSeek LLM, with their numbers

    Why this matters

    A pretraining run of a large model costs millions of dollars and runs for weeks or months. Most of the time, it does not fail because the architecture is wrong. It fails because the loss suddenly jumps up (a loss spike), turns into NaN, or slowly stops improving. The training recipe is the set of choices that prevents this: how the weights start, how the learning rate changes over time, and what guards are in place when something goes wrong.

    In session 4 you met AdamW, warmup, cosine decay and gradient clipping. In session 22 you saw Pre-LN, QK-norm and z-loss as architecture choices. In session 35 you learned how to choose model size and token count. This session puts these pieces together into one recipe and asks three practical questions:

    1. How should the weights start? If the initial weights are too large or too small, signals explode or vanish before training even begins.
    2. How should the learning rate change over time? The schedule decides both the speed of progress and the final loss.
    3. How do we keep a long run stable, and how do we pick the learning rate for a model we can afford to train only once? Here we meet loss spikes and their fixes, and μP, a way to tune on a small model and copy the settings to a big one.

    The problem is real even for the best teams. For PaLM 540B, Google reports that the loss spiked “roughly 20 times during training, despite the fact that gradient clipping was enabled”, and the spikes did not happen in the smaller models. [Certain] Their fix was simple and expensive: restart from a checkpoint about 100 steps before the spike and skip about 200 to 500 data batches. [Certain] Two years later, the Llama 3 team wrote that their 405B recipe “observed few loss spikes and did not require interventions”. [Certain] The difference between these two stories is mostly the recipe.

    Interview tipCommon questions: “Your loss spiked at step 80,000. What do you do?”, “Why is std 0.02 used for initialization?”, “What is z-loss?”, “Why does QK-norm help?”, “What is μP and why would you use it?”, “Cosine or WSD?”. Each is answered below with numbers you can check.

    Initialization: keep signals the right size

    Training starts from random weights. The question is: random how large? Think of one linear layer \(\mathbf{y} = W\mathbf{x}\) with input size \(n\) (the fan-in). Each output is a sum of \(n\) products \(W_{ij} x_j\). If the weights are independent with mean 0 and variance \(\sigma^2\), the variances add up:

    \[ \operatorname{Var}(y_i) = n\,\sigma^2 \operatorname{Var}(x_j) \tag{36.1} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(n\)
    fan-in: the number of inputs of the layer (the width \(d\) for most transformer matrices)
    \(\sigma\)
    the standard deviation used to draw each weight at the start
    \(\operatorname{Var}(x_j)\)
    the variance of one input coordinate

    So each layer multiplies the variance by \(n\sigma^2\), and the standard deviation (std) by \(\sigma\sqrt{n}\). A ReLU sets half of the values to zero and halves the variance, so for a ReLU layer the std factor is \(\sigma\sqrt{n/2}\). After \(L\) layers without residual connections or normalization:

    \[ \operatorname{std}(\mathbf{x}_L) = \operatorname{std}(\mathbf{x}_0)\,\big(c\,\sigma\sqrt{n}\big)^{L}, \qquad c = 1 \text{ (linear)},\ \ c = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt 2} \text{ (ReLU)} \tag{36.2} \]

    A factor even a little above 1 explodes; a little below 1 vanishes. The classic fix is to choose \(\sigma\) so that the factor is exactly 1: \(\sigma = 1/\sqrt{n}\) for linear or tanh layers (LeCun or Xavier style) and \(\sigma = \sqrt{2/n}\) for ReLU (He initialization). Both are “fan-in” rules: the std shrinks as the layer gets wider.

    AnalogyA chain of loudspeakers, each one feeding the next. If each speaker amplifies by 1.3, after 32 speakers the sound is deafening; at 0.7, it is silent. Initialization sets the volume knob of every speaker before the music starts.

    Why the popular std 0.02 depends on width

    GPT-2, Megatron-LM, nanoGPT and OLMo 2 draw most weights from a normal distribution with std 0.02. [Certain for GPT-2 code and OLMo 2] This is a fixed number, not a fan-in rule. Formula (36.1) tells us when it is “right”: \(n \sigma^2 = 1\) means \(n = 1/0.02^2 = 2{,}500\). For a width of 768 (GPT-2 small) the variance factor per layer is 0.31; for 8,192 it is 3.28.

    Worked example: std 0.02 at different widths

    Variance factor per layer \(n\sigma^2\) with \(\sigma = 0.02\): width 768 gives \(768 \times 0.0004 = 0.307\); width 4,096 gives 1.638; width 8,192 gives 3.277. The std factors are \(\sqrt{\cdot}\): 0.554, 1.280 and 1.810. In a plain stack of 32 such layers, the std would be multiplied by \(0.554^{32} \approx 6 \times 10^{-9}\) at width 768 and by \(1.81^{32} \approx 1.8 \times 10^{8}\) at width 8,192. The fan-in rule \(1/\sqrt{n}\) would use 0.036 and 0.011 instead.

    So why does std 0.02 work in practice? Because a transformer is not a plain stack. Every sub-layer reads a normalized copy of the residual stream (Pre-LN), so the input to each layer has size about 1 no matter what came before; and the residual connection keeps the signal alive even if a sub-layer’s output is small. The normalization layers hide most of the width effect at initialization. But they do not hide it during training: the updates still depend on width, and that is the problem μP solves at the end of this session.

    Scaled residual initialization

    The residual stream is a sum. Each of the \(L\) blocks adds two edits (attention and MLP), so after \(L\) blocks there are \(2L\) edits. If the edits are roughly independent at initialization, their variances add:

    \[ \operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{h}_L) \approx \operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{h}_0) + \sum_{l=1}^{2L} \operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{f}_l), \qquad \sigma_{\text{out}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{2L}} \tag{36.3} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\mathbf{h}_0, \mathbf{h}_L\)
    the residual stream after the embedding, and after the last block
    \(\mathbf{f}_l\)
    the edit that sub-layer \(l\) adds to the stream
    \(\sigma_{\text{out}}\)
    the init std of the output projection of each sub-layer (attention output and MLP down-projection)

    If every edit has the same variance \(v\), the stream variance grows to \(\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{h}_0) + 2Lv\): it grows linearly with depth. GPT-2 fixes this by scaling the weights of the residual layers at initialization by \(1/\sqrt{N}\), where \(N\) is the number of residual layers. [Certain] Since variance scales with the square of std, each edit then has variance proportional to \(1/(2L)\), and their sum stays the same for any depth. nanoGPT uses \(0.02/\sqrt{2L}\) for these projections: 0.0041 for 12 layers and 0.0025 for 32 layers. [Likely] OLMo 2 found that plain std 0.02 for all weights gave fewer gradient spikes than its earlier scaled scheme. [Certain] So this is a choice to test, not a law.

    Two more details matter. Embeddings feed the stream directly, so their size sets \(\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{h}_0)\); PaLM used std 1 for the input embeddings and scaled the output logits by \(1/\sqrt{d}\), because it ties the input and output embeddings. [Certain] And zero-initialized output projections (used in some recipes) make every block start as the identity function; the model then grows its edits during training. [Likely]

    Learning-rate schedules, revisited

    You know the shapes from session 4: linear warmup (formula 4.11), then cosine decay (formula 4.12). Here we add two newer shapes and, more importantly, explain why decay lowers the loss.

    Warmup-stable-decay and multi-step

    Cosine needs the total number of steps \(T\) in advance. If you later want to train longer, the learning rate has already gone down, and you cannot simply continue. Warmup-stable-decay (WSD), introduced with MiniCPM, keeps the learning rate at its peak for most of training, then decays it over a short final phase. [Certain] With warmup length \(W\) and decay start \(S\):

    \[ \eta(t) = \begin{cases} \eta_{\max}\, t / W, & t \lt W \\ \eta_{\max}, & W \le t \lt S \\ \eta_{\max}\, \big(1 - \frac{t - S}{T - S}\big), & S \le t \le T \end{cases} \tag{36.4} \]

    The decay here is linear to 0; other decay shapes (exponential, square root, cosine) are also used. [Likely] The big advantage: you can save a checkpoint anywhere in the stable phase, and later either continue training from it or run a short decay to get a finished model. One long run can then produce finished models for several token budgets, which is exactly what you want for the scaling-law fits in session 35. DeepSeek LLM used a related multi-step schedule: after 2,000 warmup steps, the learning rate drops to 31.6% of the peak after 80% of the tokens, and to 10% after 90%. [Certain] They report that the final performance “is essentially consistent with that of a cosine scheduler”, and that the first phase can be reused when they train longer. [Certain] (31.6% is about \(1/\sqrt{10}\).)

    Why decaying the learning rate lowers the loss

    Stochastic gradients are noisy. Even at the bottom of the valley, each step jumps by a random amount, so the weights bounce around the minimum instead of sitting on it. The size of this bouncing depends on the learning rate. For one direction with curvature \(\lambda\) and gradient noise with std \(\sigma\), plain SGD settles into a cloud whose average loss is exactly

    \[ \E[\text{loss}] = \frac{\eta\,\sigma^2}{2\,(2 - \eta\lambda)} \approx \frac{\eta\,\sigma^2}{4} \quad \text{when } \eta\lambda \ll 1 \tag{36.5} \]

    This is the noise floor. It is proportional to \(\eta\): halve the learning rate, and the floor halves. A high learning rate makes fast progress along the flat directions but sits on a high floor; a low one sits on a low floor but makes slow progress. A schedule gets the best of both: high early (progress), low at the end (floor). This also explains the strange shape of WSD loss curves: the loss is worse than cosine during the stable phase, then drops sharply during the decay. The drop is the floor going down. Figure 2 shows this on a toy problem.

    How long should warmup be?

    Warmup protects the first steps, when Adam’s second-moment estimate is based on very few gradients and the weights are far from any good region. Real runs use a small fraction of the total: Llama 2 used 2,000 of about 500,000 steps (0.4%), and Llama 3 405B used 8,000 of 1,200,000 (0.67%). [Certain] Longer warmup is a common first fix for early instability, and Wortsman et al. found that warmup makes small models less sensitive to the choice of learning rate. [Likely]

    Loss spikes: what they are and where they come from

    A loss spike is a sudden jump of the training loss, often by a large amount, within a few steps. Sometimes the loss comes back down by itself in a few hundred steps; sometimes it never fully recovers; sometimes it becomes NaN. Spikes become more common in larger models, at higher learning rates, and late in training. The main known causes are these:

    1. Adam after a quiet period. If the gradients of some weights are tiny for a long time, Adam’s second moment \(\mathbf{v}\) becomes tiny too. When a large gradient then arrives, the update \(\hat m / \sqrt{\hat v}\) is suddenly large. Molybog et al. argue that, in large runs, Adam can enter a state where the update has a large norm and is almost uncorrelated with the descent direction, which leads to divergence. [Certain]
    2. Growing attention logits. As \(W_Q\) and \(W_K\) grow during training, the attention scores grow with the square of their size; softmax becomes almost one-hot, its gradient vanishes, and training becomes unstable. This was the problem in ViT-22B, fixed with QK-norm. [Certain]
    3. Drifting output logits. Cross-entropy does not care if all output logits shift by the same amount, so they can drift to very large values, where low-precision numbers lose accuracy. [Likely] z-loss fixes this.
    4. Data. Some batches are unusual (long runs of repeated tokens, for example). OLMo 2 removed documents with 32 or more repeated n-grams and masked such sequences in the loss, because they caused gradient spikes. [Certain] But PaLM found that the same batches did not cause a spike when trained from an earlier checkpoint: spikes come from a bad batch and a sensitive model state together. [Certain]
    AnalogyA driver who goes fast on a smooth highway for hours slowly relaxes their grip. Then a single pothole sends the car off the road. The pothole (an odd batch) is not dangerous by itself; the relaxed grip (a fragile model state, a tiny Adam \(\mathbf{v}\), very large logits) is what makes it dangerous.

    Cause 1 in numbers: Adam after a quiet period

    Suppose a weight sees a gradient of 0.01 for thousands of steps. Then \(m \approx 0.01\) and \(v \approx 0.01^2\), and each update is about \(\eta\) (Adam normalizes). Now a burst arrives: the gradient jumps to \(g_1 \gg 0.01\). On the first step of the burst, \(m \approx (1-\beta_1) g_1\) and \(v \approx (1-\beta_2) g_1^2\), so

    \[ \frac{|\Delta\theta|}{\eta} = \frac{|\hat m|}{\sqrt{\hat v}} \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{1 - \beta_1}{\sqrt{1 - \beta_2}} \quad \text{as } g_1 / g_0 \to \infty \tag{36.6} \]

    With \(\beta_1 = 0.9\): the limit is 3.16 for \(\beta_2 = 0.999\), 1.00 for \(\beta_2 = 0.99\), and 0.45 for \(\beta_2 = 0.95\). If the burst lasts several steps, \(m\) catches up with the new gradient faster than \(v\) does (because \(\beta_1\) forgets faster than \(\beta_2\)), and the steps get even bigger. This is one clear reason why large runs use \(\beta_2 = 0.95\) instead of the default 0.999. Note what formula (36.6) does not contain: the size of the gradient. Adam divides by the gradient’s own size, so global-norm clipping, which only rescales the gradient, helps less than you might expect.

    Two related details. First, the Adam \(\varepsilon\) matters when gradients are tiny: if \(\sqrt{\hat v}\) falls near \(\varepsilon\), the update shrinks. Wortsman et al. report that a too-large \(\varepsilon\) can make updates too small in large models, and OLMo 2 lowered \(\varepsilon\) from \(10^{-5}\) to \(10^{-8}\). [Certain for OLMo 2] Second, PaLM chose \(\beta_2 = 1 - k^{-0.8}\) (growing toward 1 with the step \(k\)) because “rare embedding tokens can have poorly estimated second moments over shorter windows”. [Certain] Rare tokens are exactly the “quiet” weights of formula (36.6).

    Causes 2 and 3: logits that grow

    Output logits. The cross-entropy loss only depends on differences between logits, so the model can add the same number to all logits without changing the loss. Nothing stops this drift. But numbers stored in BF16 have only 8 significant bits, so large values are coarse: between 256 and 512, BF16 can only represent every second integer, and between 512 and 1,024 only every fourth. Large logits lose the small differences that carry the prediction. z-loss adds a small penalty that keeps the log of the softmax normalizer near 0:

    \[ \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}} + \alpha \,(\log Z)^2, \qquad Z = \sum_{j=1}^{|V|} e^{z_j} \tag{36.7} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(z_j\)
    the output logit of vocabulary entry \(j\)
    \(Z\)
    the softmax normalizer; \(\log Z\) is the log-sum-exp of the logits
    \(\alpha\)
    a small weight; PaLM used \(10^{-4}\) [Certain]

    Its gradient is easy to read. Since \(\partial \log Z / \partial z_i = p_i\) (the softmax probability):

    \[ \frac{\partial}{\partial z_i}\, \alpha (\log Z)^2 = 2\alpha \,\log Z \; p_i \tag{36.8} \]

    When \(\log Z \gt 0\), every logit is pushed down, the likely ones most; when \(\log Z \lt 0\), they are pushed up. The cross-entropy gradient \(p_i - y_i\) sums to zero over the vocabulary, so it can never move the average logit; the z-loss gradient sums to \(2\alpha \log Z\), so it moves exactly the part that cross-entropy ignores.

    Attention logits. The score between query \(\mathbf{q}_i\) and key \(\mathbf{k}_j\) is \(\mathbf{q}_i \cdot \mathbf{k}_j / \sqrt{d_h}\). If \(W_Q\) and \(W_K\) both grow by a factor \(g\), the scores grow by \(g^2\) (session 22). QK-norm applies RMSNorm to each head’s query and key before the dot product:

    \[ s_{ij} = \frac{(\mathbf{g}_q \odot \hat{\mathbf{q}}_i) \cdot (\mathbf{g}_k \odot \hat{\mathbf{k}}_j)}{\sqrt{d_h}}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{q}} = \frac{\mathbf{q}}{\operatorname{rms}(\mathbf{q})},\ \ \hat{\mathbf{k}} = \frac{\mathbf{k}}{\operatorname{rms}(\mathbf{k})} \tag{36.9} \]

    Now the scores depend only on the directions of \(\mathbf{q}\) and \(\mathbf{k}\) and on the learned gain vectors \(\mathbf{g}_q, \mathbf{g}_k\), which grow slowly. The growth of \(W_Q, W_K\) no longer reaches the softmax. QK-norm was key for ViT-22B [Certain] and is used in OLMo 2 [Certain] and Qwen3 [Certain]. Wortsman et al. showed that attention-logit growth and output-logit drift both appear in small models trained at high learning rates, and that QK-layernorm and z-loss fix them there too, so you can study these problems cheaply. [Certain]

    Algorithm 1: what to do when the loss spikes in a large run
    1. watch every step: loss, pre-clip gradient norm, max attention logit per layer, mean \(\log Z\), update-to-weight ratio per layer
    2. if the loss jumps and does not return within a few hundred steps, or becomes NaN:
    3.   find the first step where the gradient norm or a logit statistic started to rise // often well before the loss
    4.   reload the checkpoint from before that point (PaLM: about 100 steps before the spike)
    5.   skip the batches around the spike (PaLM: about 200 to 500) // needs a loader where batch = f(step)
    6.   if it spikes again: lower the learning rate (for example by 10 to 30%) or lower \(\beta_2\) from here on
    7.   if attention logits grow: QK-norm (next run) or soft-capping; if \(\log Z\) drifts: add z-loss
    8. record every intervention in the run log, so the result can be reproduced

    μP: tune small, train big

    The biggest model in a project is usually trained once. You cannot sweep its learning rate. The usual hope is: sweep on a small model and reuse the best value. In the standard parameterization (SP, what PyTorch does by default) this does not work, because the best learning rate moves as the model gets wider. Figure 1 of the μP paper shows this for transformers trained with Adam: in standard practice the optimum shifts with width, while in μP it stays in place. [Certain] You can see the same effect in the small experiment at the end of the code section: in SP the best Adam learning rate falls from \(2^{-7}\) at width 64 to \(2^{-12}\) at width 1,024.

    Why the best learning rate moves

    Take one hidden layer \(\mathbf{h} = W\mathbf{x}\) with width \(n\), entries of \(\mathbf{x}\) of size about 1, and \(W\) initialized with variance \(1/n\), so the entries of \(\mathbf{h}\) also have size about 1. Look at the very first Adam step. With bias correction, the first update of every weight is exactly \(-\eta \operatorname{sign}(g)\). The gradient is \(\partial L / \partial W = \delta\, \mathbf{x}^\top\), where the column vector \(\delta = \partial L/\partial \mathbf{h}\) comes from above (from formula 4.4), so \(\operatorname{sign}(g_{ij}) = \operatorname{sign}(\delta_i)\operatorname{sign}(x_j)\). For the same input, the change of \(\mathbf{h}\) is

    \[ \Delta h_i = -\eta \operatorname{sign}(\delta_i) \sum_{j=1}^{n} |x_j|, \qquad \frac{|\Delta h_i|}{\operatorname{rms}(\mathbf{h})} \approx \eta\, n \sqrt{2/\pi} \approx 0.8\, \eta\, n \tag{36.10} \]

    (For a standard normal \(x_j\), the average of \(|x_j|\) is \(\sqrt{2/\pi} = 0.798\).) Every one of the \(n\) terms pushes \(h_i\) in the same direction, because the update is built from \(\mathbf{x}\) itself. So the change grows linearly with width, while \(\mathbf{h}\) itself stays the same size. A learning rate that changes \(\mathbf{h}\) by 20% at width 256 changes it by 1,300% at width 16,384. To keep the relative change the same, the learning rate of hidden matrices must scale like \(1/n\). That is the core of μP for Adam.

    The μP rules

    The μP paper (Tensor Programs V) gives the full rules for every type of parameter. Here is its Table 3 for Adam, with fan-in \(n\). [Certain] Parameters are grouped by how many of their dimensions grow with width: input weights (embeddings, the first layer; only the output side grows), hidden weights (both sides grow), and output weights (the unembedding; only the input side grows).

    Input weights and all biasesHidden weightsOutput weights
    Init variance, μP\(1/n\)\(1/n\)\(1/n^2\) (SP: \(1/n\))
    Adam learning rate, μP\(\eta\) (no scaling)\(\eta/n\) (SP: \(\eta\))\(\eta/n\) (SP: \(\eta\))
    SGD learning rate, μP\(\eta \cdot n_{\text{out}}\) (SP: \(\eta\))\(\eta\)\(\eta/n\) (SP: \(\eta\))

    For transformers there is one more change: the attention score uses \(1/d_h\) instead of \(1/\sqrt{d_h}\). [Certain] In practice, the rules are written relative to a base width \(n_0\) (the proxy model), so at the base width μP and SP agree in learning rates:

    \[ \eta_{\text{hidden}}(n) = \eta_{\text{base}} \cdot \frac{n_0}{n}, \qquad \sigma^2_{\text{out}}(n) \propto \frac{1}{n^2}, \qquad s_{ij} = \frac{\mathbf{q}_i \cdot \mathbf{k}_j}{d_h} \tag{36.11} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(n_0\)
    the width of the small proxy model on which you tuned (the base width)
    \(\eta_{\text{base}}\)
    the best learning rate found by the sweep on the proxy
    \(\sigma^2_{\text{out}}\)
    the init variance of the output (unembedding) layer; the paper also gives an equivalent version with a \(1/n\) multiplier on the output logits (its Table 8), which works with tied embeddings
    AnalogyA recipe for one cake does not work for a wedding cake if you just multiply the oven temperature. You must scale each part by the right rule: flour by the number of guests, baking time by the thickness. μP is the list of scaling rules that keeps a model “cooked the same way” at every width.
    Algorithm 2: μTransfer, tune on a proxy and copy to the target
    1. write the target model in μP (formula 36.11 and the table), with a base width \(n_0\)
    2. build a proxy: the same model with width \(n_0\) (often a few hundred), same depth or shallower, trained on fewer tokens
    3. for each candidate setting (learning rate, init scale, output multiplier, warmup, …): train the proxy and record the loss
    4. pick the best setting; check that it is stable at 2 or 3 widths between \(n_0\) and the target // the optimum should not move
    5. train the target with the same settings; μP rescales the hidden and output learning rates by \(n_0/n\) automatically

    What transfers and what does not. The paper lists learning rate, initialization scale, and parameter multipliers as transferable across width; across depth, batch size, training time and sequence length, transfer was checked only empirically on transformers. Regularization settings such as dropout and weight decay do not transfer. [Certain] The headline result: by transferring from a 40M-parameter proxy, they beat the published numbers of the 6.7B GPT-3 model, with a tuning cost of only 7% of the total pretraining cost. [Certain] Cerebras-GPT and several later model families report using μP; many large labs do not say whether they use it. [Likely]

    Watch outμP is about width. It does not by itself make depth transfer work, and it does not tell you how many tokens to train on (that is session 35). Also, a μP implementation is easy to get subtly wrong: a single layer with SP scaling breaks the transfer. The standard test is a “coordinate check”: train a few steps at several widths and check that activation sizes do not grow with width.

    The recipe in code

    The first program builds a small GPT-style model with the stable recipe: std 0.02 init with scaled residual projections (formula 36.3), QK-norm (36.9), a loss with z-loss (36.7), AdamW with \(\beta_2 = 0.95\), no decay on 1-D parameters and embeddings, and clipping. It then reproduces the Adam burst of Figure 3 with torch.optim.Adam. It needs PyTorch 2.4 or newer (for nn.RMSNorm).

    import math, torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F
torch.manual_seed(0)

# 1) Initialization: std 0.02 everywhere, residual output projections scaled by 1/sqrt(2L)
class Block(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d, n_head, qk_norm=True):
        super().__init__()
        self.h, self.dh = n_head, d // n_head
        self.n1, self.n2 = nn.RMSNorm(d), nn.RMSNorm(d)
        self.qkv, self.proj = nn.Linear(d, 3 * d, bias=False), nn.Linear(d, d, bias=False)
        self.up, self.down = nn.Linear(d, 4 * d, bias=False), nn.Linear(4 * d, d, bias=False)
        self.qn = nn.RMSNorm(self.dh) if qk_norm else nn.Identity()   # QK-norm, formula (36.9)
        self.kn = nn.RMSNorm(self.dh) if qk_norm else nn.Identity()
    def forward(self, x):
        B, T, d = x.shape
        q, k, v = self.qkv(self.n1(x)).view(B, T, 3, self.h, self.dh).unbind(2)
        q, k = self.qn(q), self.kn(k)                       # normalize each head's q and k
        a = F.scaled_dot_product_attention(q.transpose(1, 2), k.transpose(1, 2), v.transpose(1, 2), is_causal=True)
        x = x + self.proj(a.transpose(1, 2).reshape(B, T, d))
        return x + self.down(F.gelu(self.up(self.n2(x))))

class TinyLM(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, vocab=1000, d=128, n_head=4, L=4):
        super().__init__()
        self.emb = nn.Embedding(vocab, d)
        self.blocks = nn.ModuleList([Block(d, n_head) for _ in range(L)])
        self.norm, self.head = nn.RMSNorm(d), nn.Linear(d, vocab, bias=False)
        for name, p in self.named_parameters():             # formulas (36.1) and (36.3)
            if p.ndim == 2:
                std = 0.02 / math.sqrt(2 * L) if name.endswith(("proj.weight", "down.weight")) else 0.02
                nn.init.normal_(p, mean=0.0, std=std)
    def forward(self, idx):
        x = self.emb(idx)
        for b in self.blocks: x = b(x)
        return self.head(self.norm(x))

# 2) Loss with z-loss, formula (36.7); computed in FP32
def lm_loss(logits, targets, z_alpha=1e-4):
    logits = logits.float()
    logz = torch.logsumexp(logits, dim=-1)                  # log Z for every position
    ce = (logz - logits.gather(-1, targets[..., None]).squeeze(-1)).mean()
    return ce + z_alpha * (logz ** 2).mean(), ce, logz.mean()

model = TinyLM()
idx = torch.randint(0, 1000, (8, 33))
logits = model(idx[:, :-1])
loss, ce, logz = lm_loss(logits, idx[:, 1:])
print(f"CE {ce.item():.3f} (ln 1000 = {math.log(1000):.3f}), mean log Z {logz.item():.3f}")
print("check: CE equals F.cross_entropy:", torch.allclose(ce, F.cross_entropy(logits.reshape(-1, 1000), idx[:, 1:].reshape(-1))))

# 3) One step of the stable recipe: AdamW (beta2 0.95), no decay on 1-D params and embeddings, clipping at 1.0
decay   = [p for n, p in model.named_parameters() if p.ndim == 2 and "emb" not in n]
nodecay = [p for n, p in model.named_parameters() if p.ndim < 2 or "emb" in n]
opt = torch.optim.AdamW([{"params": decay, "weight_decay": 0.1}, {"params": nodecay, "weight_decay": 0.0}],
                        lr=3e-4, betas=(0.9, 0.95), eps=1e-8)
opt.zero_grad(set_to_none=True); loss.backward()
gnorm = torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)
opt.step()
print(f"grad norm before clipping {gnorm.item():.3f}")

# 4) The Adam burst toy of Figure 3: 5000 quiet steps (g = 0.01), then 10 steps with g = 1
for b2 in (0.95, 0.999):
    w = torch.zeros(1, requires_grad=True)
    o = torch.optim.Adam([w], lr=1.0, betas=(0.9, b2), eps=1e-8)
    sizes = []
    for t in range(5050):
        w.grad = torch.tensor([1.0 if 5000 <= t < 5010 else 0.01])
        before = w.item(); o.step(); sizes.append(abs(w.item() - before))
    print(f"beta2 = {b2}: largest update during the burst = {max(sizes[5000:]):.2f} x lr")

    Output with PyTorch 2.8 on CPU:

    CE 6.935 (ln 1000 = 6.908), mean log Z 6.929

    The initial cross-entropy is close to \(\ln 1000 = 6.908\), which is what a well-initialized model should give: with small weights, the output is almost uniform over the 1,000 tokens. But \(\log Z \approx 6.93\), far from 0, so the z-loss starts working from step one. The last two lines match Figure 3 (1.03 and 6.51).

    The second program is a small μP experiment: a 3-layer MLP trained with Adam on a fixed random regression task, at widths 64, 256 and 1,024, for 10 learning rates. In μP mode, the output layer is initialized with variance \(1/n^2\) and the hidden and output learning rates are divided by \(n / 64\) (base width 64).

    import math, torch
torch.manual_seed(0)
D_IN, BASE = 32, 64                       # proxy (base) width = 64
teacher = torch.nn.Sequential(torch.nn.Linear(D_IN, 256), torch.nn.Tanh(), torch.nn.Linear(256, 1))
X = torch.randn(4096, D_IN)
with torch.no_grad(): Y = teacher(X) * 3

def make(width, mup):
    torch.manual_seed(1)
    l1, l2, l3 = torch.nn.Linear(D_IN, width), torch.nn.Linear(width, width), torch.nn.Linear(width, 1)
    for l in (l1, l2, l3):                 # init variance 1/fan_in for input and hidden
        torch.nn.init.normal_(l.weight, std=l.in_features ** -0.5); torch.nn.init.zeros_(l.bias)
    if mup:
        torch.nn.init.normal_(l3.weight, std=1.0 / l3.in_features)   # output: variance 1/fan_in^2
    return l1, l2, l3

def train(width, lr, mup, steps=300):
    l1, l2, l3 = make(width, mup)
    m = width / BASE
    groups = [{"params": l1.parameters(), "lr": lr},
              {"params": [l2.weight], "lr": lr / m if mup else lr},  # hidden: lr / width
              {"params": [l2.bias], "lr": lr},
              {"params": [l3.weight], "lr": lr / m if mup else lr},  # output: lr / width
              {"params": [l3.bias], "lr": lr}]
    opt = torch.optim.Adam(groups)
    g = torch.Generator().manual_seed(2)
    for t in range(steps):
        idx = torch.randint(0, 4096, (128,), generator=g)
        h = torch.relu(l2(torch.relu(l1(X[idx]))))
        loss = ((l3(h) - Y[idx]) ** 2).mean()
        if not torch.isfinite(loss): return float("nan")
        opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    with torch.no_grad():
        return ((l3(torch.relu(l2(torch.relu(l1(X))))) - Y) ** 2).mean().item()

lrs = [2.0 ** k for k in range(-13, -3)]
for mup in (False, True):
    print("muP" if mup else "SP ")
    for width in (64, 256, 1024):
        res = [train(width, lr, mup) for lr in lrs]
        best = min(range(len(lrs)), key=lambda i: res[i] if res[i] == res[i] else 1e9)
        print(f"  width {width:5d}: best log2(lr) = {int(math.log2(lrs[best]))}, loss {res[best]:.4f}")

    Output (about one minute on a laptop CPU):

    SP

  width   256: best log2(lr) = -9, loss 0.0089

    In SP, the best learning rate falls by a factor of \(2^5 = 32\) when the width grows by 16, close to the \(1/n\) of formula (36.10). In μP it stays at \(2^{-7}\) for widths 256 and 1,024. At width 64 the μP curve is almost flat between \(2^{-7}\) (loss 0.010) and \(2^{-5}\) (0.009), so the argmin is noisy there. A small toy, one seed: treat it as an illustration, not a benchmark. Adding width 4,096 (which takes about ten times longer) moved the SP optimum to \(2^{-14}\), the edge of the grid, while μP stayed at \(2^{-7}\).

    Common mistakes

    • Copying “std 0.02” without checking the width. It equals the fan-in rule only near width 2,500. It works in Pre-LN transformers because the norms hide it, but it is not a universal constant. If you remove norms or change the architecture, recheck formula (36.1).
    • Forgetting the residual scaling when adding depth. Without it, the stream variance grows with \(2L\) (formula 36.3), and deeper models are harder to train.
    • Tuning the learning rate on a small SP model and using it on a big one. In SP the optimum moves down with width (Figure 5 and the code output); the big model will be unstable. Either use μP or fit how the best learning rate scales with size.
    • Relying on clipping alone. Clipping at 1.0 did not prevent PaLM’s spikes. For Adam, the update size is set by \(\hat m/\sqrt{\hat v}\), which clipping barely changes (Figure 3).
    • Resuming a finished cosine run at the peak learning rate. The sudden jump causes a spike. Re-warm up, or keep a stable-phase (WSD) checkpoint for later continuation.
    • No deterministic data order. Restart-and-skip needs to know exactly which batches came before the spike. Make the batch a function of the step number, as PaLM did.
    • Looking only at the loss. The gradient norm, the max attention logit and the mean \(\log Z\) usually start to rise well before the loss spikes. Log them from step one.
    • Half-implementing μP. One layer left in SP (often the output layer or the attention scaling) silently breaks transfer. Run a coordinate check before trusting the transfer.

    Interview questions

    Why does initialization variance need to depend on width?basic
    Each output of a linear layer is a sum of \(n\) terms, so its variance is \(n\sigma^2\) times the input variance (formula 36.1). To keep signals the same size through many layers, \(n\sigma^2\) should be about 1, which means \(\sigma = 1/\sqrt{n}\) (or \(\sqrt{2/n}\) with ReLU). A fixed \(\sigma\) such as 0.02 is only right near width 2,500; at width 768 the variance shrinks by 0.31 per layer, at 8,192 it grows by 3.28. In a plain stack of 32 layers these factors become \(10^{-8}\) to \(10^{8}\) changes in std.
    Why do GPT-style models scale the output projection of each block by \(1/\sqrt{2L}\)?core
    The residual stream is the embedding plus \(2L\) edits (attention and MLP in each of \(L\) blocks). At init, the edit variances add, so the stream variance grows like \(1 + 2Lv\). Scaling the std of each sub-layer’s output projection by \(1/\sqrt{2L}\) makes each edit’s variance proportional to \(1/(2L)\), so the total is independent of depth. GPT-2 introduced this; nanoGPT uses \(0.02/\sqrt{2L}\). It is a default, not a law: OLMo 2 found plain 0.02 everywhere worked better for them.
    Cosine vs warmup-stable-decay: which and why?core
    Both work; final quality is similar when tuned. Cosine needs the total step count in advance and gives smooth loss curves. WSD holds the peak and decays only at the end, so a stable-phase checkpoint can be continued or quickly “finished” with a short decay; one run can serve several token budgets, which is useful for scaling-law work and continued pretraining. Its loss looks worse than cosine until the decay, then drops quickly, because decay lowers the noise floor (formula 36.5). DeepSeek LLM’s multi-step schedule has the same reuse benefit and matched cosine.
    Why does lowering the learning rate at the end reduce the loss so much?core
    With noisy gradients the weights never sit exactly at a minimum; they bounce in a cloud whose average excess loss is about \(\eta\sigma^2/4\) per direction for SGD (formula 36.5). That floor is proportional to the learning rate. Decaying \(\eta\) shrinks the cloud and the loss drops, even if the model is not learning anything “new” in that phase. That is why the loss falls fast during the decay of WSD.
    Your loss spiked at step 80,000 of a large run. What do you do?core
    First, look at the logs before the spike: gradient norm, max attention logit per layer, mean \(\log Z\), update-to-weight ratios. If the loss does not recover, reload a checkpoint from before the first sign of trouble (PaLM went back about 100 steps), skip the batches around the spike (PaLM skipped 200 to 500), and continue. If it repeats, lower the learning rate or \(\beta_2\) from that point. If attention logits were growing, plan QK-norm for the next run; if \(\log Z\) was drifting, add z-loss. Keep a deterministic data loader and frequent checkpoints so this is possible, and record every intervention.
    Why does gradient clipping not prevent all loss spikes with Adam?advanced
    Adam’s update is \(\hat m/\sqrt{\hat v}\): it divides the gradient by its own running size. Clipping rescales the gradient, but the ratio barely changes. After a long quiet period, a burst of large gradients produces steps up to \((1-\beta_1)/\sqrt{1-\beta_2}\) times normal on the first step (3.16 for \(\beta_2 = 0.999\)), and more if the burst lasts, because \(\mathbf{m}\) catches up faster than \(\mathbf{v}\). In the toy of Figure 3, a 10-step burst gives steps of 6.5 times normal with \(\beta_2 = 0.999\) and 1.03 with 0.95. So lower \(\beta_2\) and a lower learning rate are stronger fixes; PaLM had clipping on and still saw about 20 spikes.
    What is z-loss, and what exactly does its gradient do?core
    z-loss adds \(\alpha(\log Z)^2\) to the loss, where \(Z\) is the softmax normalizer of the output logits; PaLM used \(\alpha = 10^{-4}\). Cross-entropy is invariant to adding a constant to all logits, so the logits can drift to large values where BF16 is coarse. The z-loss gradient is \(2\alpha \log Z\, p_i\) on logit \(i\) (formula 36.8); it sums to \(2\alpha\log Z\), so it moves the average logit back toward \(\log Z = 0\), the one direction cross-entropy cannot see. It is cheap and does not change the model’s predictions.
    How does QK-norm stop attention entropy collapse?core
    Attention scores are \(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k}/\sqrt{d_h}\); if \(W_Q\) and \(W_K\) grow by \(g\), the scores grow by \(g^2\), softmax becomes one-hot, its gradients vanish and training becomes unstable. QK-norm applies RMSNorm to each head’s \(\mathbf{q}\) and \(\mathbf{k}\) (formula 36.9), so the scores depend only on their directions and on slowly-learned gains. Weight growth in \(W_Q, W_K\) no longer reaches the softmax. Used in ViT-22B, OLMo 2 and Qwen3.
    What is μP, and why does the best learning rate move with width without it?advanced
    In standard parameterization, an Adam step changes each weight by about \(\eta\). For a hidden layer, the update is aligned with the input, so the \(n\) small changes add up coherently: \(|\Delta h|/|h| \approx 0.8\,\eta n\) after the first step (formula 36.10). Wider layers therefore need a proportionally smaller learning rate, and the optimum shifts. μP (Maximal Update Parameterization) rescales per-layer learning rates and inits so updates have the same effect at every width: for Adam, hidden and output learning rates scale as \(1/n\), the output layer’s init variance as \(1/n^2\), and attention uses \(1/d_h\). Then the best settings found on a small proxy transfer to the large model (μTransfer).
    Which hyperparameters transfer with μP, and which do not?advanced
    Across width: learning rate, init scales, and parameter multipliers (for example the output-logit multiplier) transfer with theoretical support. Transfer across depth, batch size, training length and sequence length was validated only empirically, on transformers. Regularization settings such as dropout and weight decay do not transfer. μP also does not choose model size or token count; that is the job of scaling laws. The paper’s headline: tuning on a 40M proxy beat published GPT-3 6.7B numbers with tuning cost 7% of pretraining.
    Why do large runs use \(\beta_2 = 0.95\) rather than 0.999, and what did PaLM do instead?advanced
    \(\beta_2 = 0.999\) averages squared gradients over about 1,000 steps, so when gradients suddenly grow, \(\hat v\) lags and updates become several times too large (formula 36.6, Figure 3). 0.95 averages over about 20 steps and adapts quickly. PaLM used Adafactor with \(\beta_2 = 1 - k^{-0.8}\), which starts low and rises toward 1, because rare embedding tokens have poorly estimated second moments over short windows; it balances quick adaptation early with a long memory later.

    Key takeaways

    • Initialization variance must scale like \(1/n\) to keep signals the same size (36.1, 36.2). Std 0.02 equals that only near width 2,500; Pre-LN hides the difference at init. Scaled residual init (36.3) keeps the stream from growing with depth.
    • Warmup protects the first steps; decay lowers the noise floor, which is proportional to \(\eta\) (36.5). WSD (36.4) and multi-step schedules match cosine and let you reuse or extend runs.
    • Loss spikes come from fragile model states plus unusual batches: Adam after a quiet period (36.6), growing attention logits, drifting output logits.
    • Fixes: \(\beta_2 = 0.95\) and a lower learning rate (strong), z-loss (36.7, 36.8) for output logits, QK-norm (36.9) for attention logits, clipping (weak against Adam), and restart-and-skip as a last resort.
    • In standard parameterization, Adam updates change hidden layers by about \(0.8\,\eta n\) (36.10), so the best learning rate moves with width. μP (36.11) rescales learning rates and inits per layer type so the best settings found on a small proxy transfer to the big model.

    Key formulas

    \[ \operatorname{Var}(y) = n\sigma^2\operatorname{Var}(x), \quad \sigma_{\text{out}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{2L}}, \quad \E[\text{loss}] \approx \frac{\eta\sigma^2}{4}, \quad \frac{|\Delta\theta|}{\eta} \to \frac{1-\beta_1}{\sqrt{1-\beta_2}} \]
    \[ \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}} + \alpha(\log Z)^2, \quad \frac{|\Delta h|}{|h|} \approx 0.8\,\eta\, n, \quad \eta_{\text{hidden}}(n) = \eta_{\text{base}}\frac{n_0}{n} \]
