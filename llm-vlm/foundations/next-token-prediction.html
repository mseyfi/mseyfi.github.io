    Language modeling: next-token prediction, from n-grams to neural models

    Session 1 showed that an LLM predicts the next token. This session asks the harder questions: how much of the past do you need, why counting stops working, and how neural networks fixed it. We follow the history from n-gram models to the first neural language model, with real numbers on real data, and end with the one weakness that next-token training still has.

    Time: 3 hours (reading 2 hours, exercises 1 hour)Level: Beginner to interview levelBefore this: Sessions 1, 3 and 5

    What you will learn

    • The chain rule and the Markov assumption: what an n-gram model throws away
    • n-gram counting, data sparsity, and why bigger n overfits, shown with real train and validation numbers
    • Smoothing, interpolation and back-off, and the idea behind Kneser–Ney
    • Why neural language models generalize: shared embeddings and a fixed number of parameters
    • Teacher forcing vs free running, and exposure bias
    • Language modeling as compression

    Why this matters

    “Predict the next token” sounds simple. The hard part is the condition: the next token given everything before it. For sixty years, language models could not afford “everything”, and the reasons why are exactly the reasons transformers work.

    Every idea in this session still matters today. Context length is still the main cost of an LLM. Sparsity is why we use subword tokens and embeddings. Smoothing became weight decay and label smoothing. Exposure bias is still a known weakness of next-token training, and part of the reason we do reinforcement learning after pretraining. And n-gram counts are still used to find duplicated training data and to check whether a test set leaked into training.

    Interview tip“Why did neural LMs beat n-grams?” The short answer: n-grams treat every context as a separate row in a table, so they cannot share what they learned between similar contexts. Neural LMs map contexts into a vector space where similar contexts get similar predictions.

    The exact model and the Markov shortcut

    The chain rule from Session 1 is exact. It loses nothing:

    \[ p(x_1, \dots, x_T) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} p(x_t \mid x_1, \dots, x_{t-1}) \tag{6.1} \]

    The problem is the condition. With a vocabulary of \(|V|\) tokens, there are \(|V|^{t-1}\) possible histories of length \(t - 1\). Nobody can store a probability table for each of them. The classic shortcut is the Markov assumption: only the last \(n - 1\) tokens matter.

    \[ p(x_t \mid x_1, \dots, x_{t-1}) \approx p(x_t \mid x_{t-n+1}, \dots, x_{t-1}) \tag{6.2} \]

    A model that uses this with \(n\) tokens in total (\(n - 1\) of context plus the one predicted) is an n-gram model: unigram (\(n = 1\), no context), bigram (\(n = 2\), Session 5), trigram (\(n = 3\)), and so on.

    AnalogyThe Markov assumption is a reader with a tiny window: they can only see the last few words through a slot in a piece of cardboard. Everything before the slot is forgotten. A transformer removes the cardboard.

    What does the shortcut throw away? In “The keeper who played for three clubs in Spain finally saves”, the verb agrees with “keeper”, eight words back. A trigram model sees only “Spain finally” and has no idea a person is the subject. Long-range agreement, topic, the names of people in a story, the question in a prompt: all of it is lost outside the window.

    n-gram models by counting

    As in Session 5, the maximum-likelihood estimate is a frequency. With \(c(\cdot)\) meaning “how many times this sequence appears in the training data”:

    \[ p(x_t \mid x_{t-n+1:t-1}) = \frac{c(x_{t-n+1}, \dots, x_{t-1}, x_t)}{c(x_{t-n+1}, \dots, x_{t-1})} \tag{6.3} \]

    It looks perfect: more context, better predictions. So why not use \(n = 10\)? Because of sparsity. The number of possible n-grams grows as \(|V|^n\), but the data does not.

    With words instead of characters it is much worse. With a 50,000-word vocabulary there are \(1.25 \times 10^{14}\) possible trigrams, more than any corpus could ever contain. Most perfectly normal sentences contain at least one trigram that was never seen in training.

    Fixing sparsity: smoothing, interpolation and back-off

    Statistical language modeling spent about thirty years on one question: how much probability to give to things you have never seen.

    Add-\(k\) smoothing

    \[ p_k(x_t \mid h) = \frac{c(h, x_t) + k}{c(h) + k\,|V|} \tag{6.4} \]

    Simple, but crude: it gives the same extra probability to every unseen continuation, sensible or not.

    Interpolation and back-off

    A better idea: mix models of different orders. A long context is precise but often unseen; a short context is vague but always available. Linear interpolation combines them with weights that sum to 1:

    \[ p(x_t \mid x_{t-3:t-1}) = \lambda_4\, p_4(x_t \mid x_{t-3:t-1}) + \lambda_3\, p_3(x_t \mid x_{t-2:t-1}) + \lambda_2\, p_2(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) + \lambda_1\, p_1(x_t) \tag{6.5} \]

    The \(\lambda\)s are tuned on held-out data. On the names, interpolating the 1- to 4-gram models with \(\lambda = (0.01, 0.1, 0.3, 0.59)\) gives a validation loss of 2.083, better than the best single model (2.102). Back-off is the related idea of using the longest context that was actually seen, and “backing off” to a shorter one otherwise.

    Kneser–Ney: the best classical method

    Kneser–Ney smoothing adds one clever observation. When you back off to a shorter context, ask not “how frequent is this word?” but “in how many different contexts does it appear?”. “Francisco” is frequent, but almost only after “San”, so it is a bad guess after an unseen context. “Glasses” follows many different words, so it is a better guess. This is the continuation probability:

    \[ p_{\text{cont}}(w) = \frac{\big|\{v : c(v, w) \gt 0\}\big|}{\big|\{(v, w') : c(v, w') \gt 0\}\big|} \tag{6.6} \]

    Modified Kneser–Ney with interpolation was the standard baseline until neural models took over in the 2010s. [Certain]

    Neural language models: sharing what you learn

    Even with perfect smoothing, n-grams have a deeper problem. They treat every context as a separate, unrelated row of a table. Seeing “the keeper saves the shot” teaches the model nothing about “the goalkeeper stops the shot”, because those are different rows.

    Bengio et al. (2003) fixed this with three ideas, all still inside every LLM:

    1. Map every word to a learned embedding vector (Session 1, formula 1.4). Similar words get similar vectors.
    2. Predict the next word with a neural network that reads the embeddings of the context words.
    3. Train everything together with the cross-entropy loss.

    With context embeddings \(\mathbf{e}_{t-n+1}, \dots, \mathbf{e}_{t-1} \in \R^{d}\) joined into one long vector, the model is:

    \[ \mathbf{h} = \tanh\!\big( H\, [\mathbf{e}_{t-n+1}; \dots; \mathbf{e}_{t-1}] + \mathbf{b}_h \big), \qquad p(\cdot \mid \text{context}) = \softmax(U \mathbf{h} + \mathbf{b}) \tag{6.7} \]

    Why does this generalize? If “keeper” and “goalkeeper” have similar embeddings, any context containing one predicts almost like a context containing the other, even if one of them was never seen in that position. The model shares statistical strength between similar contexts, which a table cannot do.

    The parameter count also changes from exponential to linear in the context size:

    \[ \underbrace{|V|^{n-1} \cdot (|V| - 1)}_{\text{n-gram table}} \quad \text{vs} \quad \underbrace{|V|\,d}_{\text{embeddings}} + \underbrace{(n-1)\,d\,h}_{H} + \underbrace{h\,|V|}_{U} \tag{6.8} \]
    Worked example

    Words with \(|V| = 50{,}000\), a 5-gram (\(n = 5\)): a full table would need about \(50{,}000^5 \approx 3 \times 10^{23}\) numbers. The neural model with \(d = 100\) and \(h = 500\) needs \(5{,}000{,}000 + 200{,}000 + 25{,}000{,}000 \approx 30\) million. And doubling the context only adds another \((n-1)\,d\,h\) block.

    This model still has a fixed window of \(n - 1\) tokens. Recurrent networks (session 7) removed the fixed window but forgot slowly; transformers (Track 2) let every token look at every earlier token directly. Each step is the same story: use more of the context, without a table that explodes.

    Training vs generation: teacher forcing and exposure bias

    During training, the model always conditions on the true previous tokens from the data. This is teacher forcing. It is what makes training parallel (Session 1, Figure 5) and stable.

    During generation, the model conditions on its own previous tokens. If it makes one mistake, every later prediction is conditioned on a history it never saw in training. This mismatch is called exposure bias. A rough way to see why it matters: if each token is “acceptable” with probability \(p\), independently, a whole output of \(T\) tokens has no bad token with probability

    \[ P(\text{no error in } T \text{ tokens}) = p^{T} \tag{6.9} \]

    Even with \(p = 0.99\), an output of 100 tokens is error-free only \(0.99^{100} = 36.6\%\) of the time. With \(p = 0.999\) and 1,000 tokens: \(36.8\%\). The independence assumption is too simple (models can recover from small slips), but the trend is real: long generations drift.

    How the field deals with it: scheduled sampling (sometimes feed the model its own predictions during training), sequence-level training with rewards (reinforcement learning after pretraining, Track 4), better decoding (session 79), and asking the model to check and correct its own work.

    Language modeling is compression

    There is a deep link between predicting and compressing. With arithmetic coding, a text can be stored in almost exactly \(\sum_t -\log_2 p(x_t \mid x_{\lt t})\) bits. So the cross-entropy loss is literally the number of bits per token needed to store text with that model. A better language model is a better compressor. Shannon used this idea in 1951 to estimate the entropy of English by asking people to guess the next letter. This is also why bits per byte (Session 3) is the natural unit for comparing language models.

    Pseudo-code

    Algorithm 1: interpolated n-gram model
    1. input: training sequences, max order \(n\), smoothing \(k\), weights \(\lambda_1..\lambda_n\) (sum to 1)
    2. for \(m = 1\) to \(n\) do count every m-gram \(c_m(\cdot)\) and every context \(c_m(\text{context})\)
    3. function \(p(x \mid h)\):
    4. \(s \leftarrow 0\)
    5. for \(m = 1\) to \(n\) do
    6. \(h_m \leftarrow\) last \(m - 1\) tokens of \(h\)
    7. \(s \leftarrow s + \lambda_m \cdot \dfrac{c_m(h_m, x) + k}{c_m(h_m) + k|V|}\) // formulas (6.4), (6.5)
    8. return \(s\)
    9. tune \(k\) and \(\lambda\) on a validation set by minimizing its average \(-\log p\)
    Algorithm 2: fixed-window neural language model (one training step)
    1. input: a batch of (context of \(n-1\) token IDs, next token ID)
    2. \(E \leftarrow C[\text{context}]\) // (B, n−1, d): one shared table
    3. \(\mathbf{x} \leftarrow\) reshape \(E\) to \((B, (n-1)d)\) // concatenate
    4. \(\mathbf{h} \leftarrow \tanh(\mathbf{x} H^\top + \mathbf{b}_h)\)
    5. \(\mathbf{z} \leftarrow \mathbf{h}\, U^\top + \mathbf{b}\) // (B, |V|) logits, formula (6.7)
    6. \(\mathcal{L} \leftarrow\) cross-entropy\((\mathbf{z}, \text{next token})\)
    7. backpropagate and take an AdamW step // Session 4

    Algorithm 2 in PyTorch, for the names data (a context of 3 characters). This is the model you will build in the next labs:

    import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F
torch.manual_seed(1337)
words = open("names.txt").read().split()
stoi = {c: i + 1 for i, c in enumerate(sorted(set("".join(words))))}; stoi["."] = 0
ctx = 3
X, Y = [], []
for w in words:
    h = [0] * ctx                                  # start with "..."
    for ch in w + ".":
        X.append(h); Y.append(stoi[ch]); h = h[1:] + [stoi[ch]]
X, Y = torch.tensor(X), torch.tensor(Y)

class BengioLM(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, V=27, d=10, ctx=3, h=200):
        super().__init__()
        self.C = nn.Embedding(V, d)                # shared table, |V| x d
        self.H = nn.Linear(ctx * d, h)
        self.U = nn.Linear(h, V)
    def forward(self, idx):                       # idx: (B, ctx) int64
        x = self.C(idx).flatten(1)                 # (B, ctx*d): concatenation
        return self.U(torch.tanh(self.H(x)))      # (B, V) logits, formula (6.7)

model = BengioLM()
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=1e-2, weight_decay=0.01)
for step in range(5000):
    ix = torch.randint(0, len(X), (64,))           # a random mini-batch (Session 4, formula 4.7)
    loss = F.cross_entropy(model(X[ix]), Y[ix])
    opt.zero_grad(set_to_none=True); loss.backward(); opt.step()
with torch.no_grad():
    print(F.cross_entropy(model(X), Y).item())     # compare with the 4-gram table (about 1.9 train, 2.1 validation)

    Use a validation split as in Session 5 before you believe any number. The neural model should match or beat the best interpolated n-gram on validation once it is trained long enough, with far fewer parameters than a 4-gram table. Measure it rather than trusting this sentence: the result depends on size and training time.

    Common mistakes

    • Choosing \(n\) on training loss. Training loss always prefers larger \(n\). Choose on validation (Figure 1).
    • Tuning smoothing on the test set. Use a separate validation split, or your reported number is optimistic.
    • Forgetting start padding. The first tokens need a context too; pad with start tokens (“...” in the code).
    • Thinking a longer context window always helps. Only if the model can use it and has the data to learn it; that is the whole lesson of sparsity.
    • Mixing up “model of the next token” and “model of the truth”. A language model is trained to imitate text, so it scores fluent nonsense highly (Session 3).

    Interview questions

    What is the Markov assumption in language modeling, and what does it cost?basic
    It replaces \(p(x_t \mid x_{\lt t})\) with \(p(x_t \mid \text{last } n-1 \text{ tokens})\). It makes estimation possible with counts, but it cannot use anything outside the window: long-range agreement, topic, entities, the question being answered.
    Why do higher-order n-gram models overfit?core
    The number of possible n-grams grows as \(|V|^n\), but data grows only linearly. Most high-order n-grams are seen once or never, so maximum-likelihood estimates are extreme (0 or 1). Training loss falls while held-out loss rises, as in the names example (best at \(n = 4\), worse at \(n = 5\)).
    Explain interpolation, back-off and the main idea of Kneser–Ney.core
    Interpolation mixes estimates of several orders with tuned weights; back-off uses the highest order that has data and falls back to lower orders otherwise. Kneser–Ney uses discounted counts and, for the lower-order model, the continuation probability: how many different contexts a word follows, not how often it occurs. That avoids over-predicting words like “Francisco” that are frequent but appear in only one context.
    Why do neural language models generalize better than n-grams?core
    They map tokens to embeddings and contexts to vectors, so similar contexts produce similar predictions, sharing statistical strength across contexts that never appeared together. Their parameter count grows linearly with context size instead of exponentially, and smoothing is built in through continuous representations and regularization.
    What is exposure bias? How is it addressed?advanced
    Training conditions on gold history (teacher forcing) but generation conditions on the model’s own outputs, so errors put the model in states it never trained on, and errors can compound over long outputs. Remedies: scheduled sampling, sequence-level objectives and RL fine-tuning, better decoding, self-correction and verification. In practice large pretrained models are fairly robust, but long generations still drift.
    Why is cross-entropy the number of bits needed to compress text?advanced
    Arithmetic coding can encode a sequence in about \(\sum_t -\log_2 p(x_t\mid x_{\lt t})\) bits using the model’s probabilities. Average cross-entropy in bits is therefore the compressed size per token, so a better language model is a better compressor. This is also why bits per byte is used to compare models with different tokenizers.
    Where are n-grams still used in modern LLM work?basic
    Deduplication of training data (n-gram or MinHash overlap), detecting benchmark contamination (long n-gram matches between test and training data), cheap baselines, some speculative-decoding drafters that copy n-grams from the prompt, and rescoring in small on-device systems.

    Key takeaways

    • The chain rule (6.1) is exact; the Markov assumption (6.2) makes counting possible but throws away everything outside a short window.
    • n-gram tables grow as \(|V|^n\): on names, validation loss is best at \(n = 4\) (2.10) and worse at \(n = 5\) (2.15), while training loss keeps falling.
    • Smoothing, interpolation (2.08 on names) and Kneser–Ney were the classical fixes.
    • Neural LMs (6.7) share strength through embeddings, with parameters growing linearly in context (6.8). Transformers continue the same idea with full context.
    • Teacher forcing trains on gold history; generation runs on its own outputs, so errors can compound (exposure bias, 6.9). Cross-entropy in bits is compressed size.
