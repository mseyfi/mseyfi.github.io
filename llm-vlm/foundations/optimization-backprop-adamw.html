    Optimization: backpropagation, SGD, Adam and AdamW, learning-rate schedules

    Training means changing billions of numbers so the loss goes down. This session shows how the gradient of every weight is found (backpropagation), how we use it to take a step (SGD, momentum, Adam, AdamW), and how the step size changes during training (warmup, cosine, warmup-stable-decay). These choices decide whether a large training run works or blows up.

    Time: 3 to 4 hours (reading 2 hours, exercises 1 to 2 hours)Level: Beginner to interview levelBefore this: Sessions 1 to 3

    What you will learn

    • What a gradient is, and how backpropagation computes all gradients with one backward pass
    • Why the backward pass costs about twice the forward pass, and why it needs memory for activations
    • Gradient descent, its stability limit, and why badly shaped losses are slow
    • SGD with mini-batches, momentum, Adam with bias correction, and why AdamW is different from Adam with L2
    • Optimizer memory: 16 bytes per parameter, and what that means for an 8B model
    • Learning-rate schedules (warmup, cosine, linear, warmup-stable-decay) and gradient clipping, with the real Llama 2 recipe

    Why this matters

    A model starts with random weights and a loss of about 11.8 nats. Training is the process that brings the loss down, one small step at a time, for hundreds of thousands of steps. The optimizer decides each step.

    Two questions must be answered at every step: which direction should each of the billions of weights move (the gradient, computed by backpropagation), and how far (the learning rate, the optimizer’s rules, and the schedule). Get the first wrong and nothing learns. Get the second wrong and the loss explodes into NaN after a week of GPU time.

    Interview tipExpect: “What is the difference between Adam and AdamW?”, “Why do we need warmup?”, “How much memory does Adam need for an 8B model?”, “Why does backward cost twice the forward?”. All four are answered with numbers below.

    This session gives you the full toolkit used to train real LLMs. Llama 2, for example, was trained with AdamW (\(\beta_1 = 0.9\), \(\beta_2 = 0.95\), \(\varepsilon = 10^{-5}\)), weight decay 0.1, gradient clipping at 1.0, 2,000 warmup steps, and a cosine schedule down to 10% of the peak learning rate (\(3 \times 10^{-4}\) for the 7B model). [Certain] By the end of this session you will know why each of those numbers is there.

    Gradients: which way is downhill?

    For a function of one number, the derivative \(f'(w)\) is the slope: how much \(f\) changes when \(w\) moves a tiny bit. For a function of many numbers \(\theta = (\theta_1, \dots, \theta_N)\), each partial derivative \(\partial \mathcal{L} / \partial \theta_i\) is the slope along one weight, with all others held still. The list of all of them is the gradient:

    \[ \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L} = \left( \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta_1},\ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta_2},\ \dots,\ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta_N} \right) \tag{4.1} \]

    The gradient points in the direction where the loss grows fastest. So the steepest way down is \(-\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}\). For Llama 3 8B, this vector has about 8 billion numbers, one per weight.

    AnalogyYou stand on a foggy mountain and want to reach the valley. You cannot see far, but you can feel the slope under your feet. The gradient is that slope. Each step, you feel it again and walk a little downhill.

    The chain rule

    A network is a chain of simple functions. If \(L\) depends on \(a\), and \(a\) depends on \(z\), then the effect of \(z\) on \(L\) is the product of the two local effects:

    \[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial a} \cdot \frac{\partial a}{\partial z} \tag{4.2} \]

    “If moving \(z\) by 1 moves \(a\) by 0.2, and moving \(a\) by 1 moves \(L\) by −0.8, then moving \(z\) by 1 moves \(L\) by −0.16.” Backpropagation is nothing more than this rule, applied from the loss back to every weight.

    Backpropagation

    We draw the computation as a graph: each node is one simple operation. Then we do two passes:

    1. Forward pass: compute every node’s value from the inputs to the loss, and keep the values we will need later.
    2. Backward pass: start from \(\partial L / \partial L = 1\) and walk back. At each node, multiply the incoming gradient by the node’s local derivative (formula 4.2) and send the result to its inputs. If a value feeds several nodes, add up the gradients that come back from each of them.

    Let us do it by hand on the smallest possible network: one input \(x\), one weight \(w\), one bias \(b\), a sigmoid, and a squared error to the target \(y\):

    \[ z = w x + b, \qquad a = \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}, \qquad L = (a - y)^2 \tag{4.3} \]

    With \(x = 2\), \(w = 0.5\), \(b = -0.5\) and \(y = 1\), step through Figure 1.

    The numbers from Figure 1

    Forward: \(z = 0.5 \cdot 2 - 0.5 = 0.5\), \(a = \sigma(0.5) = 0.622\), \(L = (0.622 - 1)^2 = 0.143\).

    Backward: \(\partial L/\partial a = 2(a - y) = -0.755\). Then \(\partial a/\partial z = a(1 - a) = 0.235\), so \(\partial L/\partial z = -0.755 \times 0.235 = -0.177\). Finally \(\partial L/\partial w = \partial L/\partial z \cdot x = -0.355\) and \(\partial L/\partial b = -0.177\).

    One step with learning rate 1: \(w \leftarrow 0.5 + 0.355 = 0.855\), \(b \leftarrow -0.5 + 0.177 = -0.323\). The new loss is \(0.040\), down from \(0.143\).

    Backprop through a linear layer

    The same rule, written for matrices, gives the two formulas you need for every linear layer \(Y = XW\) with \(X \in \R^{B \times n}\) and \(W \in \R^{n \times m}\). Let \(G = \partial L / \partial Y\) (same shape as \(Y\)) be the gradient that arrives from above:

    \[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial W} = X^\top G \in \R^{n \times m}, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial X} = G\, W^\top \in \R^{B \times n} \tag{4.4} \]

    Check the shapes: \((n \times B)(B \times m) = n \times m\), the shape of \(W\). Good. Each formula is one matrix multiplication with the same cost as the forward \(XW\). So the backward pass costs about twice the forward pass: one multiplication for the weight gradient, one for the input gradient. Forward \(2N\) plus backward \(4N\) gives the \(6N\) FLOPs per token from Session 1.

    Watch outBackprop stores the input \(X\) of every linear layer until the backward pass reaches it. For long sequences this activation memory is often larger than the weights. Activation checkpointing trades it for extra compute (session 39).

    Gradient descent, and why it can be slow or unstable

    The simplest optimizer takes a step against the gradient, scaled by the learning rate \(\eta\):

    \[ \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \, \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta_t) \tag{4.5} \]

    Too small an \(\eta\), and training crawls. Too large, and each step jumps over the valley and lands higher than before. For a bowl-shaped loss we can say exactly where “too large” starts. Near a minimum, the loss looks like \(\tfrac12 \sum_i \lambda_i u_i^2\), where the \(\lambda_i\) are the curvatures along its main directions (the eigenvalues of the Hessian). Gradient descent is stable only if

    \[ \eta \lt \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}} \tag{4.6} \]

    But progress along the flattest direction is proportional to \(\eta \lambda_{\min}\). So when \(\lambda_{\max} / \lambda_{\min}\) (the condition number) is large, the steep direction forces a small \(\eta\), and the flat direction then takes forever. The loss looks like a long, narrow ravine. Deep networks have such ravines everywhere, and this is the main reason we need better optimizers than plain gradient descent. Figure 2 shows a ravine with \(\lambda = 1\) and \(\lambda = 50\).

    Stochastic gradient descent and mini-batches

    The true loss is an average over the whole dataset (trillions of tokens). We cannot compute its gradient for every step. Instead we take a random mini-batch of \(B\) examples and use their average gradient as an estimate:

    \[ \mathbf{g}_B = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{i=1}^{B} \nabla_\theta \ell_i(\theta), \qquad \E[\mathbf{g}_B] = \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{g}_B) = \frac{\sigma^2}{B} \tag{4.7} \]

    The estimate is right on average (unbiased) but noisy, and the noise shrinks as \(1/B\). This is stochastic gradient descent (SGD). Bigger batches give cleaner gradients but cost more per step; beyond a certain “critical batch size”, doubling the batch no longer halves the number of steps. A common rule for SGD is the linear scaling rule: multiply the batch by \(k\), multiply the learning rate by \(k\) (with warmup). For Adam, the best learning rate grows more slowly with batch size. [Likely] LLMs count batches in tokens: Llama 2 used about 4 million tokens per step.

    Momentum

    Momentum keeps a running average of past gradients and steps along it:

    \[ \mathbf{v}_{t} = \beta\, \mathbf{v}_{t-1} + \mathbf{g}_t, \qquad \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta\, \mathbf{v}_t \tag{4.8} \]

    Components that keep the same sign add up; components that flip sign (the zig-zag) cancel. With \(\beta = 0.9\), a steady gradient eventually produces steps \(1/(1 - \beta) = 10\) times larger, so the effective learning rate along a consistent direction is \(10\eta\).

    AnalogyA heavy ball rolling down the ravine. Small bumps (noise) and side-to-side slopes barely change its path, but a long gentle slope keeps speeding it up.

    Adam

    Adam keeps two running averages for every weight: the mean of the gradient \(\mathbf{m}\), and the mean of the squared gradient \(\mathbf{v}\). Then it divides one by the square root of the other:

    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{m}_t &= \beta_1 \mathbf{m}_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1)\, \mathbf{g}_t \\ \mathbf{v}_t &= \beta_2 \mathbf{v}_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_2)\, \mathbf{g}_t^2 \\ \hat{\mathbf{m}}_t &= \frac{\mathbf{m}_t}{1 - \beta_1^t}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{v}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{v}_t}{1 - \beta_2^t} \\ \theta_{t+1} &= \theta_t - \eta\, \frac{\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t}{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t} + \varepsilon} \end{aligned} \tag{4.9} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\mathbf{g}_t^2\)
    the gradient squared, each component separately
    \(\beta_1, \beta_2\)
    how long each average remembers: 0.9 is about the last 10 steps; 0.95 about 20; 0.999 about 1,000
    \(\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t, \hat{\mathbf{v}}_t\)
    bias-corrected averages (explained below)
    \(\varepsilon\)
    a tiny number (\(10^{-8}\) or \(10^{-5}\)) that prevents division by zero

    What does this achieve? The ratio \(\hat m / \sqrt{\hat v}\) is roughly “average gradient divided by its typical size”. If a weight’s gradient is consistently positive, the ratio is near 1 and the weight moves about \(\eta\) per step. If the gradient is mostly noise that flips sign, \(\hat m\) is near 0 and the weight barely moves. And a weight with tiny gradients moves as fast as one with huge gradients. Each weight gets its own step size. This fixes the ravine problem of Figure 2.

    Why bias correction?

    Both averages start at 0. After the first step, \(m_1 = (1 - \beta_1) g_1 = 0.1\, g_1\): ten times too small. In general, \(\E[m_t] = (1 - \beta_1^t)\, \E[g]\), so dividing by \(1 - \beta_1^t\) removes the bias exactly. The same holds for \(v\).

    Worked example: the very first Adam step

    With bias correction, \(\hat m_1 = g_1\) and \(\hat v_1 = g_1^2\), so the first update is \(\eta\, g_1 / |g_1| = \pm\eta\), whatever the gradient’s size. Without correction and with \(\beta_1 = 0.9\), \(\beta_2 = 0.999\): the update would be \(\eta \cdot 0.1 g_1 / \sqrt{0.001\, g_1^2} = 3.16\,\eta\). The first steps would be more than 3 times too large, at the moment the model is most fragile. With \(\beta_2 = 0.95\) the factor would be 0.45 instead. Explore it in Figure 3.

    AdamW: weight decay done right

    Weight decay shrinks every weight a little each step, which keeps weights small and acts as regularization. With plain SGD, adding an L2 penalty \(\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\theta\rVert^2\) to the loss gives exactly the same thing. With Adam it does not. In “Adam with L2”, the penalty’s gradient \(\lambda\theta\) is added to \(\mathbf{g}\) before the division by \(\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}}\). Weights with large gradients then get almost no decay, and weights with small gradients get a lot. AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter) decouples the decay from the adaptive part:

    \[ \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \left( \frac{\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t}{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t} + \varepsilon} + \lambda\, \theta_t \right) \tag{4.10} \]

    Here \(\mathbf{m}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) are computed from the loss gradient only, and every weight shrinks by the same fraction \(\eta\lambda\) per step. With \(\eta = 3 \times 10^{-4}\) and \(\lambda = 0.1\), that is \(0.003\%\) per step. AdamW is the standard optimizer for transformers. Weight decay is usually not applied to biases and normalization weights, and often not to embeddings. [Likely]

    Interview tip“Adam vs AdamW”: in Adam with L2, the decay term passes through the per-weight scaling \(1/\sqrt{\hat v}\), so it is not real weight decay. AdamW applies decay directly to the weights, separate from the gradient statistics. This makes the best \(\lambda\) and the best \(\eta\) much less tied together.

    The memory cost of Adam

    Adam stores \(\mathbf{m}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) for every weight, usually in FP32. With mixed-precision training, the full bill per parameter is:

    WhatBytes per parameter
    BF16 weights (used in forward and backward)2
    BF16 gradients2
    FP32 master copy of the weights4
    Adam \(\mathbf{m}\) (FP32)4
    Adam \(\mathbf{v}\) (FP32)4
    Total, before activations16

    For an 8B model: \(8 \times 10^9 \times 16 = 128\) GB, before a single activation is stored. That is why full fine-tuning of an 8B model does not fit on one 80 GB GPU, and why sharding (FSDP, ZeRO), 8-bit optimizers, Adafactor and LoRA exist (session 38, session 57). Newer optimizers such as Lion (one state instead of two) and Muon (for the matrices of hidden layers) are being used too; Adam(W) is still the default. [Likely]

    Learning-rate schedules

    Almost no LLM is trained with a constant learning rate. The learning rate follows a schedule \(\eta(t)\) with two phases.

    Warmup

    For the first \(W\) steps, the learning rate rises linearly from near 0 to its peak:

    \[ \eta(t) = \eta_{\max} \cdot \frac{t}{W}, \qquad t \lt W \tag{4.11} \]

    Why? At the start, the weights are random, the gradients are large and change fast, and Adam’s \(\mathbf{v}\) has seen only a few gradients, so its scaling is unreliable. Big steps at that moment can push the model into a bad region it never leaves, or cause a loss spike. Warmup lets the statistics settle first. Llama 2 used 2,000 warmup steps out of about 500,000 (2 trillion tokens at 4 million tokens per step).

    Decay

    After warmup, the learning rate goes down. Early on, big steps make fast progress; at the end, small steps let the model settle into the bottom of the valley instead of bouncing around it. The most common shape is cosine decay to a minimum \(\eta_{\min}\):

    \[ \eta(t) = \eta_{\min} + \tfrac12 (\eta_{\max} - \eta_{\min}) \left( 1 + \cos\!\left( \pi \, \frac{t - W}{T - W} \right) \right), \qquad W \le t \le T \tag{4.12} \]

    At the start of decay the cosine is 1 and \(\eta = \eta_{\max}\); at the end it is \(-1\) and \(\eta = \eta_{\min}\). Halfway, \(\eta\) is the average of the two: with \(\eta_{\max} = 3\times10^{-4}\) and \(\eta_{\min} = 3\times10^{-5}\), that is \(1.65\times10^{-4}\).

    The weakness of cosine: you must choose the total length \(T\) in advance. Warmup-stable-decay (WSD) keeps the learning rate constant for most of training, then decays it quickly (for example linearly) over the last 10–20% of steps. You can continue training from any point in the stable phase and only run a short decay when you need a finished model. Several recent LLMs use it. [Likely]

    Gradient clipping

    Sometimes one batch produces a huge gradient (an odd example, or a sharp cliff in the loss). One such step can undo days of training. Global-norm clipping rescales the whole gradient when its length is above a threshold \(c\):

    \[ \mathbf{g} \leftarrow \mathbf{g} \cdot \min\!\left( 1,\ \frac{c}{\lVert \mathbf{g} \rVert_2} \right), \qquad \lVert \mathbf{g} \rVert_2 = \sqrt{\textstyle\sum_i g_i^2} \ \text{ over all weights} \tag{4.13} \]

    The direction is kept; only the length is capped. Example: \(\mathbf{g} = (3, 4)\) has norm 5; with \(c = 1\) it becomes \((0.6, 0.8)\). LLMs typically use \(c = 1.0\). Watching the gradient norm over training is one of the best early warnings of instability: rising norms often come before a loss spike (session 36).

    Putting it together: one optimizer step

    Algorithm 1: one training step with AdamW, clipping and a schedule
    1. input: weights \(\theta\), states \(\mathbf{m}, \mathbf{v}\), step \(t\), batch, hyper-parameters \(\beta_1, \beta_2, \varepsilon, \lambda, c\)
    2. \(L \leftarrow\) forward pass on the batch, storing activations
    3. \(\mathbf{g} \leftarrow \nabla_\theta L\) // backward pass, formula (4.4) per layer
    4. \(\mathbf{g} \leftarrow \mathbf{g} \cdot \min(1, c / \lVert \mathbf{g} \rVert)\) // clip, formula (4.13)
    5. \(\eta \leftarrow \text{schedule}(t)\) // warmup then cosine, formulas (4.11), (4.12)
    6. \(t \leftarrow t + 1\)
    7. \(\mathbf{m} \leftarrow \beta_1 \mathbf{m} + (1 - \beta_1)\mathbf{g}\), \(\;\mathbf{v} \leftarrow \beta_2 \mathbf{v} + (1 - \beta_2)\mathbf{g}^2\)
    8. \(\hat{\mathbf{m}} \leftarrow \mathbf{m}/(1 - \beta_1^t)\), \(\;\hat{\mathbf{v}} \leftarrow \mathbf{v}/(1 - \beta_2^t)\) // bias correction
    9. \(\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\big(\hat{\mathbf{m}}/(\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}} + \varepsilon) + \lambda\,\theta \cdot \text{decay\_mask}\big)\) // formula (4.10); no decay on norms and biases
    10. set all gradients to zero for the next step

    The PyTorch version, with a check that a hand-written AdamW matches the built-in one:

    import math, torch
torch.manual_seed(0)

model = torch.nn.Sequential(torch.nn.Linear(16, 32), torch.nn.LayerNorm(32), torch.nn.Linear(32, 4))

# 1) no weight decay on biases and normalization weights (all 1-D parameters)
decay   = [p for n, p in model.named_parameters() if p.ndim >= 2]
nodecay = [p for n, p in model.named_parameters() if p.ndim <  2]
opt = torch.optim.AdamW([{"params": decay, "weight_decay": 0.1},
                         {"params": nodecay, "weight_decay": 0.0}],
                        lr=3e-4, betas=(0.9, 0.95), eps=1e-8)

# 2) warmup + cosine schedule, formulas (4.11) and (4.12), as a multiplier of the peak lr
W, T, min_ratio = 100, 1000, 0.1
def lr_mult(step):
    if step < W:
        return (step + 1) / W
    p = (step - W) / max(1, T - W)
    return min_ratio + 0.5 * (1 - min_ratio) * (1 + math.cos(math.pi * min(1.0, p)))
sched = torch.optim.lr_scheduler.LambdaLR(opt, lr_mult)

# 3) one training step: forward, backward, clip, step, schedule
x, y = torch.randn(64, 16), torch.randint(0, 4, (64,))
loss = torch.nn.functional.cross_entropy(model(x), y)
opt.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
loss.backward()
gnorm = torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), max_norm=1.0)   # formula (4.13); returns the norm before clipping
opt.step()
sched.step()                                    # once per optimizer step, not per epoch
print(loss.item(), gnorm.item(), sched.get_last_lr())

# 4) a hand-written AdamW update, formula (4.10), matches torch.optim.AdamW
w = torch.randn(5, requires_grad=True); w2 = w.detach().clone().requires_grad_(True)
ref = torch.optim.AdamW([w2], lr=1e-2, betas=(0.9, 0.95), eps=1e-8, weight_decay=0.1)
m = torch.zeros(5); v = torch.zeros(5)
for t in range(1, 6):
    g = torch.sin(w.detach() * t)                  # any gradient sequence
    w2.grad = g.clone(); ref.step()
    with torch.no_grad():
        w.mul_(1 - 1e-2 * 0.1)                     # decoupled decay (PyTorch applies it first)
        m = 0.9 * m + 0.1 * g; v = 0.95 * v + 0.05 * g * g
        mh, vh = m / (1 - 0.9 ** t), v / (1 - 0.95 ** t)
        w -= 1e-2 * mh / (vh.sqrt() + 1e-8)
print((w - w2).abs().max().item())                 # about 1e-8: the same update

    Common mistakes

    • Forgetting to zero the gradients. PyTorch adds new gradients to old ones. Without zero_grad() every step uses the sum of all past gradients. (Gradient accumulation does this on purpose, then divides the loss by the number of micro-batches.)
    • Calling the scheduler once per epoch when it was designed for steps, or the other way round. Plot your schedule before training.
    • Weight decay on norms and biases. Decaying LayerNorm or RMSNorm gains toward 0 hurts. Use parameter groups as in the code.
    • Adam + L2 when you meant AdamW. torch.optim.Adam(weight_decay=...) is the L2 version. Use AdamW.
    • Clipping before unscaling in FP16 training. With a gradient scaler, call scaler.unscale_(opt) before clip_grad_norm_, or you clip the scaled (huge) gradients.
    • Too small \(\varepsilon\) in low precision. In FP16, \(10^{-8}\) rounds to 0 and \(\sqrt{\hat v} + \varepsilon\) can be 0. Keep optimizer math in FP32 (the standard), or use a larger \(\varepsilon\).
    • No warmup with a large peak learning rate. Classic cause of an early loss spike or NaN.
    • Changing the batch size without changing the learning rate. They are linked (formula 4.7); re-tune or scale.

    Interview questions

    Explain backpropagation in two sentences. Why is it efficient?basic
    Backprop applies the chain rule from the loss back through the computation graph, multiplying each incoming gradient by a local derivative and summing over paths. It is efficient because it computes the gradient for all weights in one backward pass that costs about twice the forward pass, reusing shared intermediate gradients instead of differentiating each weight separately.
    Why does the backward pass cost about 2× the forward pass?core
    For each linear layer \(Y = XW\), the backward pass needs two matrix multiplications of the same size as the forward one: \(\partial L/\partial W = X^\top G\) and \(\partial L/\partial X = GW^\top\). So forward ≈ \(2N\) FLOPs per token and backward ≈ \(4N\), giving the \(6N\) per token training rule. (The first layer does not need the input gradient, a small saving.)
    What is the difference between Adam and AdamW?core
    Adam with weight decay adds \(\lambda\theta\) to the gradient, so the decay is divided by \(\sqrt{\hat v}\) and becomes uneven: weights with large gradient variance are barely decayed. AdamW applies \(\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\lambda\theta\) directly, decoupled from the adaptive scaling. This gives true weight decay and makes \(\lambda\) and \(\eta\) easier to tune separately. AdamW is the standard for transformers.
    Why does Adam need bias correction?core
    \(\mathbf{m}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) start at zero, so early averages are shrunk: \(\E[m_t] = (1-\beta_1^t)\E[g]\). Dividing by \(1-\beta_1^t\) and \(1-\beta_2^t\) removes the bias. Without it, with \(\beta_1 = 0.9\), \(\beta_2 = 0.999\), the first update is about \(3.16\eta\) instead of \(\eta\), exactly when the model is most fragile.
    How much memory does AdamW training of an 8B model need, before activations?core
    With mixed precision: 2 bytes (BF16 weights) + 2 (gradients) + 4 (FP32 master weights) + 4 + 4 (Adam m and v) = 16 bytes per parameter, so 128 GB. Plus activations, which depend on batch size and sequence length. Ways to reduce: shard across GPUs (ZeRO or FSDP), 8-bit optimizer states, Adafactor (factored second moment), or LoRA (train few parameters).
    Why do we use learning-rate warmup?core
    At initialization gradients are large and change quickly, and Adam’s second-moment estimate is based on very few steps, so adaptive step sizes are unreliable. Large early steps can cause loss spikes or move the model into a poor region. Ramping the learning rate up over the first steps lets the statistics stabilize; it also matters more with large batches and with Post-LN transformers.
    What is the stability limit of gradient descent on a quadratic, and what does it tell you about deep networks?advanced
    On \(\tfrac12\sum\lambda_i u_i^2\), gradient descent is stable only for \(\eta \lt 2/\lambda_{\max}\), while convergence along the flattest direction scales with \(\eta\lambda_{\min}\). A large condition number therefore forces slow training. Deep nets have very uneven curvature, which motivates momentum and per-parameter adaptive methods. Empirically, training often runs near this “edge of stability”, where the largest curvature hovers around \(2/\eta\). [Likely]
    Cosine vs warmup-stable-decay schedules?core
    Cosine decays smoothly from the peak to a minimum over a fixed total length, so you must know the total steps in advance; stopping early leaves the model at a high learning rate. WSD holds the peak learning rate for most of training and decays quickly at the end, so you can keep training from any stable-phase checkpoint and produce a finished model with a short decay. Both work well; WSD is more flexible for continued pretraining.
    What does gradient clipping do, and how would you use it to detect problems?basic
    Global-norm clipping rescales the full gradient to length \(c\) (often 1.0) whenever its norm is larger, keeping the direction. It prevents one bad batch from causing a huge update. Log the pre-clip norm: a steady rise or sudden jumps usually come before loss spikes, and frequent clipping means the learning rate may be too high.
    Batch size doubles. What do you do with the learning rate?advanced
    Gradient noise variance halves (formula 4.7). For SGD, the linear scaling rule suggests doubling the learning rate, with warmup. For Adam, the best learning rate typically grows more slowly (often closer to \(\sqrt{2}\)), and beyond the critical batch size, larger batches stop reducing the number of steps needed. In practice, re-tune on a small sweep, and keep tokens-per-step in mind for LLMs.
    Why is \(\beta_2 = 0.95\) common for LLMs instead of the default 0.999?advanced
    \(\beta_2 = 0.999\) averages squared gradients over about 1,000 steps. If gradients suddenly grow, \(\hat v\) lags behind, the denominator is too small, and updates become too large, which can trigger loss spikes. \(\beta_2 = 0.95\) (about 20 steps) adapts faster. Large-scale recipes such as Llama 2 use 0.95. [Likely]

    Key takeaways

    • The gradient (4.1) gives the downhill direction for every weight; backpropagation computes all of it with the chain rule (4.2) in one backward pass, at about twice the forward cost (4.4).
    • Gradient descent (4.5) is stable only for \(\eta \lt 2/\lambda_{\max}\) (4.6); badly conditioned losses are slow. Mini-batches give unbiased but noisy gradients (4.7).
    • Momentum (4.8) smooths the path; Adam (4.9) gives each weight its own step size, with bias correction for the first steps; AdamW (4.10) applies weight decay correctly.
    • AdamW with mixed precision costs 16 bytes per parameter: 128 GB for an 8B model before activations.
    • Warmup (4.11) then decay (4.12, or WSD), plus global-norm clipping at 1.0 (4.13): the standard recipe, as in Llama 2.

    Key formulas

    \[ \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\left(\frac{\hat{\mathbf{m}}}{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}}+\varepsilon} + \lambda\theta\right), \quad \frac{\partial L}{\partial W} = X^\top G, \quad \eta \lt \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}}, \quad \mathbf{g} \leftarrow \mathbf{g}\min\!\left(1, \frac{c}{\lVert\mathbf{g}\rVert}\right) \]
