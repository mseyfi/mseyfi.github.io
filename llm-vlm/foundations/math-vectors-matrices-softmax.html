    Math you need, part 1: vectors, dot products, matrices and softmax

    Every number inside an LLM lives in a vector or a matrix, and almost every step is a dot product, a matrix multiplication or a softmax. This session builds these four tools slowly, with pictures you can move and numbers you can check by hand. At the end we use all four to compute one attention step on paper.

    Time: 3 to 4 hours (reading 2 hours, exercises 1 to 2 hours)Level: Beginner to interview levelBefore this: Session 1 and high-school algebra

    What you will learn

    • What a vector is, and how to read its length and direction
    • The dot product: what it measures, why it is the heart of attention, and why attention divides by the square root of the dimension
    • A matrix as a machine that transforms vectors, and how a linear layer works
    • Matrix multiplication: the shape rule, the cost in FLOPs, and the real numbers for an 8B model
    • Tensor shapes in PyTorch: (B, T, d), broadcasting and einsum
    • Softmax in depth: stability, the log-sum-exp trick, its gradient (the famous p − y), and the online softmax used by FlashAttention
    • One attention step computed fully by hand

    Why this matters

    Open any LLM and look at what it computes. You will find only a few kinds of operations, repeated billions of times: lists of numbers (vectors), tables of numbers (matrices), multiplications between them, and softmax.

    In Session 1 we saw the path: tokens become vectors, vectors pass through blocks, the last vector becomes scores, and softmax turns scores into probabilities. Every arrow in that picture is one of the tools in this session:

    • The embedding lookup picks a row of a matrix.
    • Attention compares tokens with dot products, turns the results into weights with softmax, and mixes vectors with a weighted sum.
    • The MLP and all the projections are matrix multiplications. More than 95% of the compute of an LLM is matrix multiplication. [Likely]
    • The output layer is one more matrix multiplication followed by softmax.
    AnalogyThese four tools are the alphabet of deep learning. Once you can read them, every paper becomes a sentence you can read slowly, word by word.

    Interviewers test these tools all the time, often in disguise: “What is the shape of \(QK^\top\)?”, “Why divide by \(\sqrt{d_k}\)?”, “What is the gradient of cross-entropy with respect to the logits?”, “How many FLOPs is this layer?”. By the end of this session you can answer all of them with numbers.

    Vectors: lists of numbers that mean something

    A vector is an ordered list of numbers. We write vectors in bold, and we say how many numbers they have:

    \[ \mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_d) \in \R^{d} \tag{2.1} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\mathbf{x}\)
    the whole vector (bold letter)
    \(x_i\)
    its \(i\)-th number, also called its \(i\)-th component (normal letter)
    \(d\)
    the number of components, the dimension
    \(\R^{d}\)
    “the set of all lists of \(d\) real numbers”. The symbol \(\in\) means “is in”.

    There are two ways to picture a vector, and you need both:

    • As an arrow from the origin to a point. With \(d = 2\) you can draw it on paper. Its direction and its length carry meaning.
    • As a list of features. Each position measures something. For example, a made-up 3-number vector for a word could be (how much about sport, how much about a person, how much about fruit).

    Inside Llama 3 8B every token vector has \(d = 4096\) numbers. Nobody chose what each position means; training found useful features by itself. But the geometry is the same as in 2D: similar meanings point in similar directions.

    Adding and scaling vectors

    We add vectors component by component, and we scale (multiply by a number) every component:

    \[ \mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} = (a_1 + b_1,\ \dots,\ a_d + b_d), \qquad c\,\mathbf{a} = (c\,a_1,\ \dots,\ c\,a_d) \tag{2.2} \]

    As arrows, adding means “walk along \(\mathbf{a}\), then along \(\mathbf{b}\)”. Scaling by 2 makes the arrow twice as long; scaling by \(-1\) turns it around. The residual stream from Session 1 is exactly this: each block adds its small vector to the running vector.

    Length: the norm

    The length of a vector is its norm, from Pythagoras:

    \[ \lVert \mathbf{x} \rVert_2 = \sqrt{x_1^2 + x_2^2 + \dots + x_d^2} \tag{2.3} \]

    For \(\mathbf{x} = (3, 4)\): \(\lVert \mathbf{x} \rVert = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5\). A vector divided by its own length has length 1; we call it a unit vector, and this step is called normalizing. RMSNorm, used in every modern LLM, is a close cousin: it divides a vector by \(\sqrt{\text{mean of } x_i^2}\) (Session 22).

    The dot product: how much do two vectors agree?

    The dot product takes two vectors of the same size and returns one number. Multiply matching components and add everything up:

    \[ \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \sum_{i=1}^{d} a_i\, b_i = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + \dots + a_d b_d \tag{2.4} \]

    The same number has a geometric meaning. With \(\theta\) the angle between the two arrows:

    \[ \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert \, \lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert \cos\theta \tag{2.5} \]

    So the dot product mixes two things: how long the vectors are, and how well they point the same way. If we only want the direction, we divide out the lengths. That is the cosine similarity:

    \[ \cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{\lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert \, \lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert} \in [-1, 1] \tag{2.6} \]
    Angle\(\cos\theta\)Meaning
    0°1same direction: very similar
    90°0perpendicular: unrelated
    180°−1opposite directions
    Worked example: similar words point the same way

    Use made-up features (sport, person, fruit): keeper \(= (0.9, 0.8, 0.1)\), goalkeeper \(= (0.95, 0.8, 0.0)\), banana \(= (0, 0, 0.95)\).

    keeper · goalkeeper \(= 0.9 \cdot 0.95 + 0.8 \cdot 0.8 + 0.1 \cdot 0 = 1.495\). The lengths are \(1.208\) and \(1.242\), so the cosine is \(1.495 / (1.208 \times 1.242) = 0.996\): almost the same direction.

    keeper · banana \(= 0 + 0 + 0.1 \cdot 0.95 = 0.095\), and the cosine is \(0.095 / (1.208 \times 0.95) = 0.083\): almost unrelated.

    Interview tip“Dot product or cosine for retrieval?” If all embeddings are normalized to length 1, they are the same number. Most embedding models normalize, so a vector database can use the fast dot product (maximum inner product search) and still get cosine ranking.

    Projection: how much of \(\mathbf{a}\) lies along \(\mathbf{b}\)

    Divide the dot product by the length of \(\mathbf{b}\) and you get the length of the “shadow” of \(\mathbf{a}\) on the line of \(\mathbf{b}\):

    \[ \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{a}) = \frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{\lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert^2}\, \mathbf{b} \tag{2.7} \]

    This is a useful way to read attention scores: a query “asks a question” in some direction, and each key’s dot product says how much it points that way.

    Why the dot product is the heart of attention

    In attention, every token makes a query vector \(\mathbf{q}\) (“what am I looking for?”) and a key vector \(\mathbf{k}\) (“what do I contain?”). The score between token \(i\) and token \(j\) is just \(\mathbf{q}_i \cdot \mathbf{k}_j\). A high score means “this token has what I am looking for”. The full story is in Session 17; here we only need one fact about how big these numbers get.

    Dot products grow with the dimension

    Take two random vectors whose components are independent, with mean 0 and variance 1. Each product \(a_i b_i\) also has mean 0 and variance 1. A sum of \(d\) independent terms has variance \(d\). So:

    \[ \operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}) = d, \qquad \operatorname{std}(\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}) = \sqrt{d} \tag{2.8} \]

    With \(d = 128\) (a typical attention head), typical dot products are about \(\pm 11\). Softmax of numbers that far apart puts almost all weight on one token, and its gradients become tiny. Dividing by \(\sqrt{d}\) brings the spread back to about 1. That is exactly why attention uses \(\mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{k} / \sqrt{d_k}\). Figure 2 shows it with real random samples.

    Matrices: machines that transform vectors

    A matrix is a table of numbers with \(m\) rows and \(n\) columns. We write \(W \in \R^{m \times n}\) and \(W_{ij}\) for the number in row \(i\), column \(j\). Multiplying a matrix by a vector gives a new vector:

    \[ \mathbf{y} = W \mathbf{x}, \qquad y_i = \sum_{j=1}^{n} W_{ij}\, x_j, \qquad W \in \R^{m \times n},\ \mathbf{x} \in \R^{n},\ \mathbf{y} \in \R^{m} \tag{2.9} \]

    Read this in two ways. Both are useful.

    • Row view: \(y_i\) is the dot product of row \(i\) of \(W\) with \(\mathbf{x}\). So a matrix is \(m\) “detectors”, and each one measures how much \(\mathbf{x}\) points in its direction.
    • Column view: \(\mathbf{y}\) is a mix of the columns of \(W\), with the numbers \(x_j\) as the mixing amounts. So the input decides how much of each column to use.
    AnalogyRow view: a panel of judges, each scoring the same performance from their own angle. Column view: a recipe, where the input says how many spoons of each ingredient (column) to use.

    A linear layer in a neural network is exactly this, plus a bias vector: \(\mathbf{y} = W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}\). Its weights \(W\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) are learned. Note that PyTorch stores the weight of nn.Linear(n, m) with shape \((m, n)\) and computes \(\mathbf{x} W^\top\) for row vectors; the math is the same.

    In 2D you can see what a matrix does: it moves every point of the plane. It can rotate, stretch, shear, flip, or squash the plane onto a line. Try each preset in Figure 3.

    Matrix multiplication

    Multiplying two matrices is many matrix-vector products at once. If \(A\) is \(m \times k\) and \(B\) is \(k \times n\), then \(C = AB\) is \(m \times n\), and each entry is one dot product: row \(i\) of \(A\) with column \(j\) of \(B\):

    \[ C_{ij} = \sum_{p=1}^{k} A_{ip}\, B_{pj}, \qquad (m \times k)\,(k \times n) \to (m \times n) \tag{2.10} \]

    The shape rule: the inner sizes must match (both \(k\)), and they disappear. The outer sizes remain. Check shapes before anything else; most bugs in model code are shape bugs.

    Worked example
    \[ \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 2 & -1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1{+}0{+}6 & 0{+}2{-}3 \\ 4{+}0{+}12 & 0{+}5{-}6 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 7 & -1 \\ 16 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \]

    Shapes: \((2 \times 3)(3 \times 2) \to (2 \times 2)\). Click any cell of the result in Figure 4 to see its dot product.

    The cost of a matrix multiplication

    Each of the \(m \cdot n\) output cells needs \(k\) multiplications and about \(k\) additions. So:

    \[ \text{FLOPs}(AB) \approx 2\, m\, k\, n \tag{2.11} \]

    A linear layer from \(n\) to \(m\) features applied to one token costs \(2mn\) FLOPs: two FLOPs per weight. That is where the “\(2N\) FLOPs per token” of Session 1 comes from.

    Real numbers: one MLP in Llama 3 8B

    Each MLP has three matrices of size \(4096 \times 14336\). Per token: \(3 \times 2 \times 4096 \times 14336 = 352{,}321{,}536\), about 352 million FLOPs per token per layer. With 32 layers that is about 11.3 GFLOPs per token for the MLPs alone, most of the \(2N \approx 16\) GFLOPs total.

    Transpose and batched multiplication

    The transpose \(A^\top\) swaps rows and columns: \((A^\top)_{ij} = A_{ji}\), and an \(m \times n\) matrix becomes \(n \times m\). One rule you will use often: \((AB)^\top = B^\top A^\top\).

    Put the \(T\) query vectors of a sentence as the rows of \(Q \in \R^{T \times d}\), and the keys as the rows of \(K \in \R^{T \times d}\). Then all query–key dot products come from one multiplication:

    \[ S = Q K^\top \in \R^{T \times T}, \qquad S_{ij} = \mathbf{q}_i \cdot \mathbf{k}_j \tag{2.12} \]

    GPUs are built to do such multiplications fast, so writing many dot products as one matrix multiplication is the main speed trick of deep learning. With a batch of \(B\) sentences and \(H\) heads, PyTorch does \(B \cdot H\) of these at once: shapes \((B, H, T, d) \times (B, H, d, T)\) \(\to (B, H, T, T)\).

    Real numbersFor \(T = 8192\) and \(H = 32\) heads, the score tensor \(S\) has \(32 \times 8192^2 \approx 2.1\) billion numbers per sentence per layer: about 4.3 GB in 16-bit. That is why FlashAttention never stores it (session 124).

    Tensors, shapes and PyTorch

    A tensor is the general name for an array with any number of axes: a vector has 1 axis, a matrix 2, and an LLM activation usually has 3: \((B, T, d)\) for batch, tokens and features. Here is how the shape changes inside one block (Llama 3 8B, \(B = 2\), \(T = 16\)):

    StepOperationShape
    Inputtoken vectors(2, 16, 4096)
    Queries\(X W_Q\), split into 32 heads of 128(2, 32, 16, 128)
    Scores\(Q K^\top / \sqrt{128}\)(2, 32, 16, 16)
    Weightssoftmax over the last axis(2, 32, 16, 16)
    Mixweights × \(V\), heads joined back(2, 16, 4096)
    MLP up\(\times W_1\)(2, 16, 14336)
    MLP down\(\times W_2\)(2, 16, 4096)

    Broadcasting

    When two tensors have different shapes, PyTorch can stretch the smaller one without copying. It compares shapes from the right. Two sizes are compatible if they are equal, or if one of them is 1 (that axis is repeated). A missing axis counts as size 1.

    Example: adding a mask

    Scores have shape \((B, H, T, T)\). A padding mask has shape \((B, T)\). Adding them directly fails, or worse, broadcasts the wrong way. Reshape the mask to \((B, 1, 1, T)\): now it is repeated over heads (\(H\)) and over query rows (\(T\)), and it masks the key axis, which is what we want.

    Einsum: write the math, get the code

    torch.einsum lets you write a product by naming the axes. Repeated letters that disappear from the output are summed:

    y = torch.einsum("btd,de->bte", x, W)          # linear layer: sum over d
s = torch.einsum("bhqd,bhkd->bhqk", q, k)      # all attention scores, formula (2.12)
o = torch.einsum("bhqk,bhkd->bhqd", a, v)      # weighted sum of values

    Softmax, in depth

    Session 1 introduced softmax. Here we go deeper, because softmax is everywhere: the output layer, every attention head, and the router of a mixture-of-experts layer (session 29).

    \[ \softmax(\mathbf{z})_i = p_i = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} e^{z_j}} \tag{2.13} \]

    Five properties, with short reasons

    1. Every \(p_i\) is positive and they sum to 1. Exponentials are positive, and we divide by their sum.
    2. Order is kept. \(e^z\) grows with \(z\), so a bigger score always gets a bigger probability.
    3. Adding the same number to all scores changes nothing. This is the key to stable code:
      \[ \frac{e^{z_i + c}}{\sum_j e^{z_j + c}} = \frac{e^{c}\, e^{z_i}}{e^{c} \sum_j e^{z_j}} = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}} \tag{2.14} \]
    4. Only differences matter. A gap of \(\Delta\) between two scores means a ratio \(e^{\Delta}\) between their probabilities. With two classes, softmax becomes the sigmoid: \(p_1 = 1 / (1 + e^{-(z_1 - z_2)})\).
    5. Temperature scales all differences: \(\softmax(\mathbf{z}/\tau)\) is sharper for \(\tau \lt 1\) and flatter for \(\tau \gt 1\).

    Stable softmax and the log-sum-exp trick

    Computers overflow at about \(e^{89}\) in 32-bit float and much sooner in 16-bit. Scores like 1000 would give infinity. Property 3 saves us: subtract the largest score \(m = \max_j z_j\) first. Then the largest exponent is \(e^0 = 1\), and nothing overflows.

    We often need the log of the probability (for the loss). Computing \(\log(p_i)\) after softmax can give \(\log 0 = -\infty\) when \(p_i\) underflows. Instead we use the log-sum-exp function:

    \[ \operatorname{LSE}(\mathbf{z}) = \log \sum_j e^{z_j} = m + \log \sum_j e^{z_j - m}, \qquad \log p_i = z_i - \operatorname{LSE}(\mathbf{z}) \tag{2.15} \]

    For \(\mathbf{z} = (1000, 999, 998)\): \(m = 1000\), and

    \[ \operatorname{LSE} = 1000 + \log\left(1 + e^{-1} + e^{-2}\right) = 1000.408 \]

    No overflow anywhere. This is what torch.log_softmax and F.cross_entropy do inside.

    The gradient of softmax

    How does \(p_i\) change when we move score \(z_j\)? Using the quotient rule:

    \[ \frac{\partial p_i}{\partial z_j} = p_i\,(\delta_{ij} - p_j), \qquad \text{in matrix form } J = \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{p}) - \mathbf{p}\mathbf{p}^\top \tag{2.16} \]

    Here \(\delta_{ij}\) is 1 when \(i = j\) and 0 otherwise. Raising your own score raises your probability (by \(p_i(1 - p_i)\)); raising another score lowers it (by \(p_i p_j\)). Every row of \(J\) sums to 0, because adding the same amount to all scores changes nothing (property 3).

    Softmax plus cross-entropy: the gradient is \(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{y}\)

    This is one of the most asked derivations. Let the correct class be \(c\), and let \(\mathbf{y}\) be the one-hot vector (1 at \(c\), 0 elsewhere). The loss is

    \[ \mathcal{L} = -\log p_c = -z_c + \log \sum_j e^{z_j} \tag{2.17} \]

    Take the derivative with respect to \(z_i\). The first term gives \(-1\) if \(i = c\) and 0 otherwise, which is \(-y_i\). The second term gives \(e^{z_i} / \sum_j e^{z_j} = p_i\). So:

    \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{z}} = \mathbf{p} - \mathbf{y} \tag{2.18} \]

    Beautiful and simple: the gradient is “what the model said minus what it should have said”. The correct score is pushed up by \(1 - p_c\); every wrong score is pushed down by its own probability. A gradient step moves all scores at once, as Figure 5 shows.

    Worked example

    Scores \((2, 1, 0.1)\), correct class 1. Then \(\mathbf{p} = (0.659, 0.242, 0.099)\), the loss is \(0.417\), and the gradient is \((-0.341,\ 0.242,\ 0.099)\). One step with learning rate 1 gives scores \((2.341, 0.758, 0.001)\), new probabilities \((0.768, 0.158, 0.074)\), and a lower loss of \(0.264\).

    Online softmax: softmax in one pass, block by block

    To compute softmax over a very long row, we normally need two passes: one to find the maximum \(m\), and one to sum the exponentials. FlashAttention cannot afford to read the row twice. The trick is to keep a running maximum \(m\) and a running sum \(\ell\), and fix the old sum whenever the maximum grows:

    \[ m' = \max(m, m_{\text{new}}), \qquad \ell' = \ell \, e^{m - m'} + \ell_{\text{new}} \, e^{m_{\text{new}} - m'} \tag{2.19} \]

    where \(m_{\text{new}}\) and \(\ell_{\text{new}}\) are the maximum and the sum of \(e^{z - m_{\text{new}}}\) for the new block. The factor \(e^{m - m'}\) rescales the old sum to the new reference point. The result is exactly the same as the two-pass softmax. Step through it in Figure 6.

    Putting it together: one attention step by hand

    Now we can compute a full attention step with all four tools. Three tokens, “keeper”, “ball”, “it”, each with a 2-number query, key and value (in a real model these come from multiplying token vectors by \(W_Q\), \(W_K\), \(W_V\)):

    \[ Q = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad K = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 0.2 & 0.6 \end{pmatrix}, \quad V = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 0.5 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix} \]

    The four steps are: scores \(S = QK^\top / \sqrt{2}\) (formula 2.12); a causal mask (each token sees only itself and earlier tokens); softmax on each row (formula 2.13); and the output \(O = AV\) (formula 2.10):

    \[ O = \softmax\!\left( \frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d}} + M \right) V \tag{2.20} \]

    Look at the last row: “it” puts weight 0.552 on “ball”, 0.314 on itself and only 0.134 on “keeper”. Its output \((0.291, 0.709)\) is mostly the value of “ball”. In words: the model has worked out what “it” refers to, using nothing but dot products, a softmax and a weighted sum. Track 2 builds the full mechanism around this one line.

    Pseudo-code

    Algorithm 1: matrix multiplication, and its cost
    1. input: \(A \in \R^{m \times k}\), \(B \in \R^{k \times n}\)
    2. \(C \leftarrow\) zeros\((m, n)\)
    3. for \(i = 1\) to \(m\) do
    4. for \(j = 1\) to \(n\) do
    5. for \(p = 1\) to \(k\) do
    6. \(C_{ij} \leftarrow C_{ij} + A_{ip} B_{pj}\) // 1 multiply + 1 add
    7. return \(C\) // total about \(2mkn\) FLOPs, formula (2.11)
    Algorithm 2: stable softmax and log-softmax
    1. input: scores \(\mathbf{z} \in \R^{n}\)
    2. \(m \leftarrow \max_j z_j\) // formula (2.14): shifting changes nothing
    3. \(e_j \leftarrow \exp(z_j - m)\) for all \(j\) // largest value is exactly 1
    4. \(s \leftarrow \sum_j e_j\)
    5. \(p_j \leftarrow e_j / s\) // softmax
    6. \(\log p_j \leftarrow z_j - (m + \log s)\) // log-softmax via LSE, formula (2.15)
    Algorithm 3: online softmax (one pass over blocks)
    1. input: blocks of scores \(\mathbf{z}^{(1)}, \dots, \mathbf{z}^{(B)}\)
    2. \(m \leftarrow -\infty\), \(\ell \leftarrow 0\)
    3. for each block \(\mathbf{z}^{(b)}\) do
    4. \(m_b \leftarrow \max \mathbf{z}^{(b)}\), \(\ell_b \leftarrow \sum \exp(\mathbf{z}^{(b)} - m_b)\)
    5. \(m' \leftarrow \max(m, m_b)\)
    6. \(\ell \leftarrow \ell\, e^{m - m'} + \ell_b\, e^{m_b - m'}\) // formula (2.19)
    7. \(m \leftarrow m'\)
    8. return \(m\), \(\ell\) // then \(p_j = e^{z_j - m} / \ell\)
    Algorithm 4: one attention head
    1. input: \(Q, K, V \in \R^{T \times d}\), causal flag
    2. \(S \leftarrow Q K^\top / \sqrt{d}\) // (T, T) scores, formula (2.12)
    3. if causal then \(S_{ij} \leftarrow -\infty\) for all \(j \gt i\)
    4. \(A \leftarrow\) softmax of each row of \(S\) // Algorithm 2 per row
    5. return \(O = A V\) // (T, d), formula (2.20)

    The same ideas as a short PyTorch script you can run. Each block checks one claim from this session:

    import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F
torch.manual_seed(0)

# 1) dot product and cosine, formulas (2.4) and (2.6)
keeper, goalkeeper = torch.tensor([0.9, 0.8, 0.1]), torch.tensor([0.95, 0.8, 0.0])
print(keeper @ goalkeeper, F.cosine_similarity(keeper, goalkeeper, dim=0))   # 1.495, 0.996

# 2) shapes and FLOPs of a linear layer, formula (2.11)
B, T, d, d_ff = 2, 16, 4096, 14336
x = torch.randn(B, T, d)
W = torch.randn(d, d_ff) / d ** 0.5
y = x @ W                                        # (2, 16, 14336)
print(y.shape, "FLOPs:", 2 * B * T * d * d_ff)

# 3) softmax is shift-invariant; the naive version overflows
z = torch.tensor([1000.0, 999.0, 998.0])
print(torch.softmax(z, dim=0))                  # fine: subtracts the max inside
print(torch.exp(z) / torch.exp(z).sum())        # nan: inf / inf
print(torch.logsumexp(z, dim=0))                # 1000.408, formula (2.15)

# 4) gradient of cross-entropy w.r.t. the logits is p - y, formula (2.18)
z = torch.tensor([2.0, 1.0, 0.1], requires_grad=True)
loss = F.cross_entropy(z[None], torch.tensor([0]))
loss.backward()
print(z.grad, torch.softmax(z, 0).detach() - torch.tensor([1.0, 0.0, 0.0]))

# 5) random dot products have std close to sqrt(d), formula (2.8)
for dd in [4, 64, 1024]:
    s = (torch.randn(10000, dd) * torch.randn(10000, dd)).sum(-1)
    print(dd, round(s.std().item(), 2), round(dd ** 0.5, 2))

# 6) the attention example of Figure 7, formula (2.20)
Q = torch.tensor([[1., 1.], [1., 0.], [0., 2.]])
K = torch.tensor([[1., 0.], [0., 1.], [0.2, 0.6]])
V = torch.tensor([[1., 0.], [0., 1.], [0.5, 0.5]])
S = Q @ K.T / 2 ** 0.5
S = S.masked_fill(torch.triu(torch.ones(3, 3, dtype=torch.bool), 1), float("-inf"))
A = torch.softmax(S, dim=-1)
print(A.round(decimals=4), (A @ V).round(decimals=4))   # last row: 0.1342 0.5522 0.3136 and 0.291 0.709

    Common mistakes

    • Softmax over the wrong axis. Attention weights must sum to 1 over the keys (the last axis of \((B, H, T, T)\)). Softmax over queries gives a model that trains, but badly. Always write dim=-1 on purpose and check that rows sum to 1.
    • Computing log(softmax(z)) in two steps. It can give \(-\infty\) and NaN gradients. Use log_softmax or cross_entropy on raw logits.
    • Applying softmax before F.cross_entropy. It already contains log-softmax. Applying softmax twice trains much worse and often goes unnoticed.
    • Silent broadcasting. Shapes \((B, T)\) and \((B, T, T)\) may broadcast in a way you did not mean. Add explicit axes with [:, None, None, :] and assert shapes.
    • Mixing up \(W\) and \(W^\top\). nn.Linear(in, out).weight has shape (out, in). When you copy weights between frameworks, check this first.
    • Dot product when you meant cosine. If embeddings are not normalized, long vectors win every search. Normalize, or use cosine explicitly.
    • Masking with a large negative number in 16-bit. \(-10^9\) overflows FP16 (its range ends at 65,504). Use float("-inf") or the dtype’s minimum, and make sure no row is fully masked, or softmax returns NaN.

    Interview questions

    What does a dot product measure? When is it zero?basic
    It measures length times alignment: \(\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert\cos\theta\). It is zero when the vectors are perpendicular (or one is zero), positive when they point the same way, negative when opposite. Cosine similarity removes the lengths and keeps only the direction.
    Why does attention divide by \(\sqrt{d_k}\)?core
    If query and key components are roughly independent with unit variance, \(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k}\) has variance \(d_k\), so its spread grows like \(\sqrt{d_k}\). Large score differences push softmax toward one-hot, where its gradient (formula 2.16) is nearly zero, so learning stalls. Dividing by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) keeps the scores at unit scale at initialization. Some models add QK-norm or a learned scale for the same reason.
    How many FLOPs does an \((m \times k)(k \times n)\) multiplication take? And a linear layer per token?basic
    About \(2mkn\): \(mkn\) multiplies and about as many adds. A linear layer with \(n\) inputs and \(m\) outputs costs \(2mn\) per token, two FLOPs per weight. Example: one Llama 3 8B MLP (three \(4096 \times 14336\) matrices) costs about 352 MFLOPs per token per layer.
    Derive the gradient of softmax cross-entropy with respect to the logits.core
    \(\mathcal{L} = -z_c + \log\sum_j e^{z_j}\). The derivative of \(-z_c\) w.r.t. \(z_i\) is \(-y_i\); the derivative of the log-sum-exp is \(p_i\). So \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{p} - \mathbf{y}\). It is bounded in \([-1, 1]\) per component, which is one reason this loss trains stably.
    Why subtract the maximum in softmax? What is log-sum-exp?basic
    Softmax is invariant to adding a constant (formula 2.14), so subtracting the max changes nothing mathematically but makes the largest exponent \(e^0 = 1\), avoiding overflow. Log-sum-exp computes \(\log\sum e^{z_j}\) as \(m + \log\sum e^{z_j - m}\), giving a stable log-softmax \(z_i - \operatorname{LSE}(\mathbf{z})\) without ever forming tiny probabilities.
    What is the Jacobian of softmax, and why is it singular?advanced
    \(J = \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{p}) - \mathbf{p}\mathbf{p}^\top\), i.e. \(\partial p_i/\partial z_j = p_i(\delta_{ij} - p_j)\). It is symmetric and its rows sum to zero, so \(J\mathbf{1} = \mathbf{0}\): the all-ones direction is in its null space. This matches shift invariance: adding the same value to every logit does not change the output. When \(\mathbf{p}\) is nearly one-hot, every entry of \(J\) is near zero, which is the saturation problem.
    How does FlashAttention compute softmax without storing the full score matrix?advanced
    With online softmax: it processes keys in blocks, keeps a running max \(m\), running sum \(\ell\) and running output \(O\) per query row, and when a new block raises the max it rescales the old \(\ell\) and \(O\) by \(e^{m - m'}\) (formula 2.19). The final result is exact. The \(T \times T\) matrix is never written to HBM, which saves memory traffic, the real bottleneck.
    Shape and memory of the attention score tensor for B=1, H=32, T=8192 in FP16?core
    Shape \((1, 32, 8192, 8192)\): about 2.15 billion entries, times 2 bytes, about 4.3 GB per layer. With 32 layers that is far more than the weights. Standard attention writes and reads this tensor; FlashAttention computes it in tiles inside on-chip memory instead.
    Dot product or cosine similarity for retrieval?core
    Cosine compares only direction; the dot product also rewards length, so long vectors dominate. If embeddings are L2-normalized, they are identical, and you can use fast maximum-inner-product indexes. Some models deliberately keep length meaningful (for example as a confidence); then use the dot product they were trained with. Always match the similarity used in training.
    What is the rank of a matrix, and why does it matter for LoRA?core
    Rank is the number of independent directions the matrix can output. A \(d \times d\) matrix of rank \(r\) can be written as \(BA\) with \(B \in \R^{d\times r}\), \(A \in \R^{r\times d}\): \(2dr\) numbers instead of \(d^2\). LoRA assumes the fine-tuning update has low rank, so it learns \(B\) and \(A\) only. With \(d = 4096\) and \(r = 16\): 131k parameters instead of 16.8M per matrix.
    You add a padding mask of shape (B, T) to scores of shape (B, H, T, T). What goes wrong, and how do you fix it?core
    Broadcasting aligns from the right: (B, T) is treated as (1, 1, B, T), which is wrong (it fails, or silently mixes batch and query axes when B equals T). Reshape to (B, 1, 1, T) so the mask repeats over heads and queries and applies to the key axis. Assert shapes, and test with a batch where padding differs between examples.

    Key takeaways

    • A vector is an arrow and a list of features. The dot product (2.4–2.5) measures length times alignment; cosine (2.6) measures alignment only.
    • Random dot products grow like \(\sqrt{d}\) (2.8); this is why attention divides by \(\sqrt{d_k}\).
    • A matrix transforms vectors: rows are detectors, columns are ingredients. A linear layer is \(W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}\).
    • \((m\times k)(k\times n) \to (m\times n)\) costs about \(2mkn\) FLOPs (2.11). \(QK^\top\) computes all attention scores at once (2.12).
    • Softmax is shift-invariant (2.14), computed stably with the max trick and log-sum-exp (2.15). With cross-entropy its gradient is \(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{y}\) (2.18). Online softmax (2.19) makes FlashAttention possible.

    Key formulas

    \[ \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = \sum_i a_i b_i = \lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert\cos\theta, \quad \operatorname{FLOPs}(AB) \approx 2mkn, \quad \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{z}} = \mathbf{p} - \mathbf{y}, \quad O = \softmax\!\left(\tfrac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d}} + M\right)V \]
