    text-to-video retrieval

    In this lab you build a small text-to-video search system and measure it. You cut videos into segments, embed every frame with SigLIP, pool each segment into one vector, index the segments with FAISS, search with text queries, and compute recall@k. Then you train a one-layer temporal model on top of the frozen frames and see why mean pooling cannot tell which way something moves.

    Time: 3 to 4 hours (about 25 minutes of it is CPU time)Level: Intermediate, hands-onBefore this: Sessions 167 (SigLIP), 208 (text-to-video retrieval) and 213 (evaluating video understanding); Python and basic PyTorch

    What you will learn

    • How to turn a video library into a segment index: frame sampling, segment length and stride, pooling, normalization and a metadata table
    • How to embed frames and queries with SigLIP correctly (padding to 64 tokens, unit vectors) and how to read its small cosine values
    • How to index and search with FAISS (IndexFlatIP, IndexIDMap) and how IVF trades recall for speed through nlist and nprobe
    • How to compute recall@k with an explicit hit rule for overlapping segments, and how noisy 40 queries are
    • Why mean and best-frame pooling are blind to the order of frames, measured: R@1 near 50% when both directions of a motion exist
    • How a one-layer temporal transformer with position embeddings, trained with a symmetric contrastive loss, raises R@1, and how much seeds move the result

    Why this matters

    In session 208 you learned the ideas: embed frames, pool them, put the vectors in an index, and rank by cosine similarity. In this lab you build the whole system and measure it. You cut videos into segments, embed every frame with SigLIP, pool the frames of each segment into one vector, index the segments with FAISS, search with text queries, and compute recall@k. Then you train a small temporal model and check whether it beats mean pooling.

    Every number on this page comes from running the code below on a CPU laptop. Some results will surprise you:

    • Mean pooling cannot tell direction. On our library, mean pooling of SigLIP frames gets R@1 = 45.0% with 8-second segments. Most misses are not random: 17 of the 22 misses return the right object moving the wrong way.
    • A one-layer transformer over the frames fixes most of it. With the same frozen SigLIP features, the temporal model reaches R@1 = 95.0% (one seed), 85.8% on average over three seeds.
    • The loss matters as much as the model. Training the same model with only the video-to-text half of the contrastive loss gave 65.0% on average.

    The previous session, Session 213, explained how to score video systems, including R@k. Here you apply it to your own system. The next lab, Session 215, reuses the same segment scores to find moments inside one video.

    Interview tip“Design video search over a large library” is a common system-design question. Interviewers want to hear four concrete things: what one vector stands for (frame, segment or video), how you get it (encoder plus pooling), how you store and search it (index type, metadata), and how you know it works (a labeled test set and R@k). This lab gives you real numbers for each.

    Setup

    You need Python 3.9 or newer and about 1 GB of disk for the model. No GPU is needed.

    pip install torch transformers faiss-cpu sentencepiece pillow numpy protobuf

    We use google/siglip-base-patch16-224: a ViT-B/16 image encoder at 224×224 pixels and a text encoder, both giving 768-dimensional embeddings. [Certain: checked with transformers 4.57] SigLIP (Zhai et al., 2023) is a CLIP-style dual encoder trained with a sigmoid loss instead of a softmax loss, so each image–text pair is scored on its own, as a yes/no question (session 167).

    A toy library with known answers

    To measure recall you need queries whose correct answer you know exactly. Real benchmarks such as MSR-VTT give that, but they need large downloads. So we generate a small library: a shape of one color moves across a light background for 4 to 10 seconds, and the rest of the video is empty background. The vocabulary is 5 colors × 4 shapes × 2 directions = 40 events. Each event has one caption, for example “a green circle moving from left to right”.

    • Test library: 10 videos of 64 seconds at 1 frame per second (640 frames). Each video holds 4 events; every one of the 40 events appears exactly once. The events cover 247 of the 640 seconds; they last 6.2 seconds on average.
    • Queries: the 40 captions. Each has exactly one correct moment in the whole library.
    • Training clips (only for the temporal model in Step 6): 600 short clips of 4, 8 or 16 frames, rendered with a different random seed. They never appear in the test library.

    The hard part is built in. For every object, the library holds both directions, for example a green circle moving left to right and a green circle moving right to left. A system that ignores time can find the object but must guess the direction.

    AnalogyA synthetic library is like a flight simulator. It is simpler than the real sky, but you control every event, so you can see exactly why the system fails. Once the pipeline works here, you point the same code at real videos and real captions.

    Everything is deterministic: rendering uses numpy.random.default_rng(214) (test) and default_rng(7) (training); SigLIP runs in eval mode on CPU; training uses torch.manual_seed. Running the scripts twice gave bit-identical embeddings.

    Step 1: the pipeline

    Figure 1 shows the whole system. The top row runs offline, once per video. The bottom row runs online, once per query. They meet in the index.

    Three design choices decide almost everything: the encoder (here SigLIP, frozen), the unit of retrieval (here segments of \(L\) seconds with stride \(S\)), and the pooling that turns the frames of a segment into one vector (mean, best frame, or a learned temporal model). Steps 2 to 6 build and measure each one.

    Step 2: embed frames and queries with SigLIP

    Every frame goes through the image encoder; every caption through the text encoder. We normalize both to length 1, so the dot product is the cosine similarity:

    \[ c_j = \mathbf{t}^\top \mathbf{z}_j, \qquad \lVert \mathbf{t} \rVert = \lVert \mathbf{z}_j \rVert = 1, \qquad p_j = \sigma\big(e^{t'} c_j + b\big) \tag{214.1} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\mathbf{z}_j\)
    the SigLIP embedding of frame \(j\) (768 numbers, length 1)
    \(\mathbf{t}\)
    the SigLIP embedding of the query text
    \(c_j\)
    cosine similarity of frame \(j\) and the query
    \(e^{t'}, b\)
    SigLIP’s learned scale and bias; the paper initializes \(t' = \log 10\) and \(b = -10\) [Certain]. In this checkpoint \(e^{t'} = 117.33\) and \(b = -12.93\) [Certain: read from the model]
    \(p_j\)
    the model’s probability that frame and text match; \(\sigma\) is the logistic sigmoid

    Two practical details. First, the SigLIP text encoder was trained with every caption padded to 64 tokens, and the Hugging Face documentation says to pass padding="max_length" for that reason. [Certain] With other padding you get different text vectors. Second, SigLIP cosines are small. In our library the best frame–caption cosines are about 0.15, and the whole range is about −0.07 to 0.19. That is normal: with scale 117.33 and bias −12.93, a cosine of 0.110 already gives \(p = 0.5\). Do not compare raw cosine thresholds across models.

    Worked example: what a cosine of 0.148 means

    In video 8, the segment from 52 to 60 s (the green circle moving left to right) has cosine 0.1484 with the query “a green circle moving from left to right”. Then \(117.33 \times 0.1484 - 12.93 = 4.48\) and \(\sigma(4.48) = 0.989\). The segment from 8 to 16 s shows the green circle moving the other way; its cosine is 0.1388, which gives \(p = 0.974\). Both are “yes” with high confidence. The model sees the green circle, but direction barely moves the score.

    One frame per second is enough here, because each event lasts at least 4 seconds. For real video, 1 fps is a common starting point for retrieval (CLIP4Clip also samples 1 frame per second [Certain]); fast actions need more.

    Step 3: cut segments and pool

    A segment is a window of \(L\) seconds that starts every \(S\) seconds (the stride). If \(S \lt L\), neighboring segments overlap. A video of \(D\) seconds gives

    \[ n_{\text{seg}} = \left\lfloor \frac{D - L}{S} \right\rfloor + 1 \tag{214.2} \]

    For our 64-second videos with \(L = 8, S = 4\): \(\lfloor 56/4 \rfloor + 1 = 15\) segments per video, 150 in the library. With \(L = 4, S = 2\) it is 31 per video (310 in total); with \(L = 16, S = 8\) it is 7 (70 in total).

    Mean pooling

    The simplest segment vector is the normalized mean of its frame vectors, as in CLIP4Clip’s “meanP” (session 208). Its score with a query can be written with only frame cosines and frame–frame similarities:

    \[ s_k = \mathbf{t}^\top \frac{\bar{\mathbf{z}}_k}{\lVert \bar{\mathbf{z}}_k \rVert} = \frac{\frac{1}{L}\sum_{j \in k} c_j}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{L^2} \sum_{i \in k} \sum_{j \in k} \mathbf{z}_i^\top \mathbf{z}_j}} \tag{214.3} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(k\)
    one segment; \(j \in k\) are its \(L\) frames
    \(\bar{\mathbf{z}}_k\)
    the mean of the frame vectors of segment \(k\)
    numerator
    the average frame cosine with the query
    denominator
    the length of the mean vector: near 1 if the frames look alike, smaller if they differ (then normalization lifts the score)

    Formula (214.3) is how Figure 2 computes scores in your browser: it stores the 64 × 64 frame similarity matrix of one real video and the frame cosines of six queries, and it gives exactly the same numbers as the Python code.

    Worked example: one segment score

    Segment [52, 60) of video 8 and the query “a green circle moving from left to right”. The eight frame cosines are 0.1482, 0.1486, 0.1479, 0.1479, 0.1479, 0.1456, 0.1477, 0.1495; their mean is 0.14789. The frames look almost the same to SigLIP, so the mean vector has length 0.99679. The segment score is \(0.14789 / 0.99679 = 0.1484\).

    Best-frame scoring

    A text-conditioned alternative with no training is to score a segment by its best frame, \(\max_{j \in k} c_j\). You index frames, not segments, and map each frame hit to the segments that contain it. It helps when the query names a short part of a long segment. It has the same blind spot as the mean: a single frame shows no motion.

    Algorithm 1: build the segment index
    1. input: videos, segment length \(L\), stride \(S\), image encoder \(\phi\)
    2. vectors \(\leftarrow\) [ ];  meta \(\leftarrow\) [ ]
    3. for each video \(v\) do
    4. frames \(\leftarrow\) decode \(v\) at 1 fps;  \(Z \leftarrow\) normalize(\(\phi\)(frames)) // (D, 768), batch the frames
    5. for \(a = 0, S, 2S, \dots\) while \(a + L \le D\) do // formula (214.2)
    6. \(\mathbf{x} \leftarrow\) normalize(pool(\(Z[a : a+L]\))) // mean, or the temporal model
    7. vectors.append(\(\mathbf{x}\));  meta.append((\(v\), \(a\), \(a + L\)))
    8. index \(\leftarrow\) IndexIDMap(IndexFlatIP(768));  index.add_with_ids(vectors, 0..n−1)
    9. return index, meta // save both; the index alone does not know times

    Step 4: index with FAISS and search

    FAISS (Facebook AI Similarity Search; Johnson et al., 2017) is a library for nearest-neighbor search over dense vectors. [Certain] For a library of our size we use the exact index IndexFlatIP: it stores all vectors and computes the inner product of the query with every one of them. For unit vectors, the inner product is the cosine, so the ranking is exactly formula (214.3). Wrapping it in IndexIDMap lets us attach our own 64-bit ids to the vectors; a separate table maps each id to (video, start, end).

    Algorithm 2: answer one query
    1. input: query text, index, meta, \(k\)
    2. \(\mathbf{t} \leftarrow\) normalize(SigLIP_text(query, padding = max_length))
    3. scores, ids \(\leftarrow\) index.search(\(\mathbf{t}\), \(k'\)) // \(k' \gt k\): ask for extra, overlaps will be merged
    4. hits \(\leftarrow\) [ (meta[id], score) for id, score ]
    5. for each hit in score order: drop it if it overlaps a kept hit of the same video by more than half // temporal non-maximum suppression
    6. return the first \(k\) kept hits as (video, start, end, score)

    Our evaluation in Step 5 does not merge hits, so that the numbers match the plain index. In a product you should merge: with 50% overlap, one event usually appears in two neighboring segments, and they fill two of the top 5 places.

    How big does the index get?

    \[ \text{bytes} = n_{\text{seg}} \cdot d \cdot b \tag{214.4} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(n_{\text{seg}}\)
    the number of segments in the whole library
    \(d\)
    the embedding size, 768 for SigLIP base
    \(b\)
    bytes per number: 4 for float32, 2 for float16; product quantization (PQ) stores a code of a few bytes per vector instead

    Our library: 150 segments × 768 × 4 = 460,800 bytes. Tiny. Now take 1,000 hours of video with \(L = 8, S = 4\): about 3,600,000 / 4 = 900,000 segments, which is 2.76 GB in float32, 1.38 GB in float16, or 57.6 MB with 64-byte PQ codes. A frame-level index at 1 fps would hold 3.6 million vectors, 11.06 GB in float32. These are simple products, so check them for your own numbers.

    Approximate search: IVF

    An exact index compares the query with every vector. That is fine up to a few million vectors on a CPU [Likely], but it grows linearly. An inverted file index (IVF) first clusters the vectors with k-means into nlist lists. At query time it compares the query with the nlist centroids, opens only the nprobe closest lists, and searches inside them. If the lists have similar sizes, it scans about

    \[ \text{scanned} \approx \frac{\text{nprobe}}{\text{nlist}} \cdot N \ +\ \text{nlist} \tag{214.5} \]

    vectors instead of \(N\). The price is recall of the index: a true neighbor that sits in a list you did not open is lost. In FAISS, IndexIVFFlat has nprobe = 1 by default [Certain: checked with faiss 1.13], which is often too low. Figure 4 lets you see the trade-off.

    For millions of segments you would use IVF with PQ codes (IndexIVFPQ) or a graph index such as HNSW (IndexHNSWFlat; its default efSearch is 16 in faiss 1.13 [Certain: checked]). Session session 208 explains both. The rule does not change: measure index recall against an exact search on a sample of queries before you trust the fast index.

    Step 5: measure recall@k

    For each query we sort all segments by score and find the rank \(r_i\) of the first correct segment. With overlapping segments of different lengths, “correct” needs a rule. We use: a segment is a hit for an event if they are in the same video and

    \[ \operatorname{hit}(k, e) \iff \lvert k \cap e \rvert \ \ge\ \tfrac{1}{2} \min\big(\lvert k \rvert, \lvert e \rvert\big) \tag{214.6} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(k, e\)
    a segment and the labeled event, as time intervals [start, end)
    \(\lvert k \cap e \rvert\)
    their overlap in seconds
    \(\min(\lvert k \rvert, \lvert e \rvert)\)
    the shorter of the two lengths: a long segment must contain at least half of the event; a short segment must lie at least half inside it

    Then recall at \(K\) over \(Q\) queries is

    \[ \text{R@}K = \frac{100}{Q} \sum_{i=1}^{Q} \mathbb{1}\big[r_i \le K\big] \tag{214.7} \]

    With \(Q = 40\), each query is worth 2.5 points. Change the hit rule and the numbers change, so always state it next to your results. Temporal grounding papers use a temporal IoU threshold instead (R1@IoU, session 213).

    Algorithm 3: recall@k with a hit rule
    1. input: queries with labeled events \((v_i, e_i)\), ranked id lists, meta
    2. for each query \(i\) do
    3. \(r_i \leftarrow\) 1 + position of the first id whose meta \((v, a, b)\) has \(v = v_i\) and hit\(([a, b), e_i)\) // formula (214.6)
    4. R@K \(\leftarrow 100 \cdot\) mean\((r_i \le K)\) for K = 1, 5, 10;  MdR \(\leftarrow\) median\((r_i)\) // formula (214.7)

    Results for mean pooling and best-frame scoring, with 50% overlap (\(S = L/2\)), on all 40 queries:

    Pooling\(L\)segmentsR@1R@5R@10MdR
    mean4 s31050.0100.0100.01.5
    mean8 s15045.082.597.52
    mean16 s7037.565.087.52
    best frame4 s31047.582.5100.03
    best frame8 s15040.0100.0100.02
    best frame16 s7042.5100.0100.02

    Read it in two parts. R@1 sits near 50% for both methods at every length: the system finds the object and then guesses the direction. At \(L = 8\), 17 of the 22 top-1 misses of mean pooling are the opposite-direction twin. R@5 falls with longer segments for the mean (100 → 82.5 → 65.0): empty frames dilute the event, as in the dilution formula of session 208. Best frame does not dilute, so its R@5 stays at 100% for 8 and 16 seconds.

    Step 6: a small temporal model

    Why can mean pooling never learn direction? Because the mean does not depend on the order of the frames. For any reordering \(\pi\) of the frames,

    \[ \frac{1}{L} \sum_{j=1}^{L} \mathbf{z}_{\pi(j)} = \frac{1}{L} \sum_{j=1}^{L} \mathbf{z}_j \tag{214.8} \]

    A circle moving left to right and the same circle moving right to left are the same set of frames in opposite order, so they get (almost) the same vector. Best-frame scoring is order-blind in the same way. To see motion, the pooling must know where each frame is in time.

    We use the CLIP4Clip “seqTransf” idea at a small scale: add a learned position embedding to each frame vector, run one transformer encoder layer over the frames of the segment, add the result back to the frames (a residual connection), and then mean-pool:

    \[ H = \operatorname{Enc}\big(Z + P_{1:L}\big), \qquad \mathbf{v} = \operatorname{normalize}\Big( \frac{1}{L} \sum_{j=1}^{L} (\mathbf{z}_j + \mathbf{h}_j) \Big), \qquad \mathbf{t}' = \operatorname{normalize}(\mathbf{t} + W\mathbf{t}) \tag{214.9} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(Z\)
    the \(L \times 768\) frame embeddings of one segment (SigLIP, frozen)
    \(P_{1:L}\)
    learned position embeddings, one per position, starting at zero
    \(\operatorname{Enc}\)
    one pre-norm transformer encoder layer, 8 heads, feed-forward size 1,024
    \(\mathbf{h}_j\)
    the output for frame \(j\): what it learned from the other frames, including their order
    \(W\)
    a 768 × 768 linear layer on the text side, initialized to zero, so \(\mathbf{t}' = \mathbf{t}\) at the start

    Why also adapt the text? The SigLIP text vectors of “… moving from left to right” and “… moving from right to left” have cosine 0.993 for the red circle. If the text side cannot separate them, a perfect video side does not help. The small residual \(W\) lets training stretch that difference.

    AnalogyMean pooling is like putting all the photos of a trip in a bag: you know what you saw, not in which order. The position embeddings are page numbers in a photo album. With page numbers, “first the beach, then the city” and “first the city, then the beach” become different stories.

    The loss

    Each training batch has \(B = 32\) clips of 32 different events with their captions. Choosing different events matters: two clips of the same event in one batch would count as negatives for each other (a false negative). We use the symmetric contrastive loss with temperature \(\tau = 0.05\):

    \[ \mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2B} \sum_{i=1}^{B} \left[ -\log \frac{e^{\mathbf{v}_i^\top \mathbf{t}'_i/\tau}}{\sum_{j} e^{\mathbf{v}_i^\top \mathbf{t}'_j/\tau}} \ -\ \log \frac{e^{\mathbf{v}_i^\top \mathbf{t}'_i/\tau}}{\sum_{j} e^{\mathbf{v}_j^\top \mathbf{t}'_i/\tau}} \right] \tag{214.10} \]
    Read the symbols
    first term
    video → text: each clip must pick its own caption among the 32 captions
    second term
    text → video: each caption must pick its own clip among the 32 clips. This is exactly the retrieval task
    \(\tau\)
    the temperature: smaller values make the softmax sharper

    Training runs 1,500 steps of AdamW (learning rate \(3 \cdot 10^{-4}\), weight decay 0.01), with the segment length drawn from {4, 8, 16} each step. It takes about 1.5 minutes on a laptop CPU, because SigLIP is frozen and its features are computed once.

    How stable is this? Three seeds

    We trained each variant with seeds 0, 1 and 2 (only the model initialization, dropout and batch order change). R@1 in percent:

    Three conclusions are safe. (1) The temporal model beats mean pooling in all 18 runs, by 5 to 50 points of R@1. (2) On average the symmetric loss is better than the video→text loss alone at every length, but single runs overlap: at \(L = 8\) the best v→t run (80.0) equals the worst symmetric run (80.0). (3) The spread between seeds is up to 15 points. With 40 queries, the 95% confidence interval of a recall near 85% is about ±11 points (\(1.96\sqrt{0.85 \cdot 0.15 / 40} = 0.111\)). A real comparison needs more queries and several seeds.

    Why should the text→video term help? Retrieval compares one caption against many segments. The video→text term only teaches each clip to prefer its caption over other captions; the scores of different clips for the same caption are never compared, so they need not be calibrated against each other. The text→video term trains exactly that comparison. [Likely: this is the usual explanation; our three seeds support it on average but do not prove it.]

    Watch outThe temporal model was trained on the same 40 captions it is tested on (on different renders). That measures whether it learned to see direction, not whether it generalizes to new words. On real data, keep test videos and test captions out of training, and compare against zero-shot mean pooling on the same split.

    The full program

    Three files. build_library.py renders the videos and runs SigLIP (about 20 minutes on a laptop CPU, mostly the 5,628 training frames). search.py builds FAISS indexes and computes recall; it imports only numpy and faiss. temporal.py trains the temporal model and writes its segment and query vectors. Keep FAISS and PyTorch in separate processes on macOS: with the pip wheels we used (torch 2.8, faiss-cpu 1.13), importing both in one process aborted with “OMP: Error #15” (two copies of the OpenMP runtime).     # build_library.py: render a toy video library, embed every frame and query with SigLIP
import itertools, json, numpy as np, torch
from PIL import Image, ImageDraw
from transformers import AutoModel, AutoProcessor
torch.manual_seed(0)
COLORS = {"red": (220, 30, 30), "green": (30, 160, 40), "blue": (30, 60, 220),
          "yellow": (240, 210, 20), "purple": (140, 40, 180)}
SHAPES = ["circle", "square", "triangle", "star"]
EVENTS = [(c, s, d) for c in COLORS for s in SHAPES for d in ("lr", "rl")]      # 40 events
BGS = [(235, 235, 235), (225, 235, 245), (245, 235, 220), (230, 245, 230), (240, 228, 240)]
def caption(c, s, d):
    return f"a {c} {s} moving from " + ("left to right" if d == "lr" else "right to left")

def frame(rng, bg, shape=None, color=None, x=0.5, size=0.18):     # one 224x224 RGB frame
    a = np.clip(np.array(bg)[None, None] + rng.normal(0, 6, (224, 224, 3)), 0, 255)
    im = Image.fromarray(a.astype(np.uint8)); dr = ImageDraw.Draw(im)
    cx, cy, r, col = 224 * x, 112, 224 * size, COLORS.get(color)
    if shape == "circle":   dr.ellipse([cx - r, cy - r, cx + r, cy + r], fill=col)
    if shape == "square":   dr.rectangle([cx - r, cy - r, cx + r, cy + r], fill=col)
    if shape == "triangle": dr.polygon([(cx, cy - r), (cx - r, cy + r), (cx + r, cy + r)], fill=col)
    if shape == "star":
        pts = [(cx + (r if k % 2 == 0 else 0.45 * r) * np.cos(np.pi / 2 + k * np.pi / 5),
                cy - (r if k % 2 == 0 else 0.45 * r) * np.sin(np.pi / 2 + k * np.pi / 5)) for k in range(10)]
        dr.polygon(pts, fill=col)
    return im

def event_frames(rng, c, s, d, dur, bg):          # 1 frame per second, the shape crosses the screen
    size = rng.uniform(0.12, 0.2)
    xs = np.linspace(0.2, 0.8, dur)
    return [frame(rng, bg, s, c, x, size) for x in (xs if d == "lr" else xs[::-1])]

def make_video(rng, events, D=64):                # D seconds = D frames at 1 fps
    bg = BGS[rng.integers(len(BGS))]
    durs = [int(rng.integers(4, 11)) for _ in events]                      # 4 to 10 s per event
    free = D - sum(durs)
    gaps = np.diff(np.concatenate([[0], np.sort(rng.integers(0, free + 1, len(events))), [free]]))
    frames, spans = [], []
    for k, e in enumerate(events):
        frames += [frame(rng, bg) for _ in range(gaps[k])]                 # empty background
        st = len(frames); frames += event_frames(rng, *e, durs[k], bg)
        spans.append((st, len(frames)))                                     # [start, end) in seconds
    frames += [frame(rng, bg) for _ in range(gaps[-1])]
    return frames, spans

model = AutoModel.from_pretrained("google/siglip-base-patch16-224").eval()
proc = AutoProcessor.from_pretrained("google/siglip-base-patch16-224")
@torch.no_grad()
def embed_images(ims, bs=64):
    z = torch.cat([model.get_image_features(**proc(images=ims[i:i + bs], return_tensors="pt"))
                   for i in range(0, len(ims), bs)])
    return torch.nn.functional.normalize(z, dim=-1).numpy()                # (n, 768), unit length
@torch.no_grad()
def embed_texts(texts):                           # SigLIP was trained with padding="max_length" (64 tokens)
    t = model.get_text_features(**proc(text=texts, padding="max_length", return_tensors="pt"))
    return torch.nn.functional.normalize(t, dim=-1).numpy()

if __name__ == "__main__":
    rng = np.random.default_rng(214)              # test library: 10 videos, 4 events each, all 40 events once
    perm = rng.permutation(len(EVENTS)); Z, meta = [], []
    for v in range(10):
        evs = [EVENTS[i] for i in perm[4 * v:4 * v + 4]]
        frames, spans = make_video(rng, evs)
        Z.append(embed_images(frames)); meta.append({"events": evs, "spans": spans})
    T = embed_texts([caption(*e) for e in EVENTS])
    np.savez("library.npz", Z=np.stack(Z), T=T)                            # Z: (10, 64, 768), T: (40, 768)
    json.dump({"meta": meta, "events": EVENTS}, open("library.json", "w"), default=int)
    rng = np.random.default_rng(7)                # training clips: other renders, never seen at test time

import json, sys, numpy as np, faiss
lib = np.load("library.npz"); J = json.load(open("library.json"))
Z, T = lib["Z"].astype("float32"), lib["T"].astype("float32")          # (10, 64, 768), (40, 768)
EVENTS = [tuple(e) for e in J["events"]]
gt = {tuple(e): (v, tuple(sp)) for v, m in enumerate(J["meta"]) for e, sp in zip(m["events"], m["spans"])}

def segments(D, L, S):                            # windows [a, a+L) with stride S
    return [(a, a + L) for a in range(0, D - L + 1, S)]

def is_hit(seg, ev):                              # overlap >= half of the shorter interval
    inter = max(0, min(seg[1], ev[1]) - max(seg[0], ev[0]))
    return inter >= 0.5 * min(seg[1] - seg[0], ev[1] - ev[0])

def build_index(vecs, ids):
    index = faiss.IndexIDMap(faiss.IndexFlatIP(vecs.shape[1]))         # exact inner product
    index.add_with_ids(vecs, np.asarray(ids, dtype="int64"))
    return index

def recall(rank_lists, segs_of_id, ks=(1, 5, 10)):
    ranks = []
    for e, ids in zip(EVENTS, rank_lists):
        v, ev = gt[e]
        ranks.append(next(r + 1 for r, i in enumerate(ids) if segs_of_id[i][0] == v and is_hit(segs_of_id[i][1:], ev)))
    ranks = np.array(ranks)
    return {f"R@{k}": 100 * float(np.mean(ranks <= k)) for k in ks} | {"MdR": float(np.median(ranks))}, ranks

def run(L, S, method, seg_vecs=None, queries=None):
    segs_of_id = [(v, a, b) for v in range(Z.shape[0]) for a, b in segments(Z.shape[1], L, S)]
    if method == "mean":                          # one vector per segment: the normalized mean frame
        X = np.stack([Z[v, a:b].mean(0) for v, a, b in segs_of_id]); faiss.normalize_L2(X)
        index = build_index(X, range(len(segs_of_id)))
        _, I = index.search(T, len(segs_of_id))
    elif method == "maxframe":                    # index frames, a segment scores its best frame
        index = build_index(Z.reshape(-1, Z.shape[2]), range(Z.shape[0] * Z.shape[1]))
        Sf, If = index.search(T, Z.shape[0] * Z.shape[1])
        I = []
        for q in range(len(T)):
            best = {}
            for s, f in zip(Sf[q], If[q]):        # frames come best first
                v, t = divmod(int(f), Z.shape[1])
                for j, (vv, a, b) in enumerate(segs_of_id):
                    if vv == v and a <= t < b and j not in best: best[j] = s
            I.append(sorted(best, key=lambda j: -best[j]))
    else:                                         # vectors written by temporal.py
        index = build_index(seg_vecs, range(len(segs_of_id)))
        _, I = index.search(queries, len(segs_of_id))
    return recall(I, segs_of_id)

import json, sys, numpy as np, torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F
SEED = int(sys.argv[1]) if len(sys.argv) > 1 else 0
SYMMETRIC = (sys.argv[2] != "v2t") if len(sys.argv) > 2 else True
torch.manual_seed(SEED); np.random.seed(SEED)
lib = np.load("library.npz"); tr = np.load("train.npz")
Z, T = torch.tensor(lib["Z"]), torch.tensor(lib["T"])                    # frozen SigLIP features
y = tr["y"]; clips = [tr[f"c{n}"] for n in range(len(y))]

class Temporal(nn.Module):                        # video side: frames -> one segment vector
    def __init__(self, d=768, max_len=32):
        super().__init__()
        self.pos = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(max_len, d))                   # learned position embeddings
        layer = nn.TransformerEncoderLayer(d, 8, 1024, dropout=0.1, batch_first=True, norm_first=True)
        self.enc = nn.TransformerEncoder(layer, num_layers=1)
    def forward(self, z):                         # z: (B, L, d)
        h = self.enc(z + self.pos[: z.shape[1]])
        return F.normalize((z + h).mean(1), dim=-1)                        # residual, then mean pool

class TextAdapter(nn.Module):                     # text side: t + W t, starts as the identity
    def __init__(self, d=768):
        super().__init__(); self.lin = nn.Linear(d, d)
        nn.init.zeros_(self.lin.weight); nn.init.zeros_(self.lin.bias)
    def forward(self, t): return F.normalize(t + self.lin(t), dim=-1)

vm, tm = Temporal(), TextAdapter()
opt = torch.optim.AdamW([*vm.parameters(), *tm.parameters()], lr=3e-4, weight_decay=0.01)
by = {}                                           # (event, length) -> clip indices
for i, c in enumerate(clips): by.setdefault((int(y[i]), len(c)), []).append(i)
tau = 0.05
for step in range(1500):
    L = int(np.random.choice([4, 8, 16]))
    evs = [e for e in range(len(T)) if (e, L) in by]
    evs = list(np.random.choice(evs, min(32, len(evs)), replace=False))   # 32 DIFFERENT events: no false negatives
    z = torch.tensor(np.stack([clips[int(np.random.choice(by[(e, L)]))] for e in evs]))
    logits = vm(z) @ tm(T[evs]).T / tau           # (32 clips, 32 captions)
    target = torch.arange(len(evs))
    loss = F.cross_entropy(logits, target)        # video -> text
    if SYMMETRIC:
        loss = (loss + F.cross_entropy(logits.T, target)) / 2              # + text -> video
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

vm.eval(); tm.eval(); out = {}

    Common mistakes

    • Forgetting to normalize. IndexFlatIP ranks by inner product. Without unit vectors, long vectors win, and a pooled mean is shorter than its frames. Normalize after pooling, and normalize queries.
    • Default text padding with SigLIP. The model was trained with captions padded to 64 tokens; use padding="max_length".
    • Using a fixed cosine threshold from another model. SigLIP cosines of about 0.15 are strong matches here; CLIP cosines live in a different range. Calibrate thresholds per model, or use ranks.
    • Losing the metadata. A FAISS id means nothing without the table that maps it to (video, start, end). Save both together, and rebuild both together.
    • No hit rule, or an unreported one. With overlapping segments, “correct” must be defined (formula 214.6). Different rules give different R@k.
    • Trusting one run on a small test set. 40 queries give ±11 points of noise at 85%; seeds moved our R@1 by up to 15 points.
    • Only one half of the contrastive loss, or repeated events in a batch. Train text→video as well, and keep false negatives out of the batch.
    • Measuring an approximate index without an exact baseline. IVF with the default nprobe = 1 lost about 29% of the true top-10 in Figure 4.

    Interview questions

    Why can mean pooling of frame embeddings not tell “left to right” from “right to left”?basic
    The mean is invariant to the order of the frames (formula 214.8). The two motions show the same set of frames in opposite order, so they give almost the same vector. Any order-free pooling (mean, max, best frame) has this blind spot. In our lab, 17 of the 22 top-1 misses of mean pooling at 8-second segments were the opposite-direction twin. You need position information: a temporal model over frames, or a video encoder with temporal attention.
    You use FAISS IndexFlatIP. What must be true about the vectors for this to be cosine search?basic
    Both the stored vectors and the query must have length 1. Then the inner product equals the cosine. If you mean-pool frames, the mean is shorter than 1 (less so when frames look alike), so normalize after pooling, not only before. faiss.normalize_L2 does it in place on a float32 array.
    How do you choose the segment length L and the stride S?core
    Match L to the length of the events people search for and to the clip length the encoder was trained on. Too long dilutes short events: in our lab, mean-pooling R@5 dropped from 100% at 4 s to 65% at 16 s. Too short multiplies the index (formula 214.2), loses context for actions, and floods the results with near-duplicates. Overlap (S = L/2 is common) avoids cutting events at a boundary but doubles the index. Cutting at shot boundaries is a good alternative. Measure R@k on a labeled set for two or three choices.
    What exactly does R@5 = 82.5% mean in this lab?core
    For 33 of the 40 queries, at least one of the top 5 segments is a hit, where hit means same video and overlap of at least half of the shorter interval (formula 214.6). It depends on the gallery (150 segments here), on the hit rule, and on overlap: with S = L/2 one event often has two hit segments, while its twin also has two, so they compete for the top places. Always report gallery size and hit rule with R@k.
    SigLIP gives a cosine of 0.15 for a perfect match. Is something wrong?core
    No. SigLIP is trained with a sigmoid loss and a learned scale and bias. In this checkpoint the scale is 117.33 and the bias −12.93, so \(p = \sigma(117.33 \cdot 0.15 - 12.93) \approx 0.99\), and a cosine of 0.110 already means \(p = 0.5\). Ranking only needs the order of the scores. If you need a yes/no threshold, apply the scale and bias, and calibrate on labeled data.
    Your temporal model gets 95% R@1 with one seed. Is it better than mean pooling? Is the symmetric loss better than video→text only?advanced
    Better than mean pooling: yes, in all 18 runs, by 5 to 50 points. Symmetric vs v→t: probably, because it wins on average at every length (85.8 vs 65.0 at 8 s), but single runs overlap and seeds move R@1 by up to 15 points. With 40 queries the 95% interval near 85% is about ±11 points. To be sure: more queries, several seeds, a paired test on the same queries, and report mean and spread.
    Why should the text→video term of the contrastive loss matter for retrieval?advanced
    Retrieval ranks many segments for one caption. The video→text term only compares captions for a fixed clip, so it never forces the scores of different clips for the same caption to be comparable. The text→video term trains exactly that comparison: for caption i, clip i must beat the other clips. CLIP and CLIP4Clip use both terms. [Likely] Also keep repeated events out of a batch, or their clips become false negatives.
    Design the index for 1,000 hours of video. What sizes and choices?advanced
    With 8-second segments and 4-second stride, about 900,000 segments. With 768-d float32 that is 2.76 GB, 1.38 GB in float16: an exact flat index still fits in RAM on one machine. For 100× more video, use IVF-PQ (for example 64-byte codes: 57.6 MB per 900,000 segments) or HNSW, tune nprobe or efSearch against an exact baseline on sample queries, and re-rank the top candidates with exact vectors or a VLM. Store (video, start, end) per id, merge overlapping hits, and update the index incrementally as new videos arrive.
    Best-frame scoring or mean pooling?core
    Best frame (index frames, score a segment or video by its top frame) is text-conditioned and does not dilute short events: in our lab its R@5 stayed at 100% for 8- and 16-second segments while the mean fell to 82.5% and 65%. But it indexes every frame (more memory), it is noisy on single bad frames, it ties overlapping segments that share the best frame, and it is as order-blind as the mean. Mean pooling is better when the query describes the whole segment.
    Your offline R@k looks great, but users complain. What do you check?core
    That the test set looks like real queries (length, vocabulary, language) and real videos (length, domain); that test captions and videos were not in training; that the index in production is the same one you evaluated (same encoder version, same normalization, same nprobe); that overlapping hits are merged so the result page is not five copies of one event; and whether users need moments that a fixed segment grid cuts in half. Then add a labeled set from real query logs.

    Key takeaways

    • A text-to-video system is: frames → SigLIP vectors → segments (214.2) → pooled, normalized vectors → FAISS index with a metadata table → text query → ranked moments.
    • For unit vectors, IndexFlatIP is exact cosine search; mean-pooled segment scores follow formula (214.3). Index memory is \(n_{\text{seg}} \cdot d \cdot b\) (214.4); IVF scans about nprobe/nlist of it (214.5) at a measurable loss of recall.
    • R@k needs a stated hit rule (214.6, 214.7). With 40 queries, each query is 2.5 points and noise is about ±11 points.
    • Mean and best-frame pooling are order-blind (214.8): on our library they find the object but guess the direction (R@1 45.0% and 40.0% at 8 s).
    • A one-layer temporal transformer with position embeddings over frozen SigLIP frames (214.9), trained with the symmetric loss (214.10), raised R@1 to 85.8% on average over three seeds.
