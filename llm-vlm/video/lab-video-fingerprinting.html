    video fingerprinting

    In this lab you build a video copy detector on a laptop and then attack it. You cut 45 reference clips and 630 attacked queries from three open movies, describe every frame with a perceptual hash, CLIP and the SSCD copy-detection model, match videos with a temporal line search that also finds speed and location, and measure everything with micro-AP. Then you find the weakest transform of each descriptor, fix it, and see where the weakness moves.

    Time: 3 to 4 hours (mostly hands-on; computing the descriptors takes about 20 minutes on a CPU)Level: Intermediate, hands-on, interview levelBefore this: Session 209 (video fingerprinting); Session 208 and Session 166 (CLIP) help; Python, PyTorch and FFmpeg basics

    What you will learn

    • How to turn a video into a sequence of frame descriptors: a 64-bit perceptual hash, a CLIP embedding and an SSCD copy-detection descriptor
    • How to compare two videos with a frame similarity matrix, and how max, Chamfer and a temporal line search turn it into one score
    • How a line search over speeds and offsets handles sped-up copies and reports where the copy sits in the reference
    • How to measure copy detection honestly with micro-AP over all pairs, with distractor queries and one global threshold
    • How to attack your own system (crop, resize, re-encode, speed, logo, flip, combinations) at several strengths and find the weakest transform
    • Why score normalization and descriptor fusion help in some settings and hurt in others
    • Why many false alarms are a label-policy question (the same shot a few seconds later), not a descriptor bug

    Why this matters

    In session 209 you saw the ideas: a fingerprint is a compact description of a video that stays the same when someone crops, shrinks, re-encodes, speeds up or brands the video, but changes when the content changes. In this lab you build one, end to end, and then you attack it. You will write per-frame descriptors, a temporal matcher that finds where a copy sits in time and at what speed, and an evaluation that gives one honest number, micro-AP. Then you apply seven attacks of your own, measure every one of them, find the weakest, fix it, and find the next weakest.

    The task is called video copy detection: given a query video, find the reference videos that it was copied from. It is not the same as semantic search (session 208). Two different videos of a rabbit in a meadow are semantically similar but are not copies. A re-uploaded clip with a TV logo, a black-and-white filter and half the bitrate is a copy, even though it looks worse. Platforms run this at very large scale: to match uploads against a catalog of protected content, to find re-uploads of content that was already removed, and to remove near-duplicate clips from training data (session 187).

    Everything here runs on a laptop CPU. The data is cut from three open movies of the Blender Foundation, the descriptors are a 64-bit perceptual hash, CLIP ViT-B/32 and SSCD (a ResNet-50 trained for image copy detection), and every number on this page comes from our own run of the code below, unless it is tagged as coming from a paper. In short, what you will find: the copy-detection descriptor (SSCD) beats the semantic one (CLIP) by a wide margin (micro-AP 0.931 against 0.478); the perceptual hash breaks completely under crop and mirror; SSCD’s weakest transform is heavy re-encoding; a simple fusion with the hash fixes that (0.969); and most of the remaining false alarms are not copies at all but the same scene a few seconds later.

    Interview tip“Design a system that finds re-uploads of a removed video” is a common system-design question. The strong answer has the parts of this lab: frame sampling, a robust per-frame descriptor, a vector index, a temporal check that also returns the matching segment, an evaluation with many distractors (micro-AP), and a list of attacks you have measured, with the weakest one named.

    Step 1: three per-frame descriptors

    A descriptor is a fixed-size vector (or bit string) computed from one frame. We compare three kinds, because they fail in different ways.

    Perceptual hash (pHash). A hand-made 64-bit code. Convert the frame to gray, shrink it to 32 × 32 pixels, take the two-dimensional discrete cosine transform (DCT, the transform JPEG uses), keep the 8 × 8 lowest frequencies, and write one bit per coefficient: 1 if it is above the median of the 64, else 0. Low frequencies describe the coarse layout of light and dark, which survives resizing and compression. We compare two hashes by their Hamming distance \(d_H\), the number of bits that differ, and turn it into a similarity between −1 and 1:

    \[ s_{\text{pHash}}(\mathbf{h}, \mathbf{h}') = 1 - \frac{2\, d_H(\mathbf{h}, \mathbf{h}')}{64} = \frac{1}{64}\, \tilde{\mathbf{h}}^\top \tilde{\mathbf{h}}', \qquad \tilde{\mathbf{h}} = 2\mathbf{h} - 1 \in \{-1, +1\}^{64} \tag{216.1} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\mathbf{h}, \mathbf{h}'\)
    two 64-bit hashes (bits 0 or 1)
    \(d_H\)
    Hamming distance: the number of positions where the bits differ (0 to 64)
    \(\tilde{\mathbf{h}}\)
    the same hash written with −1 and +1; then the similarity is a scaled dot product, so the same matcher code works for hashes and for float vectors
    \(s_{\text{pHash}}\)
    1 for identical hashes, about 0 for unrelated frames (32 of 64 bits differ by chance), −1 for exactly opposite hashes

    CLIP ViT-B/32 (session 166): a 512-dimensional image embedding trained to match images with captions. It knows what is in a frame (a rabbit, a meadow), not which exact frame it is. We include it as the semantic baseline, because many teams try it first.

    SSCD (Pizzi et al., 2022): a ResNet-50 that outputs a 512-dimensional descriptor trained for image copy detection [Certain]. It is trained with a contrastive self-supervised loss: two strongly edited versions of the same image must be close, and all other images far apart. The paper changes the SimCLR recipe for this task and adds an entropy regularization term that spreads the descriptors out in the space, so that unrelated images are not accidentally close [Certain]. Its training edits are close to our attacks: crops, resizes, overlays, blur, JPEG compression and more [Likely]. We use the sscd_disc_mixup model, which was trained on the DISC21 image copy-detection dataset, with the preprocessing that the authors recommend: resize the short side to 288 pixels and normalize with the ImageNet mean and standard deviation [Certain].

    AnalogyCLIP is a librarian who sorts books by topic: two different books about rabbits end up on the same shelf. SSCD is a detective who checks whether two pages are the same printed page, even if one was photocopied, cut and stamped. pHash is a very fast clerk who only compares the shape of the dark and light areas: he is not fooled by a bad photocopy, but a page cut in half or held up to a mirror confuses him completely.
    Algorithm 1: describe a video (frame sampling and descriptors)
    1. input: a video file, a frame rate \(f\) (2 per second here), a descriptor model
    2. decode the frames at times \(0, 1/f, 2/f, \dots\) // FFmpeg fps filter
    3. for each frame: preprocess exactly as the descriptor was trained // SSCD: short side 288, ImageNet normalization
    4.   pHash: gray → 32 × 32 → 2-D DCT → 8 × 8 low frequencies → 64 bits (above the median)
    5.   CLIP or SSCD: one forward pass → 512 numbers → divide by the length (unit vector)
    6. return the matrix of frame descriptors, one row per sampled frame, in time order // keep the order: the matcher needs it
    # embed.py: per-frame descriptors for every reference clip and query (pHash, CLIP, SSCD)
import glob, json, numpy as np, torch, torch.nn.functional as F
from PIL import Image
from scipy.fft import dctn
from torchvision import transforms as T
from transformers import CLIPModel, CLIPImageProcessor
torch.manual_seed(0); torch.set_grad_enabled(False)

def phash(im):                                   # 64-bit perceptual hash, as in the imagehash library
    g = np.asarray(im.convert("L").resize((32, 32), Image.LANCZOS), dtype=np.float64)
    low = dctn(g, type=2, norm="ortho")[:8, :8]    # the 8 x 8 lowest frequencies
    return (low > np.median(low)).flatten()        # 64 bits

sscd = torch.jit.load("data/sscd.pt").eval()      # ResNet-50, 512-d
sscd_tf = T.Compose([T.Resize(288), T.ToTensor(), T.Normalize([0.485, 0.456, 0.406], [0.229, 0.224, 0.225])])
clip = CLIPModel.from_pretrained("openai/clip-vit-base-patch32").eval()
clip_pp = CLIPImageProcessor.from_pretrained("openai/clip-vit-base-patch32")

def describe(frame_dir):
    ims = [Image.open(p).convert("RGB") for p in sorted(glob.glob(frame_dir + "/*.png"))]
    h = np.stack([phash(im) for im in ims])
    s = F.normalize(sscd(torch.stack([sscd_tf(im) for im in ims])), dim=-1)
    c = clip.get_image_features(pixel_values=clip_pp(images=ims, return_tensors="pt")["pixel_values"])
    c = F.normalize(c if isinstance(c, torch.Tensor) else c.pooler_output, dim=-1)
    return h, s.numpy(), c.numpy()

meta = json.load(open("work/meta.json")); out = {}
for kind in ("ref", "qry"):
    for v in meta[kind]:
        out[v["id"]] = describe(f"work/{kind}/{v['id']}_f")
np.savez("work/desc.npz", **{f"{k}|{n}": a for k, (h, s, c) in out.items()
                             for n, a in (("phash", h), ("sscd", s), ("clip", c))})

    Step 2: the similarity matrix and a temporal matcher

    A video is a sequence of frame descriptors, so comparing two videos means comparing two sequences. The basic object is the frame similarity matrix: one row per query frame, one column per reference frame.

    \[ S_{ij} = \mathbf{q}_i^\top \mathbf{r}_j, \qquad i = 0, \dots, n_q - 1, \quad j = 0, \dots, n_r - 1 \tag{216.2} \]

    If the query is a copy of part of the reference, played at speed \(\rho\), its frame \(i\) matches reference frame \(o + \rho\, i\) for some start offset \(o\). So a copy shows up as a straight line of high values in \(S\), with slope \(\rho\). We compare four ways to turn \(S\) into one video score:

    \[ \text{max} = \max_{i,j} S_{ij}, \qquad \text{Chamfer} = \frac{1}{n_q} \sum_{i=0}^{n_q-1} \max_{j} S_{ij} \tag{216.3} \]
    \[ L(\rho, o) = \frac{1}{|I_{\rho,o}|} \sum_{i \in I_{\rho,o}} S_{i,\, \lfloor o + \rho i \rceil}, \qquad \text{temporal} = \max_{\rho \in \mathcal{P}} \; \max_{o} \; L(\rho, o) \tag{216.4} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\mathbf{q}_i, \mathbf{r}_j\)
    descriptor of query frame \(i\) and of reference frame \(j\) (unit vectors, or ±1 hash vectors scaled as in 216.1)
    \(n_q, n_r\)
    number of sampled frames in the query (12, or 8 at 1.5×) and in the reference (20)
    max
    the single best frame pair: the rule of the VSC 2022 descriptor track [Certain]; one lucky frame decides
    Chamfer
    every query frame finds its best reference frame anywhere, and we average; the order of frames is ignored. ViSiL uses Chamfer similarity to aggregate [Certain]
    \(\rho, o\)
    a speed (slope) and a start offset in reference frames; \(\lfloor x \rceil\) rounds to the nearest integer (halves go up)
    \(I_{\rho,o}\)
    the query frames whose matching reference frame falls inside the reference; we require at least half of \(\min(n_q, n_r/\rho)\) frames, so a line that just touches a corner cannot win
    \(\mathcal{P}\)
    the speeds we try: \(\{0.5, \tfrac{2}{3}, 1, 1.5, 2\}\); “temporal, \(\rho = 1\)” tries only normal speed
    pool (not in the formula)
    the simplest baseline: average all frame descriptors of a video into one vector and take a cosine; no matrix at all

    The temporal matcher is a small version of temporal Hough voting, an idea from earlier video copy-detection systems (for example Douze, Jégou and Schmid, 2010 [Likely]): every (speed, offset) pair is a candidate line, and the line with the highest average similarity wins. It gives two things the other scores cannot give: the speed \(\rho\) and the location \(o\) of the copy inside the reference. Learned matchers such as ViSiL go further: they run a small CNN over \(S\) to learn which patterns (lines, broken lines, blocks) mean “copy” [Certain]; the VSC 2022 baseline localizes copies with a temporal network over \(S\) [Certain].

    Algorithm 2: temporal line matcher
    1. input: similarity matrix \(S\) of shape \((n_q, n_r)\), speeds \(\mathcal{P}\), minimum coverage \(c = 0.5\)
    2. best \(\leftarrow (-\infty, \text{none}, \text{none})\)
    3. for each \(\rho \in \mathcal{P}\):
    4.   for each integer offset \(o\) from \(-\lceil \rho (n_q - 1) \rceil\) to \(n_r - 1\): // vectorized in practice
    5.     \(j_i \leftarrow \lfloor o + \rho i \rceil\) for every query frame \(i\); keep the \(i\) with \(0 \le j_i \lt n_r\)
    6.     if fewer than \(c \cdot \min(n_q, n_r / \rho)\) frames are kept: skip
    7.     \(L \leftarrow\) mean of \(S_{i, j_i}\) over the kept frames // formula (216.4)
    8.     if \(L \gt\) best score: best \(\leftarrow (L, \rho, o)\)
    9. return best // score, speed, and where the copy starts in the reference
    # fp.py: the matchers and micro-AP
import numpy as np, torch
RHOS = [0.5, 2 / 3, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0]

def to_mat(x, d):                         # pHash bits -> +-1/8, so a dot product = 1 - 2 * Hamming / 64
    x = torch.from_numpy(np.asarray(x, dtype=np.float32))
    return (2 * x - 1) / 8.0 if d == "phash" else x

def temporal(S, rhos=RHOS, min_cover=0.5):   # best line j = o + rho * i through S, formula (216.4)
    nq, nr = S.shape; i = torch.arange(nq, dtype=torch.float32); best = (-9.0, None, None)
    for rho in rhos:
        o = torch.arange(-int(rho * (nq - 1)) - 1, nr)                    # every integer offset
        J = torch.floor(o[:, None] + rho * i[None, :] + 0.5).long()      # round half up (torch.round rounds half to even)
        ok = (J >= 0) & (J < nr)
        vals = S[i.long()[None, :].expand_as(J), J.clamp(0, nr - 1)] * ok
        n = ok.sum(1); sc = vals.sum(1) / n.clamp(min=1)
        sc[n < min_cover * min(nq, nr / rho)] = -9.0                       # the line must cover enough frames
        k = int(sc.argmax())
        if sc[k] > best[0]: best = (float(sc[k]), rho, int(o[k]))
    return best

def video_scores(S):
    t = temporal(S)
    return dict(maxmax=S.max().item(), chamfer=S.max(1).values.mean().item(),
                temporal1=temporal(S, rhos=[1.0])[0], temporal=t[0], rho=t[1], off=t[2])

def ap(y, s):                             # micro-AP of one ranked list of (query, reference) pairs, formula (216.5)
    o = np.argsort(-np.asarray(s), kind="stable"); y = np.asarray(y, float)[o]
    prec = np.cumsum(y) / np.arange(1, len(y) + 1)
    return float((prec * y).sum() / y.sum())

# quick self-test: a perfect line with slope 1.5 starting at reference frame 4
S = torch.zeros(8, 20)
for i in range(8): S[i, int(np.floor(4 + 1.5 * i + 0.5))] = 1.0
print(temporal(S), temporal(S, rhos=[1.0]))   # (1.0, 1.5, 4) (0.375, 1.0, 5)

With \(\rho = 1.5\) half values appear on every second frame, so the page’s figure and the Python code disagreed until both used “add 0.5, then floor”.

    Worked example: finding a 1.5× copy (Sintel clip 24, SSCD)

    The speed query has 8 frames. With the true line \(\rho = 1.5\), \(o = 4\), the reference frames are \(\lfloor 4 + 1.5 i \rceil\) for \(i = 0..7\): 4, 6, 7, 9, 10, 12, 13, 15. The similarities on these cells (rounded to two decimals, as Figure 2 shows them) are 0.95, 0.71, 0.93, 0.90, 0.97, 0.94, 0.97 and 0.82. Their mean is \(7.19 / 8 = 0.899\): this is \(L(1.5, 4)\).

    A matcher that only tries normal speed must use \(\rho = 1\). Its line \(o = 4\) visits frames 4 to 11; after the first few frames it falls behind the action, and the mean drops to 0.565. Its best offset is \(o = 5\) with 0.751. So without the speed search the score of a true copy falls from 0.899 to 0.751, and the estimated location is wrong. The Chamfer score of this pair is 0.905, a little higher than the line, because each query frame may pick its best reference frame freely, with no line constraint.

    Step 3: score every pair and measure micro-AP

    A copy-detection system outputs a list of (query, reference, score) predictions, and the product sets one threshold: everything above it is reported as a copy. The right metric therefore ranks all predictions of all queries together, not each query separately. Sort the predictions by score, walk down the list, and at each position \(k\) compute the precision \(P(k)\) (the fraction of the first \(k\) predictions that are true copies) and the recall \(R(k)\) (the fraction of all true copies found so far). The area under this precision–recall curve is the micro average precision:

    \[ \mu\mathrm{AP} = \sum_{k=1}^{N} P(k)\, \big(R(k) - R(k-1)\big) = \frac{1}{N_{+}} \sum_{k\,:\, \text{pair } k \text{ is a copy}} P(k) \tag{216.5} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(N\)
    the number of predictions; here every (query, reference) pair: \(630 \times 45 = 28{,}350\)
    \(N_{+}\)
    the number of true copy pairs: 315 (every non-distractor query has exactly one source)
    \(P(k), R(k)\)
    precision and recall of the top \(k\) predictions; \(R\) only grows at a true copy, which gives the right-hand form

    This is the metric of the DISC21 image challenge and of the VSC 2022 video challenge, where it is called micro-AP (µAP) [Certain]. It punishes two things that per-query metrics hide. First, calibration: a score of 0.5 must mean the same for every query, because one threshold serves all of them. Second, distractors: a distractor query has no true match, so every prediction it makes can only lower precision [Certain]. In VSC 2022 most queries are distractors, like in real upload traffic [Certain].

    Each descriptor has a different weakest transform, and each failure has a clear reason:

    • pHash: crop and flip. The hash describes where light and dark areas sit in the full frame. A crop moves and scales everything; a mirror swaps left and right. Both change about half of the 64 bits, like an unrelated frame. But pHash is perfect on re-encoding: block artifacts are high-frequency noise, and the hash keeps only the 8 × 8 lowest frequencies.
    • CLIP: re-encoding, resize and combo. CLIP’s problem is not that copies look different to it (in the Sintel table above, every copy cosine is above 0.8) but that non-copies look the same: the median temporal score of a pair that is not a copy is 0.721 for CLIP, against 0.100 for SSCD. A blurred, blocky frame loses the details that separate two frames of the same scene, so a copy falls into the crowd of similar clips from the same movie.
    • SSCD: heavy re-encoding. At CRF 45 the queries average about 32 kbit/s. The weakest copies are from the two low-resolution movies (426 × 240 and 564 × 240), where the encoder leaves only blocks; the lowest copy score is 0.153, below many distractor pairs. SSCD learned compression from JPEG-style training edits [Likely], but not this level of video block artifacts.

    Step 5: look at the false alarms

    Before you fix anything, read the top of the ranked list. With SSCD and the temporal matcher, the highest-scoring wrong pair is the speed query of Bunny clip 05 against reference Bunny 04, at 0.847, higher than the median true copy (0.767). Of the 50 highest-scoring wrong pairs, 42 are a held-out clip against its neighbour reference: the clip that ends 5 seconds before it or starts 5 seconds after it. Figure 2 shows why: the second half of Bunny 04 and all of Bunny 05 belong to one long, almost static meadow shot, with the same background and the same rabbit. The detector is not wrong about the pixels; our labels say “not a copy” because the two clips do not share a single frame.

    This is the real definition problem of video copy detection. Is “the same shot, 5 seconds later” a copy? For a copyright system it usually is (it is the same film); for training-data deduplication it may be; for a news-verification system it is not. The VSC 2022 dataset adds 500 hard negative pairs labeled by humans to each split, to measure exactly this kind of confusion [Certain]. Decide the policy first, write it into the labels, and only then tune thresholds. Two more lessons from this list:

    • Mirror invariance cuts both ways. SSCD is almost blind to flips (flip µAP 1.000), so a flipped neighbour shot also matches: the second-highest wrong pair is the flipped Bunny 05 against Bunny 04 (0.832).
    • Static shots make blocks, not lines. In an almost static shot every frame looks like every other, so the similarity matrix has a dark block instead of a thin line, and any line through the block scores high. In Figure 2 the best line for Bunny 05 against Bunny 04 starts at reference frame 11 (second 80.5 of the movie) and matches query frame 0 (second 92): every matched pair is 11.5 seconds apart. A line is real evidence only if it stands out from the cells next to it; a matcher can subtract the average of its neighbourhood, and a longer minimum coverage helps too. Both have a cost: a real copy of a static shot, or a short real copy inside a long query, then scores lower.

    Step 6: fix the weakest transform, then find the next one

    Try 1: score normalization. The VSC 2022 baseline subtracts from every frame similarity the similarity of that query frame to its nearest neighbour in a separate background set, scaled by \(\beta\) [Certain]. A query frame that looks like many things (a dark frame, a sky) is then pushed down:

    \[ \tilde{S}_{ij} = S_{ij} - \beta \max_{\mathbf{b} \in \mathcal{B}} \mathbf{q}_i^\top \mathbf{b}, \qquad \beta = 1.2 \tag{216.6} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(\mathcal{B}\)
    background descriptors of frames that are surely not in the database; we used 270 frames at 1 per second from seconds 470 to 560 of each movie, outside every clip
    \(\beta\)
    how much of the background similarity to remove; VSC used the nearest neighbour (\(k = 1\)) and \(\beta = 1.2\) [Certain]

    In the VSC paper this step raised the SSCD baseline from µAP 0.010 to 0.605 on the challenge test set, because without it empty and solid-color frames matched everything [Certain]. In our lab the effect depends on the descriptor: CLIP rises from 0.478 to 0.653 (its scores were badly calibrated), pHash moves from 0.670 to 0.656, and SSCD drops from 0.931 to 0.913. Our clips have no empty frames, and our small background set contains scenes from the same movies, so for SSCD it mostly adds noise. A per-query alternative that uses the database itself, “score minus the best score of any other reference”, gave SSCD 0.956 and CLIP 0.788, but it cannot report two true sources for one query. Normalization is a calibration tool; measure it on your own distribution.

    Try 2: combine two descriptors that fail differently. SSCD’s weakness (heavy compression) is pHash’s strength, and pHash’s weaknesses (crop, flip) are SSCD’s strengths. Both scores live on the same scale (about 0 for unrelated frames, near 1 for copies), so the simplest fusion is the maximum of the two video scores:

    \[ s_{\text{fuse}}(q, r) = \max\big(s_{\text{SSCD}}(q, r),\; s_{\text{pHash}}(q, r)\big) \tag{216.7} \]

    This raises the overall µAP from 0.931 to 0.969, and the re-encoding µAP from 0.867 to 1.000. Its cost is tiny: 8 more bytes per frame and no neural network. But the attack table shows the next weakest transform at once: combo drops from 0.918 to 0.836. The reason is the max: the fused score of a pair is high if either descriptor is fooled, so pHash adds its own false alarms (for example 0.719 for the Bunny 05 → Bunny 04 neighbour) while it cannot help on cropped combo queries. A max is a good fusion when the second descriptor is rarely wrong with high confidence; otherwise learn the fusion on held-out data, or use the second descriptor only to re-rank. And then attack again: this loop never ends, which is why public benchmarks add transforms to the validation and test sets that are not in the training set [Certain].

    Algorithm 3: the robustness loop
    1. input: a reference set, held-out distractor clips, a list of attacks with strength levels
    2. repeat:
    3.   make queries: every source × every attack × every strength; keep distractors in the same proportion
    4.   score all (query, reference) pairs; compute µAP overall, per attack and per strength // formula (216.5)
    5.   read the top wrong pairs and the lowest true copies; label the cause (descriptor, matcher, label policy)
    6.   fix the weakest attack (new descriptor, fusion, normalization, training edits) // one change at a time
    7.   re-run everything; check that no other attack got worse // combo got worse in Step 6
    8. until every attack in your threat list meets its target, then add a new attack

    Common mistakes

    • Using a semantic embedding as a fingerprint. CLIP placed last here (µAP 0.478), below a 64-bit hash, because it finds all frames of a scene similar. Use a descriptor trained for copy detection.
    • Evaluating per query, without distractors. If every query has a true match and you report top-1 accuracy, SSCD looks almost perfect (98.7%). With half the queries as distractors and one global threshold, it needs 91 false alarms to reach 90% recall.
    • Testing only the attacks you trained for, at one strength. The SSCD re-encoding curve is flat until CRF 40 and then falls fast; a single test at CRF 30 would never show it.
    • Labeling neighbours of the same shot as negatives without deciding the policy. 42 of the 50 top false alarms here are “the same scene a few seconds later”. Fixing the descriptor will not help; fix the labels or the coverage rule.
    • Mean-pooling a video into one vector. It is the worst matcher in every row of the table, and it cannot localize.
    • Getting the clip timing wrong in FFmpeg. -t after the input limits the output, so a 1.5× query silently covers 9 seconds of source. Check every generated file with ffprobe.
    • Mismatched rounding between components. torch.round rounds halves to even, JavaScript rounds them up; with \(\rho = 1.5\) the line visits different frames. Pin the rule in one place.
    • Copying a normalization trick without measuring it. Background score normalization was essential in VSC 2022 but lowered SSCD here (0.931 to 0.913).

    Interview questions

    What is video copy detection, and how is it different from text-to-video retrieval?basic
    Copy detection asks whether a query reuses actual frames of a reference video, after edits such as crop, resize, re-encoding, overlays or speed change; it should not match a different video of the same subject. Retrieval asks whether a video matches a meaning. So the descriptors are trained differently: copy descriptors (SSCD) are trained to be invariant to edits but to separate different images of the same kind, semantic ones (CLIP) to group by meaning. In this lab the semantic descriptor was the worst choice (µAP 0.478 against 0.931 for SSCD).
    How does a perceptual hash work, and which attacks break it?basic
    pHash shrinks the gray frame to 32 × 32, takes the 2-D DCT, keeps the 8 × 8 lowest frequencies and writes 64 bits, one per coefficient above or below their median. Hashes are compared with the Hamming distance; unrelated frames differ in about 32 bits. Because it keeps only the coarse layout, it survives resizing and heavy compression (µAP 1.000 at CRF 45 in our lab). Geometric edits change the layout: a 60% crop or a mirror flip changes about half the bits, so µAP fell to 0.066 and 0.012. It is very cheap (8 bytes per frame) and easy to share between companies.
    You have a frame similarity matrix between a query and a reference. How do you turn it into a video score?core
    Options: the max of the matrix (one frame pair decides; VSC 2022 descriptor track), Chamfer (average over query frames of the best reference frame; order ignored; used in ViSiL), or a temporal alignment that searches straight lines \(j = o + \rho i\) and averages the similarity along the best line (temporal Hough voting), or a learned network over the matrix (ViSiL’s CNN, temporal networks). In our lab Chamfer and the line matcher tied on µAP (0.930 vs 0.931 for SSCD), and both beat pooling (0.874) and max (0.907). The line matcher also returns the speed and offset of the copy, which the others cannot.
    How do you handle a copy that was sped up or slowed down?core
    A speed change turns the diagonal of the similarity matrix into a line with another slope. Search several slopes (we used 0.5, 2/3, 1, 1.5, 2) and offsets, and keep the best mean similarity along the line. With only slope 1, a 1.5× copy gets a lower score and a wrong location: in our worked example 0.751 instead of 0.899, and on the whole speed set µAP rose from 0.979 to 0.995 for SSCD and from 0.837 to 0.969 for CLIP when the speed search was added. For real streams, frame-rate changes and dropped frames add noise around the line, so learned or dynamic-programming aligners are more robust.
    What is micro-AP, and why is it used instead of per-query mAP or top-1 accuracy?core
    Micro-AP ranks all predictions of all queries in one list and computes the area under one precision–recall curve, \(\sum_k P(k)(R(k) - R(k-1))\). It matches how the system runs: one global threshold. It punishes badly calibrated scores (a score of 0.5 must mean the same for every query) and false alarms from distractor queries, which have no true match. Per-query mAP ignores both. In our lab SSCD had 98.7% top-1 accuracy, but at 90% recall over all pairs its precision was only 0.757.
    Your SSCD-based system is weakest on heavily compressed uploads. What do you do?core
    First measure how fast it breaks: in our sweep SSCD was 0.968 at CRF 40, 0.867 at CRF 45 and 0.671 at CRF 51. Then look at the failures: the worst copies came from low-resolution sources. Fixes, cheapest first: fuse with a descriptor that is strong on compression (the max with pHash raised re-encoding to 1.000 and overall µAP from 0.931 to 0.969); add video-codec artifacts to the descriptor’s training edits and fine-tune; compute descriptors at a lower resolution so blocks matter less. After any change re-run all attacks: the max fusion lowered the combo attack from 0.918 to 0.836.
    What is score normalization in copy detection, and when does it help?advanced
    Subtract from each frame similarity \(\beta\) times the similarity of the query frame to its nearest neighbour in a background set of frames known not to be in the database. Frames that look like everything (dark, empty, sky) then lose their advantage, and scores become comparable across queries, which matters for micro-AP. In VSC 2022 it raised the SSCD baseline from µAP 0.010 to 0.605 because empty frames matched everything. In our lab it helped the badly calibrated CLIP (0.478 to 0.653) but lowered SSCD (0.931 to 0.913), because our clips had no empty frames and our background set was small and from the same movies. It changes calibration, not each query’s ranking, so check it with micro-AP on your own data.
    Most of your false alarms are “the same shot, 5 seconds later”. Is the detector wrong?core
    Not necessarily; it is a label-policy question. In our lab 42 of the top 50 false alarms were held-out clips against the neighbouring reference clip that shares a continuous shot. The pixels really match. For copyright it is the same work, so you may label it positive; for verification of a specific moment, it is a different event. Write the policy down, label accordingly (VSC 2022 adds 500 human-labeled hard negative pairs per split), and if needed change the matcher: require a longer covered segment, or score segment length together with similarity.
    Design a fingerprinting service for 100,000 hours of new video per day.advanced
    Sample 1 frame per second (the VSC limit is one 512-d descriptor per second on average): \(3.6 \times 10^8\) frames per day. Float32 SSCD vectors would be 2 KB per frame, 737 GB per day; product quantization to 64 bytes gives about 23 GB per day, and a pHash only 2.9 GB. Index the reference descriptors in an approximate nearest-neighbour index (session 151). For each query frame retrieve the top-k reference frames, group hits by reference video, and run a temporal check (line or learned) only on the candidates, which also gives the segment. Calibrate the threshold with micro-AP on traffic-like data with mostly distractors, keep a cheap hash as a second signal, and send borderline cases to human review.
    How would you evaluate the robustness of a fingerprinting system honestly?advanced
    Use references and distractors from the same distribution (we used held-out clips of the same movies, which create hard negatives). Apply each attack at several strengths and report curves, not one point. Include combinations, because real copies stack edits. Keep some transforms out of training and tuning, as VSC 2022 did by adding new transforms to its validation and test sets. Report micro-AP with distractors, localization accuracy, and the top false alarms with their causes. Re-run the full suite after every change, because fixes can move the weakness (our fusion fixed re-encoding but hurt combo).

    Key takeaways

    • A fingerprint must be invariant to edits but specific to frames. A copy-detection descriptor (SSCD, µAP 0.931) beat a 64-bit pHash (0.670) and a semantic CLIP embedding (0.478) on the same 630 queries.
    • Represent a video as a sequence of frame descriptors and compare two videos with a frame similarity matrix; a copy is a line, its slope is the speed and its start is the location.
    • Chamfer and a temporal line search scored the same µAP here, but only the line search finds speed and offset; searching speeds fixed the 1.5× attack.
    • Evaluate with micro-AP over all pairs, with distractors, at one global threshold. Top-1 accuracy of 98.7% hid a precision of 0.757 at 90% recall.
    • Attack at several strengths. Each descriptor had its own weakest transform: crop and flip for pHash, re-encoding for SSCD and CLIP.
    • Fusing descriptors that fail differently fixed SSCD’s weakest transform (re-encoding 0.867 → 1.000) but moved the weakness to the combined attack (0.918 → 0.836). Repeat the loop.
    • Read the false alarms: most were the same shot a few seconds later, a label-policy question, not a descriptor bug.
