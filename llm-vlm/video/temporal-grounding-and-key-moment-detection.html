    Temporal grounding and key-moment detection

    Temporal grounding finds when something happens in a video: given a sentence, return its start and end time. Key-moment (highlight) detection scores every clip by how important it is. This session covers the metrics (R1@IoU, mAP, HIT@1), three model families (sliding windows in TALL, per-clip heads in UniVTG, DETR-style set prediction in Moment-DETR), video LLMs that write timestamps (TimeChat), and why noisy human labels put a ceiling on every score.

    Time: 3 to 4 hours (reading 2 hours, exercises 1 to 2 hours)Level: Beginner to interview levelBefore this: Sessions on action recognition (203) and long video (206); CLIP and Q-Former basics; basic PyTorch

    What you will learn

    • The two tasks: moment retrieval (text in, start and end time out) and highlight detection (a score for every clip), and the main datasets, Charades-STA and QVHighlights
    • How to compute temporal IoU, R1@0.5 / R1@0.7, mIoU, mAP over IoU thresholds, and HIT@1 averaged over several annotators
    • How TALL scores sliding windows and regresses boundary offsets, and how UniVTG lets every clip predict foreground, offsets and saliency
    • How Moment-DETR predicts a set of moments with learned queries, Hungarian matching and a margin ranking loss for saliency, and why it needs no NMS
    • How TimeChat binds timestamps to frame tokens and writes times as text, and why frame spacing L/T limits its precision
    • Why moment labels are noisy and biased, how much humans disagree, and how to measure the ceiling before trusting small gains

    Why this matters

    People rarely want a whole video. They want the part where the dog catches the frisbee, the moment the price is announced, or the best five seconds for a preview. Temporal grounding answers “where in time?” for a text query. Key-moment detection answers “which parts matter most?”. Together they turn a long video into something you can search and skim.

    This session covers two tasks that are usually trained together:

    1. Moment retrieval (also called temporal sentence grounding or video grounding): given a video and a sentence, return the start and end time of the matching part, for example “12.4 s to 20.4 s”.
    2. Highlight detection: give every short clip of the video a score for how important (or how well it matches the query) it is, and return the top clips.

    We then follow three generations of models: sliding windows with boundary regression (TALL), DETR-style set prediction (Moment-DETR) and one-head-per-clip models (UniVTG), and finally video LLMs that simply write the timestamps as text (TimeChat). At the end we look hard at the labels themselves: two careful people often do not agree on where a moment starts, and that sets a ceiling for every metric.

Each has a worked answer below.

    The two tasks and their data

    All modern methods start the same way. A frozen encoder cuts the video into short clips of fixed length \(l\) (often 1 or 2 seconds) and turns each clip into a feature vector. The query sentence becomes a sequence of token features. Everything after that works on these two sequences.

    \[ V = (v_1, \dots, v_{L_v}), \quad t_i = \big(i - \tfrac12\big)\, l, \qquad \text{MR: } \{(\hat s_k, \hat e_k, c_k)\}_{k}, \qquad \text{HD: } \hat{\mathbf{S}} \in \R^{L_v} \tag{207.1} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(v_i\)
    the feature vector of clip \(i\) (for example CLIP and SlowFast features of 2 seconds of video)
    \(L_v\)
    the number of clips; a 150-s video with 2-s clips has \(L_v = 75\)
    \(t_i\)
    the center time of clip \(i\), in seconds
    \(\hat s_k, \hat e_k, c_k\)
    start, end and confidence of predicted moment \(k\) (moment retrieval returns a ranked list)
    \(\hat{\mathbf{S}}\)
    one saliency score per clip (highlight detection)

    Moment retrieval is like temporal action localization, but the “class” is a free sentence that changes for every query, so the model must compare video and text. Highlight detection is a ranking problem over clips. It comes in two forms: query-based (“highlights about the beach”) and generic (“the best parts of this video”, as used for thumbnails and previews).

    AnalogyA film editor gets a note: “the scene where she opens the letter”. Moment retrieval is marking in and out points on the timeline. Highlight detection is putting a star rating on every shot, so the trailer can use the five-star shots.

    In QVHighlights, annotators first wrote a query and clicked on all relevant 2-s clips in a grid; then other workers rated each selected clip from “Very Bad” to “Very Good”. [Certain] So one query can have several separate moments, and every clip inside a moment has a quality score. This makes it possible to train and test both tasks on the same data.

    Metrics: R1@IoU, mAP and HIT@1

    Temporal IoU

    A predicted moment \(\hat{M} = [\hat s, \hat e]\) is compared with the true moment \(M = [s, e]\) by temporal intersection over union, exactly as in action localization:

    \[ \operatorname{IoU}(\hat M, M) = \frac{\max\!\big(0,\ \min(\hat e, e) - \max(\hat s, s)\big)}{\max(\hat e, e) - \min(\hat s, s)} \tag{207.2} \]

    For overlapping intervals the bottom is the length of the union. IoU is 1 for a perfect match and 0 when the intervals do not touch.

    Recall at the top results

    The classic single-moment metric is R@n, IoU=m: the fraction of queries for which at least one of the top \(n\) predictions has IoU of at least \(m\) with the truth. Most papers report \(n = 1\), written R1@m, at \(m \in \{0.3, 0.5, 0.7\}\), plus the mean IoU of the top-1 prediction:

    \[ \text{R}n@m = \frac{1}{N_q}\sum_{q=1}^{N_q} \mathbb{1}\Big[\max_{k \le n} \operatorname{IoU}\big(\hat M_{q,k}, M_q\big) \ge m\Big], \qquad \text{mIoU} = \frac{1}{N_q}\sum_{q=1}^{N_q} \operatorname{IoU}\big(\hat M_{q,1}, M_q\big) \tag{207.3} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(N_q\)
    the number of test queries
    \(\hat M_{q,k}\)
    the \(k\)-th ranked prediction for query \(q\)
    \(m\)
    the IoU threshold; a larger \(m\) demands tighter boundaries

    Papers differ on “\(\ge m\)” versus “\(\gt m\)” (TALL writes “larger than”, QVHighlights writes “\(\ge\)”). [Certain] It rarely matters, but it does when predictions are snapped to a grid and ties are common. Figure 1 lets you move a prediction and see the thresholds pass and fail.

    Worked example: R1@m and mIoU on five queries

    Five queries, one top-1 prediction each:

    • truth [8.0, 14.5], prediction [9.0, 16.0]: IoU 0.688
    • truth [2.1, 9.8], prediction [2.0, 10.0]: IoU \(7.7/8.0 = 0.963\)
    • truth [15.0, 21.3], prediction [11.0, 18.0]: overlap 3, union 10.3, IoU 0.291
    • truth [0.0, 6.4], prediction [0.5, 5.5]: IoU \(5/6.4 = 0.781\)
    • truth [20.2, 27.0], prediction [3.0, 9.0]: IoU 0

    R1@0.3 = 3/5 = 0.6 (the third query just misses with 0.291), R1@0.5 = 0.6, R1@0.7 = 2/5 = 0.4, mIoU = 0.5445. One bad query (the last) lowers mIoU a lot, but it costs R1 only one hit: the two metrics answer different questions.

    mAP when a query has several moments

    In QVHighlights a query can match several separate moments, so R1 is not enough. The benchmark also uses mean average precision, as in detection: rank the predictions, mark each one as a true positive if it has IoU \(\ge \theta\) with a still-unmatched true moment, compute the area under the precision–recall curve, and average:

    \[ \text{mAP}@\theta = \frac{1}{N_q}\sum_{q=1}^{N_q} \text{AP}_q(\theta), \qquad \text{avg mAP} = \frac{1}{10}\sum_{\theta \in \{0.5, 0.55, \dots, 0.95\}} \text{mAP}@\theta \tag{207.4} \]

    QVHighlights reports mAP@0.5, mAP@0.75 and the average over the ten thresholds 0.5:0.05:0.95. [Certain] Example: true moments [20, 34] and [60, 70] s; ranked predictions [21, 35] (IoU 0.867), [40, 48] (IoU 0) and [58, 69.5] (IoU 0.792). For \(\theta \le 0.75\) the hits are TP, FP, TP and AP \(= \tfrac12 \cdot 1 + \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac23 = 0.833\). At 0.8 and 0.85 only the first hits (AP 0.5); at 0.9 and 0.95 nothing hits. The average over the ten thresholds is \((6 \cdot 0.833 + 2 \cdot 0.5)/10 = 0.6\).

    Highlight detection: mAP and HIT@1 against each annotator

    For highlight detection the truth is a score per clip. QVHighlights counts a clip as positive if its score is “Very Good”, ranks clips by the predicted saliency, and reports mAP and HIT@1: is the single top-ranked clip a positive? Because the saliency scores come from 3 people, the metric is computed against each annotator separately and then averaged: [Certain]

    \[ \text{HIT@1} = \frac{1}{N_q}\sum_{q=1}^{N_q} \frac{1}{A}\sum_{a=1}^{A} \mathbb{1}\Big[\, y^{(a)}_{q,\,i^*_q} = \text{Very Good} \Big], \qquad i^*_q = \argmax_i \hat S_{q,i} \tag{207.5} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(A\)
    the number of annotators (3 in QVHighlights)
    \(y^{(a)}_{q,i}\)
    annotator \(a\)’s rating of clip \(i\) for query \(q\)
    \(i^*_q\)
    the clip the model ranks first

    Averaging over annotators is an honest way to say “the truth is not one number”. If the three people disagree about the best clip, even a perfect model cannot reach HIT@1 = 1. We return to this in the section on label noise.

    Watch outR1@0.5 and R1@0.7 can rank two models differently. Moment-DETR beat earlier models on Charades-STA at R1@0.5 (53.63) but not at R1@0.7 (31.37 against 32.88 for IVG-DCL). [Certain] Always look at the strict threshold when boundaries matter, for example for video editing.

    Generation 1: sliding windows and boundary regression (TALL)

    The first deep approach treated grounding like early object detection. TALL (Gao et al., 2017) introduced the task “temporal activity localization via language” and the Charades-STA dataset. [Certain] Its model, CTRL (Cross-modal Temporal Regression Localizer), works in three steps:

    1. Cut the video into many candidate windows with a multi-scale sliding window (in training, 64, 128, 256 and 512 frames with 80% overlap). [Certain]
    2. For each (window, sentence) pair, fuse the window feature (plus some context before and after) with the sentence feature and predict an alignment score.
    3. Also predict two offsets that move the window’s start and end toward the true boundaries.
    \[ t^s = s - s_c, \quad t^e = e - e_c, \qquad \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{aln}} + \alpha\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}} \tag{207.6} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(s_c, e_c\)
    start and end of the candidate window
    \(s, e\)
    start and end of the true moment
    \(t^s, t^e\)
    the regression targets (offsets in time, not scaled by the window length)
    \(\alpha\)
    a weight between the two losses

    The alignment loss is a logistic loss over a batch of \(N\) aligned pairs: the matched pair \((c_i, s_i)\) should score high and all mismatched pairs \((c_i, s_j)\) low:

    \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{aln}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big[\alpha_c \log\big(1 + e^{-cs_{i,i}}\big) + \sum_{j \ne i} \alpha_w \log\big(1 + e^{cs_{i,j}}\big)\Big] \tag{207.7} \]

    Here \(cs_{i,j}\) is the alignment score of window \(i\) with sentence \(j\), and \(\alpha_c, \alpha_w\) weight positives and negatives. The paper found that these plain (“non-parameterized”) offsets worked better than the object-detection style offsets scaled by window length: for actions, “time itself is the standard scale”. [Certain] On Charades-STA, CTRL reached R1@0.5 = 23.63 and R1@0.7 = 8.89. [Certain]

    Algorithm 1: sliding-window grounding (CTRL style) at test time
    1. input: video, sentence \(q\), window lengths \(\{w\}\), overlap, trained scorer and regressor
    2. for each window length \(w\) and each start position do
    3.   \(c \leftarrow\) features of the window, plus pre- and post-context windows
    4.   score \(\leftarrow\) alignment\((c, q)\); \((t^s, t^e) \leftarrow\) regressor\((c, q)\)
    5.   refined moment \(\leftarrow [s_c + t^s,\ e_c + t^e]\) // formula (207.6)
    6. sort all refined moments by score; optionally remove duplicates with 1-D NMS
    7. return the top \(n\) moments (for R@n)

    The weak point is cost and coverage: a short moment between two windows is found only if the regressor can move far enough, and the number of windows grows with video length times the number of scales.

    Generation 2: one prediction per clip (the UniVTG view)

    A simpler idea, borrowed from anchor-free detectors such as ActionFormer: let every clip make a prediction. UniVTG (Lin et al., 2023) writes all grounding labels in one format. For every clip \(v_i\) with center \(t_i\) it defines three targets: [Certain]

    \[ v_i \mapsto (f_i,\ \mathbf{d}_i,\ s_i), \quad f_i \in \{0, 1\}, \quad \mathbf{d}_i = (d^s_i, d^e_i), \quad b_i = [\,t_i - d^s_i,\ t_i + d^e_i\,], \quad s_i \in [0, 1] \tag{207.8} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(f_i\)
    foreground indicator: 1 if the clip is inside a moment that matches the query
    \(d^s_i, d^e_i\)
    distance from the clip center back to the moment start and forward to its end (only used when \(f_i = 1\))
    \(b_i\)
    the moment this clip “votes for”
    \(s_i\)
    saliency: how relevant the clip is to the query (0 = not at all, 1 = highly)

    The point of this format is that every kind of label can be converted into it. An interval label (moment retrieval) gives \(f\) and \(\mathbf{d}\) directly. A curve label (a score per clip, highlight detection) gives \(s\), and thresholding it gives intervals. A point label (one timestamp, as in Ego4D narrations) can be grown into a pseudo-interval. [Certain] This let UniVTG pretrain on 4.2M temporal labels: 1.8M point labels from Ego4D, 0.9M interval labels from VideoCC and 1.5M pseudo curve labels made by a CLIP teacher. [Certain]

    The model has a light multimodal transformer and three small heads (foreground with binary cross-entropy, boundary with smooth-L1 plus gIoU loss, and saliency as the cosine similarity between clip and sentence features trained with a contrastive loss). At test time: [Certain]

    • Moment retrieval: rank the clip votes \(b_i\) by \(\tilde f_i\), then 1-D NMS with threshold 0.7.
    • Highlight detection: rank clips by \(\tilde f_i + \tilde s_i\) and return the top ones.

    Step through this on a toy example in Figure 2.

    On QVHighlights test, UniVTG without pretraining reached R1@0.5 = 58.86, avg mAP 35.47 and HIT@1 60.96; with its large-scale pretraining, 65.43, 43.63 and 66.28. [Certain] Its zero-shot model (no QVHighlights training at all) reached 25.16 R1@0.5, below the supervised models but above some older supervised baselines. [Certain]

    Generation 3: DETR-style set prediction (Moment-DETR)

    Per-clip voting gives many near-duplicate moments, so it needs NMS. DETR (Carion et al., 2020) showed for object detection that a transformer decoder with a fixed set of learned queries can output a set of boxes directly, trained with a one-to-one matching so that duplicates are punished. Moment-DETR (Lei, Berg and Bansal, 2021), proposed together with QVHighlights, brings this to time. [Certain]

    1. Inputs: video features every 2 s (SlowFast and CLIP ViT-B/32, concatenated to 2816 numbers) and CLIP text token features (512). Both are projected to \(d = 256\) and concatenated into one sequence. [Certain]
    2. Encoder: 2 transformer layers over the joint video+text sequence. A linear head on the video positions gives the saliency score of each clip. [Certain]
    3. Decoder: 2 layers with \(N = 10\) learned moment queries (trainable embeddings, not the text query). Each query outputs a normalized (center, width) and a foreground/background probability. [Certain]

    The model returns 10 moments for every video. Training must decide which of the 10 predictions is responsible for which true moment. That is a bipartite matching (an assignment problem), solved with the Hungarian algorithm:

    \[ \hat\sigma = \argmin_{\sigma} \sum_{i} \Big[ -\hat p_{\sigma(i)}(\text{fg}) + \lambda_{L1}\, \big\lVert m_i - \hat m_{\sigma(i)} \big\rVert_1 + \lambda_{\text{iou}}\, \big(1 - \operatorname{gIoU}(m_i, \hat m_{\sigma(i)})\big) \Big] \tag{207.9} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(m_i\)
    true moment \(i\), as (center, width) divided by the video length
    \(\sigma\)
    an assignment: true moment \(i\) is given to prediction \(\sigma(i)\); each prediction gets at most one
    \(\hat p(\text{fg})\)
    the predicted probability that the moment query is foreground
    \(\operatorname{gIoU}\)
    generalized IoU: IoU minus the fraction of the smallest enclosing interval that is not covered; it is negative for far-apart intervals, so it still gives a useful signal when there is no overlap

    After matching, the loss pulls each matched prediction toward its moment, makes it say “foreground”, and makes every unmatched prediction say “background” (with its log-probability down-weighted by 10 to balance the many background slots): [Certain]

    \[ \mathcal{L} = \lambda_{s}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{sal}} + \sum_{j=1}^{N} \Big[ -\lambda_{\text{cls}}\, w_j \log \hat p_j(c_j) + \mathbb{1}[c_j = \text{fg}]\, \big(\lambda_{L1} \lVert m - \hat m_j \rVert_1 + \lambda_{\text{iou}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{gIoU}}\big) \Big] \tag{207.10} \]

    with \(w_j = 1\) for foreground and \(0.1\) for background, and \(\lambda_{L1} = 10\), \(\lambda_{\text{iou}} = 1\), \(\lambda_{\text{cls}} = 4\), \(\lambda_s = 1\) in the paper. [Certain] The saliency loss is a hinge (margin) ranking loss on two pairs of clips: a high-rated and a low-rated clip inside the moment, and a clip inside and a clip outside:

    \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{sal}} = \max\!\big(0,\ \Delta + S(t_{\text{low}}) - S(t_{\text{high}})\big) + \max\!\big(0,\ \Delta + S(t_{\text{out}}) - S(t_{\text{in}})\big), \quad \Delta = 0.2 \tag{207.11} \]

    The loss is zero once the better clip beats the worse one by at least the margin \(\Delta\). It only needs an order between clips, not exact scores, which suits noisy human ratings.

    AnalogyTen taxi drivers wait at a station and two passengers arrive. The dispatcher gives each passenger to the driver who is closest and most ready (the matching). Only those two drivers are judged on how well they drove; the other eight are told to stay parked (background). No two drivers are rewarded for picking up the same passenger, so nobody needs to remove duplicates afterwards.
    Worked example: the matched loss in Figure 3

    With the optimal matching (query 1 → moment 1, query 4 → moment 2): L1 distances 0.03 and 0.035 (sum 0.065); gIoU 0.857 and 0.722, so the gIoU loss is \(0.143 + 0.278 = 0.421\). Cross-entropy: foreground \(-\ln 0.85 - \ln 0.30 = 1.366\); background for queries 2, 3, 5: \(-(\ln 0.45 + \ln 0.30 + \ln 0.85) = 2.165\), times 0.1 = 0.216. Total: \(10 \cdot 0.065 + 1 \cdot 0.421 + 4 \cdot (1.366 + 0.216) = 7.403\). Most of the loss is the classifier: query 4 must learn to be more confident, and query 3 must learn to say “background”.

    Algorithm 2: one Moment-DETR training step
    1. input: clip features \(E_v\), text token features \(E_q\), true moments \(\{m_i\}\), clip ratings
    2. \(X \leftarrow [\text{proj}(E_v);\ \text{proj}(E_q)]\); \(H \leftarrow \text{Encoder}(X)\) // 2 layers
    3. saliency \(S \leftarrow\) Linear\((H_{\text{video positions}})\)
    4. \(Z \leftarrow \text{Decoder}(\text{moment queries}, H)\); for each of \(N\) queries: \((\hat c, \hat w) \leftarrow\) FFN, \(\hat p \leftarrow\) softmax(Linear)
    5. build the \(N \times |\{m_i\}|\) cost matrix; \(\hat\sigma \leftarrow\) Hungarian // formula (207.9)
    6. loss \(\leftarrow\) formula (207.10) with \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{sal}}\) from (207.11); apply it at every decoder layer (auxiliary losses)
    7. AdamW step // lr 1e-4, weight decay 1e-4 in the paper
    8. at test time: return the \(N\) moments sorted by \(\hat p(\text{fg})\); no NMS

    Results on QVHighlights test: Moment-DETR reached R1@0.5 = 52.89, R1@0.7 = 33.02, avg mAP 30.73, highlight mAP 35.69 and HIT@1 55.60. With weakly supervised pretraining on 236K ASR (automatic speech recognition) caption–timestamp pairs from the training videos, it rose to 59.78, 40.33, 36.14, 37.43 and 60.17. [Certain] One surprise in the same table: plain CLIP, scoring each clip by the similarity of its center frame to the query, had the best HIT@1 (61.04), though its highlight mAP (31.30) was lower. [Certain] Picking the single best frame is mostly an image–text matching problem.

    NumbersCharades-STA, R1@0.5 / R1@0.7: CTRL 23.63 / 8.89 (2017); Moment-DETR 53.63 / 31.37; UniVTG with pretraining 60.19 / 38.55. [Certain] These rows come from different papers and not always the same video features, so read them as a trend, not a ranking.

    Generation 4: LLMs that write timestamps (TimeChat)

    Video LLMs (see session 205) answer questions in text. Early ones could describe a video but not say when things happen: in TimeChat’s zero-shot tests on Charades-STA, Video-LLaMA (7B) scored R1@0.5 = 2.7 and VideoChat-Embed (7B) 3.2. [Certain] Purely random uniform intervals score 10.77 on the same benchmark. [Certain] The models had seen frames, but nothing told them which frame came at which second.

    TimeChat (Ren et al., CVPR 2024) adds time to the input and trains the model to output time as plain text: [Certain]

    1. Timestamp-aware frame encoder. Each frame goes through an EVA-CLIP ViT-G/14 and an image Q-Former (from InstructBLIP). The Q-Former also reads a sentence such as “This frame is sampled at 2s.”, so the visual tokens of the frame carry its time.
    2. Sliding video Q-Former. A second Q-Former looks at a window of \(L_W\) frames and outputs \(N_V\) tokens, then slides with stride \(S\). Longer videos get more tokens instead of a fixed 32.
    3. LLM. LLaMA-2 7B, frozen, with LoRA (rank 32). The image and video Q-Formers and the projection are trained.
    4. Data. TimeIT, 125K instruction examples from 6 time-related tasks (dense captioning, grounding, step localization, summarization, highlight detection, transcribed speech), with answers in fixed formats such as “The given query happens in 0.0 - 6.9 seconds.”
    \[ N_{\text{tok}} = \frac{T}{S}\, N_V, \qquad R' = \frac{T\, N_P}{(T/S)\, N_V} = \frac{S\, N_P}{N_V} \tag{207.12} \]
    Read the symbols
    \(T\)
    number of sampled frames (96 in TimeChat)
    \(S, N_V\)
    window stride and tokens per window (both 32; the window length \(L_W\) is also 32)
    \(N_P\)
    patches per frame; \(R'\) is the compression rate (patches in, tokens out), which stays constant as \(T\) grows

    With TimeChat’s settings, \(96/32 \cdot 32 = 96\) video tokens go to the LLM. Training is the usual next-token loss on the answer, timestamps included:

    \[ \mathcal{L} = -\sum_{i=1}^{L_T} \log P_\theta\big(x_i \mid X_v,\ X_q,\ x_{\lt i}\big) \tag{207.13} \]

    Here \(X_v\) are the video tokens, \(X_q\) the instruction and query, and \(x_i\) the answer tokens. There is no IoU loss: the digits “2”, “5”, “0”, “.”, “0” of “250.0” are ordinary tokens. A prediction 1 s off and one 100 s off can have similar loss if they differ in one digit. This is the main difference from Moment-DETR, whose loss knows that time is a continuous quantity.

    Zero-shot results (no training on the test datasets): on Charades-STA, TimeChat reached R1@0.5 = 32.2 and R1@0.7 = 13.4; on QVHighlights highlight detection, mAP 14.5 and HIT@1 23.9. [Certain] That is far better than earlier video LLMs, but still below supervised specialists such as Moment-DETR (53.63 R1@0.5 on Charades-STA). The answers must also be parsed from free text; the authors wrote “a considerable number of heuristic rules” for this. [Certain]

    What limits the precision of a written timestamp

    A model can only point at moments it has seen. If \(T\) frames are spread evenly over a video of length \(L\), each frame stands for a slot of

    \[ \Delta = \frac{L}{T}, \qquad \operatorname{IoU}\Big([s - \delta,\ e + \delta],\ [s, e]\Big) = \frac{d}{d + 2\delta}, \quad d = e - s \tag{207.14} \]

    The second formula is the IoU when both boundaries are \(\delta\) seconds too wide. It passes 0.7 only if \(\delta \le 3d/14\): for an 8-s moment, at most 1.7 s on each side. With 96 frames on a 10-minute video, \(\Delta = 6.25\) s; an error of half a slot at each end is already 3.1 s. Figure 4 shows this ceiling.

    This is why long-video grounding needs more frames near the answer: either more tokens, or a two-pass approach (coarse search over the whole video, then dense frames around the candidate), as discussed in session 206. Later time-aware video LLMs (for example VTimeLLM, LITA, TimeSuite) explore other ways to represent time, such as special time tokens or relative positions. [Likely]

    Watch outAn LLM can write “250.0 - 256.3 seconds” for a video that is 200 s long, or swap start and end. Always clip predictions to [0, L], sort the two numbers, and count unparsable answers as misses (not as dropped queries). Report how many answers failed to parse.

    Label noise: when is a “key moment”?

    Every metric above compares a prediction with one human answer. But moments have soft edges. When does “the person opens the door” start: when the hand touches the handle, or when the door moves? Is the third goal replay a “highlight”? Different careful people give different answers, and that is label noise that no model can remove.

    Otani et al. (2020) measured it on Charades-STA and ActivityNet Captions. They collected 5 new annotations for each of 1,000 Charades-STA samples and 1,288 ActivityNet Captions samples, then scored the humans with the same R1@0.5 metric. [Certain]

    Source: Otani et al., Table 1. [Certain] A typical human scores only about 43% at IoU 0.5 against the official label on Charades-STA, and models are close to or above that. The same paper found strong location biases: many moments start near the beginning of the video, and some verbs are tied to positions (“leave” near the end). Blind baselines that never look at the video beat several published models on Charades-STA. [Certain] QVHighlights was designed with this in mind: moments are spread almost evenly over the video, and on a set of 600 queries annotated by 3 workers, about 90% of queries had an average pairwise IoU above 0.9. [Certain] Its clip-based annotation (2-s cells, click-and-drag) leaves less room for disagreement than free start and end times.

    Figure 5 simulates the effect. Each annotator marks the true moment with a random error on each boundary (Gaussian, standard deviation \(\sigma\)). We compare two annotators with each other, and a perfect model that predicts the exact true moment with one random annotator.

    Three practical lessons follow:

    • Measure the ceiling. Before you chase R1@0.7, have two people label a few hundred of your queries and compute their agreement with the same metric.
    • Prefer metrics that use several annotators, as QVHighlights does for highlights (207.5), or soft labels such as averaged ratings.
    • Choose losses that tolerate noise. The ranking loss (207.11) only needs an order; IoU-based losses do not care about the exact position of a single boundary as much as a per-frame classifier does.

    Code: metrics, matching, saliency loss, decoding and parsing

    The code below runs on CPU in well under a second and prints the numbers used in this page.

    import itertools, math, re, torch
torch.manual_seed(0)

# 1) temporal IoU (207.2) and R1@IoU, mIoU (207.3) for single-moment queries
def tiou(a, b):
    inter = max(0.0, min(a[1], b[1]) - max(a[0], b[0]))
    union = max(a[1], b[1]) - min(a[0], b[0])
    return inter / union if union > 0 else 0.0

gts   = [(8.0, 14.5), (2.1, 9.8), (15.0, 21.3), (0.0, 6.4), (20.2, 27.0)]
top1  = [(9.0, 16.0), (2.0, 10.0), (11.0, 18.0), (0.5, 5.5), (3.0, 9.0)]
ious = [tiou(p, g) for p, g in zip(top1, gts)]
r1 = {m: sum(v >= m for v in ious) / len(ious) for m in (0.3, 0.5, 0.7)}
print([round(v, 3) for v in ious], r1, "mIoU", round(sum(ious) / len(ious), 4))

# 2) highlight detection: HIT@1 against each of 3 annotators, then averaged (207.5)
pred = torch.tensor([0.1, 0.7, 0.9, 0.4, 0.2])            # predicted saliency, 5 clips
ann = torch.tensor([[1, 4, 5, 3, 1],                     # 1 = Very Bad ... 5 = Very Good
                    [1, 5, 4, 3, 2],
                    [2, 3, 3, 5, 1]])
top = pred.argmax()
print("HIT@1 per annotator", (ann[:, top] == 5).int().tolist(), "mean", round((ann[:, top] == 5).float().mean().item(), 3))

# 3) Moment-DETR style bipartite matching (207.9): (center, width) spans, normalized time
def to_se(cw): return (cw[0] - cw[1] / 2, cw[0] + cw[1] / 2)
def giou(a, b):
    inter = max(0.0, min(a[1], b[1]) - max(a[0], b[0]))
    union = (a[1] - a[0]) + (b[1] - b[0]) - inter
    hull = max(a[1], b[1]) - min(a[0], b[0])
    return inter / union - (hull - union) / hull

gt = torch.tensor([[0.26, 0.12], [0.675, 0.15]])
spans = torch.tensor([[0.27, 0.14], [0.66, 0.10], [0.50, 0.40], [0.70, 0.16], [0.10, 0.08]])
p_fg = torch.tensor([0.85, 0.55, 0.70, 0.30, 0.15])
lam_l1, lam_iou, lam_cls = 10.0, 1.0, 4.0
cost = torch.zeros(5, 2)
for j in range(5):
    for g in range(2):
        l1 = (spans[j] - gt[g]).abs().sum().item()
        cost[j, g] = -p_fg[j] + lam_l1 * l1 + lam_iou * (1 - giou(to_se(spans[j].tolist()), to_se(gt[g].tolist())))
best = min(itertools.permutations(range(5), 2), key=lambda q: sum(cost[q[g], g] for g in range(2)))
print("matched queries", [b + 1 for b in best], "cost", round(sum(cost[best[g], g].item() for g in range(2)), 3))

# loss (207.10): L1 + gIoU on matched pairs, foreground/background cross-entropy (background weight 0.1)
fg = torch.zeros(5); fg[list(best)] = 1
w = torch.where(fg == 1, 1.0, 0.1)
ce = -(w * (fg * p_fg.log() + (1 - fg) * (1 - p_fg).log())).sum()
l1 = sum((spans[best[g]] - gt[g]).abs().sum() for g in range(2))
gl = sum(1 - giou(to_se(spans[best[g]].tolist()), to_se(gt[g].tolist())) for g in range(2))
print("L1", round(l1.item(), 3), "gIoU loss", round(gl, 3), "CE", round(ce.item(), 3),
      "total", round((lam_l1 * l1 + lam_iou * gl + lam_cls * ce).item(), 3))

# 4) saliency hinge loss (207.11), margin 0.2
S = torch.tensor([0.30, 0.55, 0.62, 0.20], requires_grad=True)   # clip scores
t_high, t_low, t_in, t_out = 2, 1, 1, 3
loss_sal = torch.relu(0.2 + S[t_low] - S[t_high]) + torch.relu(0.2 + S[t_out] - S[t_in])
loss_sal.backward()
print("saliency loss", round(loss_sal.item(), 3), "grad", [round(g, 2) for g in S.grad.tolist()])

# 5) UniVTG-style decoding (207.8): per-clip foreground f, offsets (ds, de); rank by f, 1-D NMS at 0.7
t = torch.arange(30) * 2.0 + 1.0                                   # clip centers of 2-s clips
f = torch.maximum(0.93 * torch.exp(-((t - 27) / 6) ** 2), 0.62 * torch.exp(-((t - 45) / 2.6) ** 2)).clamp(min=0.03)
near = (t - 45).abs() < (t - 27).abs()
a = torch.where(near, 42.0, 20.0); b = torch.where(near, 48.0, 34.0)
i = torch.arange(30.0)
ds = (t - a + 1.2 * torch.sin(1.3 * i)).clamp(min=0.5); de = (b - t + 1.0 * torch.cos(0.9 * i)).clamp(min=0.5)
cand = [(round(f[k].item(), 3), round((t[k] - ds[k]).item(), 1), round((t[k] + de[k]).item(), 1))
        for k in torch.nonzero(f >= 0.4).flatten().tolist()]
kept = []
for c in sorted(cand, key=lambda c: -c[0]):
    if all(tiou(c[1:], k[1:]) < 0.7 for k in kept):
        kept.append(c)
print("moments", kept, "IoU of top-1", round(tiou(kept[0][1:], (20, 34)), 3))

# 6) an LLM writes timestamps as text: parse them, then score (TimeChat output format)
answer = "The given query happens in 250.0 - 256.3 seconds."
s, e = map(float, re.findall(r"(\d+(?:\.\d+)?)\s*-\s*(\d+(?:\.\d+)?)\s*seconds", answer)[0])
print("parsed", (s, e), "IoU", round(tiou((s, e), (247.8, 255.8)), 3))

    Common mistakes

    • Reporting only R1@0.5. It hides loose boundaries. Report R1@0.7 and mIoU as well, and avg mAP when queries have several moments.
    • Comparing numbers across different features. Grounding results depend strongly on the frozen encoder (SlowFast, CLIP, I3D, InternVideo). Compare models only with the same features, or say clearly that you did not.
    • Ignoring dataset priors. On Charades-STA, blind baselines that never look at the video beat several published models. Always run a “video shuffled” or “text only” baseline.
    • Dropping unparsable LLM answers. Removing the failures from the denominator inflates R1. Count them as misses and report the parse rate.
    • Sampling too few frames for long videos. With \(T\) frames, a perfect model cannot be more precise than \(L/T\) (207.14). Check \(d / \Delta\) for your typical moment before blaming the model.
    • Treating one annotator as the truth for highlights. Ratings differ between people. Evaluate against each annotator (207.5) or against averaged ratings, and measure human agreement.
    • Forgetting to clip and sort predicted boundaries. Regression heads and LLMs can produce \(\hat e \lt \hat s\) or times past the video end.
    • Using NMS with DETR-style models, or forgetting it with per-clip models. Moment-DETR is trained to avoid duplicates; per-clip voting (UniVTG) needs NMS.

    Interview questions

    What is the difference between moment retrieval and highlight detection?basic
    Moment retrieval takes a video and a sentence and returns one or more intervals \([s, e]\) that match the sentence, ranked by confidence; it is measured with R1@IoU and mAP over IoU thresholds. Highlight detection gives every clip a score (saliency) and returns the top clips; it is measured with mAP over clips and HIT@1 against “Very Good” clips. Highlights can be query-based or generic. QVHighlights labels both on the same videos, so one model (Moment-DETR, UniVTG) can learn both.
    What does “R1@0.7 = 40” mean?basic
    For 40% of the test queries, the single top-ranked predicted moment has temporal IoU of at least 0.7 with the true moment. For an 8-s moment, IoU 0.7 allows only about 1.7 s of extra length on each side if both ends are too wide (\(d/(d+2\delta) \ge 0.7 \Rightarrow \delta \le 3d/14\)). R1@0.5 is the looser, more common number; R@5 counts a hit if any of the top 5 predictions matches.
    How does Moment-DETR work, and why does it not need NMS?core
    Clip features (SlowFast + CLIP every 2 s) and CLIP text token features are projected and concatenated into one sequence, encoded by a 2-layer transformer. A linear head on the video positions gives clip saliency. A 2-layer decoder with 10 learned moment queries outputs (center, width) and a foreground probability for each query. In training, the Hungarian algorithm assigns each true moment to exactly one query using a cost of −p(fg) + L1 + gIoU loss; only matched queries are trained to be foreground and to fit their moment, all others are trained to be background. Because two queries cannot both be rewarded for the same moment, the model learns not to produce duplicates, so NMS is not needed.
    Why use a hinge (margin) loss for saliency instead of regressing the ratings?core
    Highlight ratings are subjective and their scale differs between people, but their order within a video is more stable. The hinge loss \(\max(0, \Delta + S(t_{\text{low}}) - S(t_{\text{high}}))\) only asks that a better clip beat a worse one by a margin (0.2 in Moment-DETR); it is zero once that holds. Ranking is also exactly what the metric (mAP, HIT@1) measures. UniVTG uses a contrastive version: the positive clip should beat less-relevant clips in the same video (intra-video) and the same clip should match its own query better than other queries in the batch (inter-video).
    What is UniVTG’s unified formulation and why is it useful?core
    Every clip gets three targets: a foreground flag \(f_i\), offsets \((d^s_i, d^e_i)\) to the boundaries of its moment, and a saliency \(s_i \in [0,1]\). Interval labels, curve labels (per-clip scores) and point labels (single timestamps) can all be converted into this format, sometimes with pseudo-labels. So one model and one loss can train on moment retrieval, highlight and summarization data together, which allowed pretraining on 4.2M labels (Ego4D points, VideoCC intervals, CLIP-teacher curves) and even zero-shot grounding. Inference: rank clip votes by \(f\) and apply NMS for moments; rank clips by \(f + s\) for highlights.
    How does a video LLM like TimeChat output time, and what are the weaknesses?core
    TimeChat binds time to the visual tokens (the image Q-Former reads “This frame is sampled at 2s.” for each frame), compresses frames with a sliding video Q-Former (96 frames → 96 tokens with window, stride and tokens per window all 32), and is instruction-tuned on TimeIT (125K examples, 6 tasks) to write answers like “The given query happens in 12.0 - 18.5 seconds.” Weaknesses: the next-token loss does not know that 250 and 251 are close; precision is limited by frame spacing \(L/T\); answers must be parsed from free text; and zero-shot accuracy (32.2 R1@0.5 on Charades-STA) is still below supervised specialists. Strengths: one model for many tasks, free-form queries and explanations.
    Why does a perfect model not reach R1@0.7 = 100 on Charades-STA?advanced
    Because the labels are noisy. Moment boundaries are ambiguous and different annotators mark them differently. Otani et al. re-annotated 1,000 Charades-STA samples five times: a random human scored only 42.8 R1@0.5 against the official label (52.1 for the most consistent annotator). Charades-STA was also built semi-automatically from activity labels and descriptions. With boundary noise of a couple of seconds on an 8-s moment, the IoU between two annotators is often below 0.7. So above a point, higher numbers may reflect fitting the annotation style or dataset priors, not better understanding.
    What dataset biases affect moment retrieval, and how do you test for them?advanced
    Moment positions are not uniform: on Charades-STA many moments start near the beginning, and some verbs go with positions (“leave” near the end). A model can learn these priors from the text alone. Otani et al. showed that blind baselines (prior-only, one verb, or a text-only network) beat several published models on Charades-STA. Tests: run the model with shuffled or replaced video features, run text-only and prior-only baselines, and evaluate on splits where the moment distribution differs from training. QVHighlights spreads moments evenly to reduce this.
    Design a system that adds chapter markers and “key moments” to long uploaded videos.advanced
    Decode at 1–2 fps and compute clip embeddings once (a CLIP-style encoder) plus an ASR transcript with timestamps. Propose chapter boundaries from shot changes, topic changes in the transcript (embedding similarity drops) and silence; merge to a sensible minimum length. For each chapter, a video LLM or text LLM writes a short title from the transcript and a few frames. For key moments, score clips with a highlight model (saliency from query-free features, motion, audio energy) and pick the top clips with a diversity constraint. Snap all times to shot boundaries so they look clean. Evaluate with human raters on a sample, measure inter-rater agreement first, and track click-through on the markers in production.
    Your grounding model works on 30-s clips but fails on 1-hour videos. What do you check?advanced
    (1) Frame spacing: with a fixed frame or token budget, \(\Delta = L/T\) grows from under a second to tens of seconds, which caps IoU for short moments (207.14). (2) Normalized coordinates: a regression head that outputs (center, width) in [0, 1] has to be 120× more precise in relative terms. (3) Distractors: an hour has many similar scenes, so the top-1 can be a look-alike; use R@5 and a reranker. (4) Training distribution: moments in training may cover a large fraction of the video. Fixes: coarse-to-fine search (retrieve candidate windows with embeddings, then ground densely inside them), more frames around candidates, and training on long videos with short moments.
    Explain gIoU and why it is used for moment regression.core
    Generalized IoU is \(\text{IoU} - |C \setminus (A \cup B)| / |C|\), where \(C\) is the smallest interval that contains both \(A\) and \(B\). For overlapping intervals in 1-D, \(C\) equals the union, so gIoU equals IoU; for disjoint intervals the extra term makes gIoU negative and larger in magnitude the farther apart they are. Plain IoU is 0 for any non-overlapping pair and gives no gradient; \(1 - \text{gIoU}\) still tells the model which way to move. It is paired with an L1 loss on (center, width) because IoU alone ignores scale.

    Key takeaways

    • Moment retrieval returns intervals for a sentence; highlight detection scores every clip. Both work on a sequence of clip features (207.1).
    • R1@m counts top-1 predictions with IoU \(\ge m\) (207.2, 207.3); multi-moment queries use mAP averaged over IoU 0.5–0.95 (207.4); highlights use mAP and HIT@1 averaged over annotators (207.5).
    • TALL/CTRL scored sliding windows and regressed boundary offsets (207.6, 207.7). UniVTG lets every clip predict foreground, offsets and saliency (207.8), with NMS.
    • Moment-DETR predicts a set of moments with learned queries and Hungarian matching (207.9, 207.10), plus a margin ranking loss for saliency (207.11); it needs no NMS.
    • TimeChat binds timestamps to frame tokens and writes times as text (207.12, 207.13). Its precision is capped by frame spacing \(L/T\) (207.14), and answers must be parsed.
    • Labels are noisy: random human annotators score about 43 R1@0.5 on Charades-STA against the official label, and dataset priors let blind models score well. Measure agreement and run blind baselines before trusting small gains.

    Key formulas

    \[ \operatorname{IoU} = \frac{|\hat M \cap M|}{|\hat M \cup M|}, \quad \text{R}1@m = \frac{1}{N_q}\sum_q \mathbb{1}[\operatorname{IoU}_q \ge m], \quad b_i = [t_i - d^s_i,\ t_i + d^e_i] \]
    \[ \hat\sigma = \argmin_\sigma \sum_i \mathcal{C}_{\text{match}}(m_i, \hat m_{\sigma(i)}), \quad \mathcal{L}_{\text{sal}} = \max(0, \Delta + S_{\text{low}} - S_{\text{high}}) + \max(0, \Delta + S_{\text{out}} - S_{\text{in}}) \]
    \[ N_{\text{tok}} = \frac{T}{S} N_V, \quad \Delta = \frac{L}{T}, \quad \operatorname{IoU}_{\pm\delta} = \frac{d}{d + 2\delta} \]
